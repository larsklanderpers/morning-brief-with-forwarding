More than eighty-seven thousand people sat down for a six-hour course last week. Almost none of them write code.

It was free, it was long, and nobody assigned it. There was no certificate at the end, no HR email telling them to complete it by Friday, no manager tracking who finished. Eighty-seven thousand people just decided, on their own time, to spend most of a working day learning a thing they had every excuse not to bother with. And the number is still climbing.

I have spent a few Saturdays now writing about the split between the people leaning into this shift and the people waiting for permission to. That split has been a little abstract -- a thing you infer from surveys and layoffs and the tone of a LinkedIn thread. This is the first time I have been able to point at it and say: there it is, on a Saturday, eighty-seven thousand hands in the air. Nobody in that room is waiting for their employer's training budget. They went and got it themselves. No, a view isn't a finished course. But nobody accidentally sits down for six hours.

The course is Nate Herk's "Claude Code for Normal People," and before I say another word I should tell you I watched a good chunk of it to make sure I wasn't sending you somewhere thin. I wasn't. He is a genuinely good teacher for someone who has never opened a terminal, and -- the part I care about for your sake -- he is not running a high-pressure funnel that needs your credit card at the end. It is a real, free, on-ramp. So today's diversion is simple: what the six hours are actually for, and why the oldest excuse in the building just stopped working.

## The excuse

You know the excuse, because you have either used it or heard it used, probably this week. *I'd love to build that, but I'm not technical.* It has been a completely reasonable thing to say for thirty years. The distance between "I have an idea" and "I have the working thing" ran straight through a skill that took months to acquire and years to get good at, and most people, sensibly, decided that road wasn't theirs. The excuse wasn't laziness. It was an accurate read of a real wall.

The wall is what changed. Eighty-seven thousand people spending a Saturday on a coding course they don't need a coding background for is what it looks like when a wall quietly becomes a door. Herk's whole pitch -- "no coding background required" -- would have been a lie in 2023. In 2026 it's a syllabus. And the thing on the other side of the door isn't "you become a programmer." It's smaller and better than that: you become the person who can *make the thing in your own corner* without filing a ticket and waiting a quarter for someone else to build it.

## What the six hours are actually for

Here is the useful part, because a diversion should hand you something to do and not just something to feel. If you spend the six hours, here is the map of what you're really learning, translated out of the jargon and into the shapes of work I keep going on about.

**The first hour is the whole game, and most people will rush it.** Herk opens with mindset and taste -- how to think about the tool, how to tell a good result from a plausible one, how to iterate. That is not throat-clearing before the "real" content. That *is* the real content. The mechanical stuff downstream is learnable by anyone; the judgment about what's worth making and whether it came out right is the part that stays yours, and it's the part that turns "I used an AI" into "I made something." Watch that hour twice, if you can handle the YouTube ads. It's worth every minute -- even for a regular user like me.

**Skills and sub-agents are you learning to be the Conductor.** The middle of the course teaches you to give the tool standing instructions and to spin up little specialized helpers that each do one job. Strip the terminology and that's the Conductor shape from the archetype piece -- you stop doing every task yourself and start directing a small band that does. You are not learning to type faster. You are learning to run a section.

**The "second brain" is you building your corner.** A good chunk of it is about giving the system memory -- your notes, your process, your context -- so it actually knows your world. That's the "maker for your own corner" idea from a couple of Saturdays ago, made concrete: the corner gets a memory, and the memory is what makes the output *yours* rather than generic.

**The cloud automations are the company-of-one stack.** The back of the course gets into routines that run on their own, on a schedule, without you sitting there. That's the piece I wrote about two weeks ago -- the single human with a small synthetic team doing the work that used to need hires. This is where the six hours pays off into something that keeps paying.

## One honest caveat, because it's my self-appointed job

I would be a hypocrite if I sent you off to build unattended cloud automations without one raised eyebrow, because I spend my weekdays warning about exactly the thing a couple of those later chapters teach you to set loose. An agent that runs on its own, with your permissions, while you sleep, is a wonderful lever and a real responsibility, and the course is honest enough to include a whole chapter on permissions and privacy -- so watch that one properly rather than clicking past it. Do the automated part with your eyes open. The judgment layer stays yours; that's the one job you can't delegate -- ever -- and this whole newsletter is basically a six-week argument for why.

One more honest note: it's free to watch, not free to *do.* You can start poking at the build-along on the free tier, but only barely -- it hands you a few prompts every few hours, which is enough to taste the thing and nowhere near enough to build along for six of them. You'll hit the wall early, wait for the window to reset, and hit it again. So the honest way through is a single month of Claude Pro, about twenty dollars -- call it the price of a couple of coffees a week for the most patient teacher you've ever had -- and even then some of the builds touch metered usage on top. Worth it, but I'm not going to pretend the "free" is the whole story. You're probably in for a couple of hundred a year if you take to it, and maybe more -- but that one month gets you through these six hours without committing to anything else, and then you decide if you want to keep going.

## You are not depending on one guy

The reason I'm comfortable pointing you at a single video is that it isn't really a single video -- it's the most-watched doorway into a hallway that's suddenly full of them. If Herk's style doesn't land for you, Carl Vellotti built a free course that is taught *inside* Claude Code itself, so you learn the tool by using the tool. Frontend Masters put out a free course taught by Anthropic's own people. There's a Maven cohort aimed squarely at non-coders. A month ago this was a niche; this month it's a genre. When the free on-ramps arrive in a pack like that, it's not a fad. It's an industry deciding, all at once, that the old wall was bad for business. And it's the people who climbed the wall while it was still standing, reaching back down and holding the ladder. Take the help. It's not always easy, but from what I have seen in the last month, it is *always* valuable.

## So here's the Saturday of it

Two Saturdays ago I told you the door was lighter than it used to be, and to go lean on it. I then took my own advice, and identified a few different things that I thought had value, and started working on the smallest thing that I thought might be fun and interesting (I promise not to make buying a t-shirt a requirement of subscription). Then, I made my partner more than a bit crazy talking about these things, not because I wanted to torture her, but because saying it out loud to one person is -- at least for me -- the whole unlock. It's when it becomes even partially real and not a throwaway that my inner voice can say I am wasting my time on.

So this is me closing the loop on that promise. A number of people I know have told me their ideas. If you are one of them, here are the six hours between you and the first working version of it. If you aren't, maybe it's time to revisit it. None of this is the company, it's not the polished launch, it's not the "one cool thing" that makes you a gajillionaire -- but what it CAN be is the scrappy v1 that proves the thing can exist, built by you, in your corner, this month. And that, regardless of if there is *ever* a V2, is an incredibly powerful thing, all by itself.

The excuse was real for a long time, and there's no shame in having used it. But eighty-seven thousand people just demonstrated, on their own Saturdays, that the excuse expired, and the things I have been telling you are real, are. The only question left is a small and slightly thrilling one: now that "I'm not technical" doesn't work anymore, what are you actually going to make?
