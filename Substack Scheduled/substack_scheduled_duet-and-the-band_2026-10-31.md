# A Duet, and the Rest of the Band

*Anthropic launched an always-on personal agent ten days ago. Before I say anything else about any of it, please note that this is written from my perspective as an American who is right now in Sydney. Operationally, that means my personal Claude account has Duet and my company Claude account does not. My US phone has Muse and my Australian phone does not. My Grok bots run everywhere and they just got a substantial upgrade in the last 24 hours. My dots are available in both my personal and corporate accounts but they are both Codex Pro subscriptions so that doesn’t necessarily make them available to you. My Instinct agent runs on my US iPhone and delivers really useful context not only in the iMessage frame but also with spun-up web documents that are excellent for delivering extended context. In short, my agents have agents, and figuring out what to give who and where I am using something just to see how it works has been both good and bad.* 

*I definitely have opinions, and since I haven’t actually written anything for myself for two weeks because I have been too busy with actual work and a whole bunch of design and instructional documents in this period, my writing bandwidth has been consumed. I apologize for the time gap. I also apologize because this piece will likely sound a bit more opinionated than most simply because I am a bit rusty. Please bear with me.* 

Anthropic named its new agent after a piece of music for two. That is a fair description of how it starts, and not quite how it ends up. Dots are meant to be personalized with whatever name you feel appropriate and they have copied the avatar concept from Muse so you aren’t stuck with a symbol, you can actually create something anthropomorphic. Grok bots are intentionally meant to have various shapes but still look non-human. Instinct lives in your phone and its most likely competitor will be Apple Intelligence, but right now it has an advantage (remember my pieces about building control planes?) because it sits right where you are used to, it’s good at managing and creating things like group chats and trip plans, and it does solid work as a personal agent. So everyone has a surface and a personality of sorts and a feature set. And the decision of which to use is very much going to come down to a) availability and b) use case. Your mileage (kilometerage?) will VERY much vary from mine and you need to come at it from that perspective going in.

So, to put this into at least a little bit of context, we’re going to talk about the new kid on the block, then we’ll talk about what the stack looks like at present, what works for me, what doesn’t, and what you should keep in mind depending on what you are doing. That said, two major thoughts: 1) if you are already using something, probably you should stick with that something. There will be arguments for this product or the other because the features line up with where you are right now, but honestly they are all improving so fast and all riffing on each other’s offerings so quickly that things which are working will be broadly available very quickly in all platforms, not just the one you started in. Familiarity, within reason, probably outweighs exciting new shiny. And 2), a couple of items we’ll talk about today are free. Some are freemium. Some are just flat out paid full stop. Most will be applicable to your personal life and your business life and if you are anything like me, the intersection between the two. Some will be far better at one than the other. Your decisions about what to do with that need to align with where you can find the most traction. That said, as many people are starting to learn with Chat GPT free, if you are not the customer, you are the product, and so it’s important to recognize that free is still costing you something, and that over time the likelihood that everything will remain free on a given program or product is probably pretty low, so while that can be a decent decision-making basis *right now*, I would strongly suggest being ready to abandon it when a better option comes along.

If you don’t already know, a duet is you and one other voice, trading lines, each listening to the other. Perhaps, depending on the song, in the refrain both voices will sing together. Duets come in many forms but the most common is probably easiest thought of as call and response. It is a lovely format, but the unfortunate truth is that it is also not how most working music actually gets made. Once there is more than one song a night, you have a band: somebody keeping time at the back, somebody carrying the melody, somebody who only comes in for the solo, and one person at the front counting everyone in and deciding what comes next. Nobody in a good band plays every part. The skill is in who plays which part, and in everyone being able to hear each other.

Claude Duet, which Anthropic announced on 21 October, with an opt-in waitlist and a bit of a delay, regardless of subscription level and length of membership, is a very good singer. It is also joining a stage where a lot of us already have a band playing, and the useful question for this weekend is not "should I get it" but "which part does it take" and “are the things I can use it for things where a duet makes sense or are those things already multi-player.”

So today is a check-in on four of them: Duet for the personal and the small-business side, with a more detailed aside about Instinct in the personal part of that role, Grok Bot for the standing staff that runs in the background or if you are managing an entire business or starting one, Projects in Claude Code (another new feature that I haven’t described above but that takes the agent concept and the mono-thread and turns it into something far more useful for developers) for the developer side, and Muse for the house. Dot, for all kinds of mixed items (with the trade-off that the single context point can become very confusing and runs the risk of pollution), gets its own piece next week. What each one is, what it costs, what it will not do, and how mine talk to each other.

One confession before we start, and it changed while I was writing this. I have had a Max account (the top tier of Claude's consumer plans, though they announced yesterday that they are renaming their plans, so apparently I am on an Ultra now) for over a year, and when I started this piece I was still on the Duet waitlist like everyone else. The invite landed last weekend. So the Duet section is about a week of real use, which is not much, and the others are things I have been running for a while. The Instinct section is similar – I have had it for probably six or eight weeks but my usage has been very limited until I had a couple of things come up that it really aligned with, and I have found that use case helpful I am just not sure how common it will be. 

## The one I just got into

Anthropic's own description is simple: Duet is "a new app where Claude works as a proactive agent," and "we built Claude Duet for professionals and small business owners with more on their plates than they can track." It is a standalone app on iOS and Android, and it is on claude.ai as a new tab, effectively.  
[IMAGE: Duet tab and Projects tab in the Claude sidebar]  
My new Duet tab and my personal favoritest new thing, the projects tab, both in my Claude sidebar. 

***The parts that make it different from the Claude you already chat with:***

It runs on "a virtual machine with its own browser, so it doesn't require your laptop or phone to be on." Each morning "it sends you a short brief on the most important items across your connected apps," and it "checks your connected accounts every few hours, because checking nonstop would use up your plan's limits much faster." (I like that they said the quiet part out loud. Background work still costs something, and they are pacing it. I am particularly sensitized to this as I just went through a dial-back exercise with my Grok Bots, when I realized I had some scheduled routines that were running 4x per hour, 24 hours per day, 7 days a week, which if you do the math, is a lot of running times). It also "carries over your memory, skills, and connected apps from your Claude account," so it does not start from scratch. However, there are some caveats to that quote, so be aware before you get too deep into it.

The connectors at launch are Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive, Slack, QuickBooks, HubSpot, Docusign and Calendly, with purchases going through Stripe, Link or Shop Pay on "a one-time card that only works for that purchase." For my Microsoft-shop friends (like me!), the help page is blunt: "Microsoft 365 isn't available yet." (I note that Anthropic has done the same with the Projects functionality, configuring it so that only GitHub repos are available natively inside the harness, which is irritating).

Anthropic's own small-business examples are the giveaway for who this is really for: check QuickBooks or Stripe "for unpaid invoices and draft reminders," "prepare and run payroll in Gusto," chase HubSpot follow-ups, send Docusign agreements. One of its named testers is a restaurant group with "about 110 employees" using it for "accounting, customer support, and marketing." That is the back office of a small company. And I think that’s the key here, with Duet, is that it is CLEARLY intended to subsume the Claude small business pack and then provide the equivalent of an experienced personal or executive assistant that you may not be able to find or afford to help you keep rolling in that fashion.

**The never list is the feature.** Before it "sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document, or deletes anything, it shows you exactly what it's about to do and waits for your approval." Fine, every agent says something like that. The part I would actually build a small business on is the next bit: "Some actions are off-limits even with your approval, such as trading securities, moving money between accounts, or accessing someone else's account." And it is "instructed never to share" sign-in credentials, full card and bank account numbers, or government ID numbers, "even if you ask."

Read that again, because it is unusual. A yes button gets pressed at 11pm by someone who wants to go to bed. A capability that is not there cannot be talked into anything, by you, by a distracted bookkeeper, or by a cleverly worded email it reads at 6am. For a small business, where the person saying yes is usually also the person doing four other jobs, that is worth more than any feature on the list. Moreover, for a small business, where a simple hack that costs $50,000 is a business-ending event, the security-conscious position is really valuable. 

Obviously, if you haven’t caught it already, it also has limits worth knowing before you get excited. "You can't choose a model." "You can't watch Claude Duet work in its browser or take over the browser." It "doesn't make phone calls or send text messages to other people," it "can't see all of your projects or the files on your computer," and Anthropic itself says personal errands, like booking tickets, "aren't quite as strong." That’s ok – I would recommend a different path for handling things like that anyway. A personal travel agent Duet is not. But it’s really good, from what I can see, at grind work that takes up huge parts of your day that you really can’t afford to give up.

**What it costs.** During the research preview it is "included with the Pro and Max plans at no extra cost," US only, through a waitlist, and not on Team or Enterprise yet. It uses your plan's usage limits (so, it doesn’t *really* have no extra cost as it’s doing additional work you probably haven’t given a model yet, so it likely will increase consumption, even if it doesn’t hit your limits), and "work it does in the background, like the morning brief and check-ins, also counts, so you may reach your limits sooner." New users get "one full limit reset," good until 30 November. So the honest price is: twenty dollars a month on Pro if you already pay it, and the real cost is how much of your allowance you are willing to let it spend while you sleep.

So, what did I actually hand it first? Not invoices. The first week went to the small things that fall through the cracks between a day job, two courses and a writing habit. A refund I was owed. A paid workshop got canceled, and the refund went quiet. Duet kept it on my morning brief until the refund mail arrived. Small, boring, and exactly the kind of thing I would otherwise have remembered a month later.

A reply I owed someone. A partner on a training contract had been waiting on me for longer than I would like to admit. Duet drafted the reply that owned the delay, and then the proposal I took to my own partners. I edited both and pressed send myself; it did not send anything on its own.

Two time zones. I am in Sydney at the moment (a US citizen who happens to be in Australia, for anyone wondering how that squares with "US only" for the Duet preview), one of my courses runs on US Pacific time, one on US Eastern time, and the deadlines move. One thing whose whole job is to tell me what is due, in my time zone, has already saved me a bad morning. And yes, it nagged me about this piece. Multiple times. 

The one thing I would not give it, never list or not, is anything belonging to my day job's customers. The venues I work with hold member data under real regulatory obligations, and my own agent harness strips identifying details before any model sees it. Duet is built for a small business owner's own inbox and books. I would keep it there. I don’t have any comfort at this point with its capacity to handle PII, and neither should you.

On cost, a week in on Ultra (the old Max 20x), I have not hit a limit. But I am not the customer this was priced for, and regardless of how much I use it, unless it’s for Browser Control, it’s unlikely to even come close to my development consumption. On Pro, with a morning brief and a check-in every few hours, I would watch the allowance closely for the first month.

If you are still on the list, here is where I would start, using Anthropic's own suggestions as the floor, once you get access:

Every morning, tell me the five things in my inbox and calendar that actually need me today, and draft replies for the two that are just waiting on a yes or no. Do not send anything.

And to see it set up some action:

Check Stripe for unpaid invoices older than 14 days and draft a polite reminder for each one for me to review. Group them by customer and show me the total outstanding.

***Instinct:** Not to stretch the metaphor until it snaps, but if Duet is the singer working together with you to get done the things you need and help expedite your day, Instinct is the stage manager making things happen in the random instruction set. Duet, like most AI-facing tools, works best with context. It learns context over time, maintains it, and uses it to craft more accurate responses. I have heard many people discuss this as “it learns about you,” as all models do. Instinct takes instruction in a text message and acts from the get go like your fractional personal assistant who can run off and do all the work and then come back to you with a bunch of possibilities. If you make choices it can help book them, but like Muse, it’s better at collating than acting at this point, at least in my experience. It has one big benefit, out of the box, over any of the other agent products I have used so far (in addition to the native iMessage interaction plane): it natively handles groups and has its own agent spin off a child agent in conjunction with others in the chat (even friends who don’t have Instinct themselves) so that a group chat aggregates context from you both. It then sits inside the group chat, takes feedback from the parties, and builds shared content to help solve challenging organizational problems. It’s like your friend who always makes sure everyone is included when plans are being made. It’s great at personal stuff, great at being forward enough to get your attention when needed but background enough not to be too intrusive, and it seems like they have solved their security issues.*  

## The staff in the background

Duet is the singer. Grok Bot, in my house, is the rhythm section: the part you stop noticing because it is always there, it is always rolling, and sometimes it jams and then you are really impressed with what it can do. I am not a model junkie by any means but I would say the addition of Grok 4.7 Fast has also made it substantially smarter than it was in the first iteration. It’s also being constantly improved; there are days when I get two build pushes out of them hours apart. 

I wrote about Grok Bot at length on 12 September, and the first thing to do is correct forward on price, because it changed. On 12 October the company behind it (the x.ai pages now carry the SpaceXSI name, since xAI joined SpaceX in February) folded Grok, Grok Bot, Cursor and X Premium into one plan called the X Subscription. The tiers are Free, Plus at $8, Premium at $30, Super at $100 and Ultra at $200, and "Grok Bot is included with Premium, Super, Ultra, Teams, and Enterprise." So the "about twenty dollars a month" I printed in September is now **thirty dollars a month** for anyone signing up fresh. If you were already on SuperGrok, Cursor or X Premium, "nothing changes." Usage is a shared pool that "covers Chat, Imagine, Voice, and Bot," refreshing weekly, with top-ups billed at token cost.

I am on the $30 Premium tier myself, so that is the real number, for me and for anyone signing up today. Grok bot, however, earns its keep in my stack, in my opinion. It does more in running context than anything else I run.

The architecture has not changed, and it is still the reason I use it. Each Bot is a standing agent on a cloud computer ("Every Grok Bot shares one persistent cloud computer," with isolation "per user, not per Grok Bot"), and xAI describes more-or-less the shape I run: "A chief of staff sits on top, with a specialist for each lane." (More or less because I actually run two Chiefs of Staff, one for development-facing issues and support issues, and one for everything else.) Routines give a Bot "a standing responsibility that runs on a schedule or in response to an event." There is a practical ceiling of "roughly 50 Bots per account and six per group chat," which, at thirty-something bots, I can see from here. However, I have already trimmed some of my bots to archive (because they were limited-use and I don’t need them every day) but the ones in the “A-Teams” get used pretty much all the time.

Here is how mine has grown since September. I still run a chief-of-staff bot with child bots underneath it, and Jev (a decision model from TypeSafe.ai that I have written about before) still sits in front of the inbox (as well as in front of a number of other things now, but still only costs me a few dollars a month). But I now have **two** chiefs of staff: one for coding tasks, pull requests and everything that touches a repo, as well as help desk / support tickets and anything directly related to an SLA and one for everything else. Both of them talk to Freya, my ChatGPT dot (OpenAI's always-on agent, more on dots next week). The code and support bot talks to Claude through a bespoke agent I wrote for that purpose which embeds Slack and GitHub into its efforts for communications. (I might switch that to Duet, but not really seeing a benefit yet). The control plane of the comms between GB (Grok Bot) and CC (Claude Code) lets me avoid putting Claude agents directly into Slack, which I have…opinions…about, and keep the scope of conversations very tight. That’s a topic for another time. Ultimately, aside from my own personal stack, both of them talk to the (currently three) other fleets of Grok bots in the business through a control plane built on Slack and a GitHub repository, where the busiest voice on the line is not either chief of staff; it is, in the case of one fleet, the design team bot; in the case of another, the game review and improvements team bot; and in the case of the third, the integration with stack bot. It is rapidly growing pervasive everywhere in our development container, because of how well designed it is to be so.

Two chiefs of staff, because one got crowded. Code work/support work and everything-else work run on different clocks. A pull request wants an answer when CI fails or a reviewer comments, which is an event, at any hour. My inbox, my calendar and the business run on a schedule I can live with. With one chief of staff, the repo traffic kept jumping the queue, low-level support tickets were constantly overrunning my personal cognitive load boundaries, and my morning summary started to read like a changelog. Splitting them gave each one a single kind of work, which is the same one-bot-one-job rule I use everywhere else in the fleet, applied one level up. Artie (Artemis, my original chief of staff; the new one doesn’t have a name yet) kept the everything-else lane, because she had all the context to do so.

The design team bot being the loudest voice on the control plane surprised me more than it should have. It is the one bot a whole team of humans talks to directly, and its job is to interpret design decisions. So it carries the most inbound traffic, and most of what the coding side does depends on a decision it has already recorded. The bots that write code ask it what was decided; the people ask it what the bots did. In band terms, it is the bass player: not the one anyone bought a ticket to see, but the one the rest of the band is listening to.

Separate from it all sits Brigid, the voice agent I talk to in the car, which directs Freya and both chiefs of staff. Brigid is uniquely important not because of extended skill set (she uses GPT Instant which is nowhere near as powerful as Astra or even GPT 6.1 Sol) but because she provides me a more effective bi-directional control plane than my bespoke Chief of Staff control channel did. That said, Brigid gets far more direction when I am driving alone (which is usually an hour to an hour and a half a day when I am in Sydney because of the distance to the office) than she does when my partner is in the car; those days I try not to work too much on the drive and if I do the bi-directional Chief of Staff plane is the one I use because it has a computer voice and doesn’t sound like we are in an Uber Pool, like she often feels when Brigid is there.

I promised on 10 October that we would have the whole conversation about child bots versus bots you talk to directly on a coming Saturday. This is not that piece; it is the floor plan. That one still owes you the why. I will figure it out and I promise to get it to you before Christmas.

As I go to publication with this piece, Grok bot has within the past 48 hours added, among other things, Page and Dashboard support, so the way I use it will likely change again in the next couple of weeks, which is why I am deferring the conversation about bot structures for now, because I feel like there is real value in looking at that through a dashboard.

If you want to try the shape of GB without building thirty of anything, here is a decent place to start:

You are my chief of staff. Each morning at 7, read my inbox and calendar, and hand anything about invoices to the Finance bot and anything about scheduling to the Calendar bot. Post a three-line summary of what you handed to whom in our group chat. Do not reply to anyone outside the business without asking me. Install Dr. Eggbot from Poteto (Lauren Tan, one of the Grok Bot leads) first and use the Dr. Eggbot assistant to create the Finance and Calendar bots.

## The one that writes the code

The third part of the band is the one that only comes in for the solo, and on a development team, the solo is most of the set.

First, a naming trap, because Anthropic has made one. There are now two things called Projects. The classic one, in the regular Claude chat, is still essentially a folder: instructions and files that every conversation in it can see. The new one, which launched in beta on 17 September, lives in **Claude Code** (at claude.ai/code, in the desktop app and in the mobile app, but "not in the terminal CLI, the VS Code extension, or the JetBrains plugin"), and it is a different animal. When I say Projects for the rest of this section, I mean the new one. And when I talk about how useful it is for me, I urge you to remember that every use case may vary.

Anthropic describes it as "one ongoing conversation where Claude coordinates a stream of related work," which "starts a thread for each task." The threads run in the cloud, and "cloud threads keep going after you close your laptop." A thread can watch a pull request "with auto-fix turned on," which means it "pushes fixes when CI fails, addresses review comments." You can schedule work that runs as threads in the project, and you can now push a thread down to your own machine if it needs something local. New projects run every thread on Opus at high effort by default. That’s changeable, but if you are a heavy orchestration user (Opus 5.5 runs the show, Sonnet 5.5 does most of the work, Opus promotes to Fable 5.1 on design decisions or when it’s thrashing on a problem), the core construct of the project can be frustrating. Here’s the one thing I want to emphasize about Projects before anything else: EACH PROJECT is an ongoing conversation – it’s a mono-thread type model with side threads, but it’s a mono-thread *per concept* that carries affiliated items along with it. If, like me, you struggle with cognitive and context overload, it is a game changer. When I talk about context overload, I mean my Claude Code window historically looked like this:  
[IMAGE: Claude Code window, historical context overload]  
(A limited subset of a Claude Code exercise window)

So, the new construct carries your repositories, files, instructions (up to 16,000 characters), its own memory file, and the `CLAUDE.md` from each repository (the plain text file of standing instructions Claude reads at the start of every session). And then, in the line that made me laugh out loud, Anthropic tells you to "brief Claude in the project the way you'd brief a chief of staff." Everybody, it seems, has landed on the same org chart, which just goes to my point at the top of the piece.

The limits: GitHub.com only (not GitHub Enterprise Server, GitLab or Bitbucket), 200 new threads a day, Pro and Max plans only, and "A project belongs to one user. You can't share a project." It counts against the same plan limits as everything else, and "on a Pro plan in particular you should expect to reach your limit sooner." Anthropic is also honest about the obvious weakness of running lots of threads on one codebase: when two of them touch the same code, "the overlap is resolved as a merge conflict just like any other PR." For the most part I use Worktrees to handle this, which adds a bit of complexity to PR processing, but it lets us work together on items in parallel.

I am running six Projects so far, and they have become one of my new favorite things. Four of them are pointed at parts of our product stack, and each one contains at least six different repos and allows me to work on things like “implement this new feature which has to touch every layer from this front-end the whole way to the third-party interface and maintain indirection and continuity throughout, and here is what the feature does” And then the system builds me a plan, and I review the design plan, and then I implement it. Then the system builds me an implementation plan, and I review the implementation plan, and then I kick it off.

Then the system works in all six of those repos, or however many are in question, and makes the necessary set of updates to support the entire change end to end. When it goes through and it clears the CI at the end of the build, it allows me to test it all end to end in one place.

In cases where I have Storybook or Playwright, I'm able to embed that into the frontend so that not only can all the build tests and the performance tests be run, but the actual regression testing and validation testing against data and all the other things that historically would have required someone to sit there and do them one after the other. After each of the pieces of code had been tied together and validated for continuity, I can do it all in one stack and maintain context throughout all of it.

Then I can take the concept and say, "Great. Now we're going to do that same feature, except we're going to change it in this way, in this way, in this way," and the system will pick up and resume from that point. The continuity of context, for lack of a better description, is absolutely massive in this area. 

Now add to that that four of these projects come down to system implementations that go against a different interface backend.

What I'm able to do now with the projects is I implement a solution in one of those repos that, again, transits all the elements that are necessary for it to work against that particular interface backend. Call it product number one.

Once that's finished, I can get the context object to actually give me a handoff. I can take that handoff over to projects 2, 3, and 4 and have them do the exact same implementation so that the frontend sees the same thing. Everything that the system is consuming looks the same and acts the same to the end consumer, but all the unique pieces that relate to each of those different backends are handled in place and designed in a way which makes sure that consistency is enforced not only with the products themselves, but also across the entirety of the fleet family. 

I have another project which is tied to an actual deployment structure. We're doing a very large deployment across a huge section of our fleet, and we find, as we do those deployments, that we occasionally see errors that we wouldn't expect to see in some deployments but not in others. Tying the project to the context of all the repositories that represent the binaries that go into the deployment allows me to then go in and say:

* Here's the error I'm seeing at this point here.  
* Here's where the error is occurring.  
* Here's the log it's coming from.  
* Here's the specific context.

Then the project goes off and spins up a thread for site A and compares it to what it knows it should be doing from what's in all the repositories and all the code that's extant. It comes back and says, "The problem is probably here, here, and here. Run these test queries or scripts or items or whatever it might be, and confirm that that's where it's happening." Then we can go out and figure out why it's a problem here and not everywhere else.

Every one of those steps in the process, from the development to the testing, to the regression, to the confirmation, to the CI/CD pipeline, to the actual validation/verification of the installation in the customer site, is now expedited by this project infrastructure. Each troubleshoot gets faster, and each new feature gets faster, because the system has the context that it knows what's been done in the past, and it's able to read that from the repos, from the PRs, etc. When it picks up a new item, it then runs from that point forward.

From a functional and operational perspective, this particular type of personal agent structure has a huge operational impact on me. The fact that it can be tied to a customer or an action, not just to a piece of code, means that it probably has huge impact for almost anyone.

If you use this structure together with your largest customer, for instance, and you bind not only the repositories of things that you've done for that customer (a repository could be anything, right? It's just a file store), together with:

* your CRM system's context on that customer  
* your helpdesk system, if it's not part of your CRM system  
* your phone call recordings  
* your Zoom or Teams or whatever product you use for meeting recordings

and you put all that context together, then you go to the project and you say, "Hey, this customer is having this problem right now that they haven't had ever before. What would cause this? Or where did they implement it wrong from what we told them, or whatever it may be?" The system has so much context for making those determinations that it's able to do all that from what's there, without you having to restate it over and over again.

Now, when you take this to things like particular clients, unless you have a zero data retention program with Anthropic or something comparable (and even then, perhaps it might not be enough), you have to make sure that you, in the frontend of your harness, add limiters to protect against certain data being sent back to the project and to protect against inadvertently disclosing things you shouldn't.

Ironically, about two weeks after they introduced projects, Anthropic introduced something called Claude Code mods that are designed to deal exactly with this type of thing. They sit in the harness and they prevent certain things from happening, or they provide you additional information, or they take an action every time you hit Enter, or whatever it might be. They've also started to provide the capacity for governance inside the space. Is it to the same level of governance as your own harness would be? Of course not, but is there a trade-off there that has substantial value if you end up working in ways similar to the way I end up working? Yes, there is.

A starter brief, close to the shape of Anthropic's own sample:

This project covers [repos]. Goal: [one sentence]. Start a thread per repo. In every thread, run the tests and the linter before opening a PR, and watch the PR until CI is green. Do not merge, force-push, or change CI configuration without asking me. Post a short daily note of what each thread did.

## The one that plays the house parties

The fourth part of the band does not really belong on this stage. Muse is the one who plays the house parties: nobody's idea of the headliner, knows everybody's name, and remembers who cannot eat shellfish.

I gave Muse a long aside on 19 September, and the short version is that for personal use it is outstanding, it keeps everything in one conversation instead of an org chart, and the concierge works for the hotel. I will not re-argue that today. What deserves a check-in is how far it has moved in six weeks, because it no longer stays at the house.

Before I go any further with Muse, it’s important to understand that Muse has competition. A little new to the band, a bit mysterious, and definitely dating the drummer, but holy cow effective. Instinct (as I have talked about above) is gradually punching Muse in the gut and taking over tasks, for lots of reasons, not least because it’s clean, it’s fast, it’s native, and when it delivers me back items, they are really, really pretty to look at. Muse has a great avatar which I customized (I love my little sloth-bot) which counts heavily in its favor, and it’s also really comprehensive at running things down. It’s very good at any sort of general tasks in addition to the use cases (find me a refrigerator repairman, find me a handyman to come in and hang a TV, help me plan a full-fledged date day with my partner next Saturday if the sun is shining and an alternate if it’s raining). All use cases I have put to it in the last month and all use cases that it has done well. Instinct, on the other hand, has been pulled into a business/personal trip to Boston, a personal trip to New York with my partner for the holidays and for meeting up with some friends, and maintenance and searching and negotiation on my Christmas list. Both have acquitted themselves well. I think the decision in this category will very much come down to geography, taste, and your personal feelings about Mark Zuckerberg.

***The Muse Creepy Factor**: The other problem with Muse is that it’s a bit too forward about what it knows about you (even when it probably shouldn’t know that, but it does because of its Facebook dataset) and it can get really opinionated about it. My Muse, for instance, a couple of weeks ago got in its head that my partner and I should be going to a specific performance at the Opera House, even though I indicated several times it wasn’t going to work with the rest of our schedule, and it kept proposing it [in what felt like a continuously more strident way, though that’s probably me more than it] until I finally had to say flat out “it’s not happening, please drop it.” That’s a bit off-putting, even for me, understanding how it got there. I would think many people would find it somewhat aggressive.*

**It came to the office.** In late September Meta added Muse for Small Business, which it described on its earnings call as "a set of new skills and connectors inside the existing Muse app aimed at helping SMB owners run operations, find new customers." The connector list reads a lot like Duet's: QuickBooks, Stripe, Slack and Shopify, alongside Asana, Canva, Notion and Zoom. The part Duet cannot match is that it "connects your Facebook, Instagram and Meta ads in one step," so a business that already lives on Instagram starts with an agent that knows what it sells. That arrived a few weeks before Duet did, so the singer from the first section has competition for the same customer. There is also a Mac app now, which "works with your desktop computer files, apps and browser tabs," and that is exactly the thing Duet says it cannot do. So, again, use case, if you don’t have GB and you use Instagram ads heavily, and you need to do things beyond the personal realm, Muse may have legs for you. However, the value of it drops in the long run if you aren’t lined up to all of those criteria.

**It costs nothing to find out.** Muse is "free for most of what people need," and Meta says it is available today to adults in the US and Canada. Above that, Power is $20 a month for 500 million Muse tokens a week and Maximum is $100 a month for 3 billion, with the honest caveat that Meta's page gives the allowance in tokens and nothing that translates them into tasks, and still says subscriptions are "in limited testing." Meta told investors on its earnings call that more than 10 million people have set one up, that about 25% of them use it on any given day, and that the most common use is shopping. Those are its own numbers, offered to people it wants to impress, but I have not seen numbers like that from any other agent in this piece. I would imagine the Maximum is probably for people using Muse as a control plane for their Instagram advertising and really no one else, I can’t imagine needing that many tokens for anything else given what it does. That said, as noted previously: if you aren’t the customer, you are the product, and everything you do with Muse is going into Meta’s datasets somewhere.

**The trade has not moved.** Muse asks first: "you can choose to allow an action once, always allow it, or deny it," and for the business version Meta says "nothing publishes, sends or spends without your approval, and you can undo it." What I cannot find on Meta's pages is a version of Duet's never list, a thing it will not do even after you say yes, and the "always allow" option is exactly the 11pm button I described earlier. The privacy position is the one I laid out in September. Your conversations are "not shared with Meta's ad systems," training stays on unless you turn it off, and the version encrypted with a key only you hold was promised for later this year and does not appear on Muse's product page today.

If you want to try it on something with nothing at stake:

Find three things my kids and I could do in [town] this weekend, under [budget], with the times and what needs booking. Hold anything that needs a ticket or a payment until I say yes.

## How the band hears itself

Put the four side by side and the first thing you notice is how much they have converged. All four are always-on agents on someone else's cloud machine. Three of them sell the same org chart, a chief of staff over specialists, and Muse is the holdout with its one long conversation. And all four pay for their background work out of the same allowance you use for everything else, which is the economics line that rarely makes the launch post: Duet is no extra cost on Pro or Max, Projects is no extra cost on Pro or Max, Grok Bot starts at $30 for a new subscriber, Muse is free up to a limit, and in every case the agent that works while you sleep is drawing down the allowance you will want on Monday morning.

The second thing you notice is that none of them talks to the others out of the box. Duet will not take a hand-off from a Grok bot. A Grok bot cannot open a Claude Code thread. A Project cannot be shared, even with another agent of yours. Meta previewed an open standard this month, the Personal Agent Protocol, but it is aimed at Muse talking to business agents, not at the rest of your band. What nearly all of them can get at, one way or another, is Slack and GitHub: Duet and Muse both have Slack connectors, Grok's team bots get their own Slack handles and work in GitHub, and Projects lives on GitHub repositories. Which is, not by accident, what my control plane is built on. If you want your agents to be a band rather than four soloists playing different songs, give them one place where they can all hear the count.

And the third thing is where each one should sit. Duet is the voice you talk to and the one that touches your customers' inboxes and your invoices, so it is the one with the never list, and it should be. Grok, for me, is the standing staff, many small bots doing one job each, mostly unwatched. Projects is the one you point at a codebase and leave running overnight. Muse is the one I let into the house (though the new puppy, Instinct, is making it somewhat less attractive, when combined with the fact it keeps eating some of my shoes). In my stack they are not competing for the same part. In a small business that lives on Instagram, Duet and Muse may well be. My Dot is the business manager standing at the bar drinking beer while the band plays, who has lots of opinions about how everything should work, but doesn’t play in the music. More on Dots next week, as my feelings about them have really started to solidify in the last week.

## This weekend

**Get on the list, and pick the first chore.** Join the Duet waitlist with the email on your Claude account and, when it asks, say what you would hand off first. Pick something boring and frequent, like invoice reminders or the morning inbox sweep, so you can tell within a week whether it is earning its keep.

**Give one bot one routine.** If you have Grok Bot (or any agent with a scheduler), give exactly one bot one standing job on a schedule, and have it post what it did somewhere you will see it. One job, one bot, one place to read the result. Add the second only when the first has been boring for a week.

**Brief one Project as if it were a new hire.** If you write code and you are on Pro or Max, open a new Project in Claude Code against one repository, write the brief you would give a capable contractor on day one (what the goal is, what to run before every PR, what never to touch), and let one thread run while you go do something else.

**Give the house its own agent.** If you are in the US or Canada, Muse is free to start. Pick one household errand and an account you are comfortable with it seeing, and find out how it does before you connect anything else.

**Choose where they meet.** Before you add a third agent, decide where they will all report: a Slack channel, a repository, a shared document. It matters less which one you pick than that there is exactly one.

## Counting it in

A year ago the choice was which assistant to use. Now the choice is which part each one plays, and who counts everyone in, and that is a much better problem to have.

For me, my personal band right now is really made up of Grok bot, the rhythm section; Muse/Instinct the backup singers, Claude Projects, the guitars and other soloists that take over the jam like Jerry Garcia at a Grateful Dead concert, and Freya, my Dot, running the show from the bar. If you don’t have the time, energy, or budget for four or five different agent families, stick to the power trio: Muse/Instinct for personal (both no-charge options, for now), Grok bot for corporate team management and tasking (bots designed to work as teammates and performing certain tasks only), and Claude for serious coding and workflow intelligence. If you can only afford the duo, take Muse/Instinct as the singer and Grok bot as the guitar and drum set, and you will be just fine. There are certain tasks I wouldn’t trust it with yet but that’s probably as much a function of my teams as it is of the model. If you need serious coding or workflow intelligence, either ChatGPT or Claude is more than enough, you are unlikely to need both, and which of the two you end up with will likely determine your corporate agent structure. I have left off the open source alternatives because I think for most people the startup overhead is probably too high.

Duet is a good singer, and for a small business the “never” list may be the best thing about it. Right now, it’s still in its audition, and Freya is still running the show from the bar, but it’s a solid contender. For yourself, if you choose to try it, just make sure, when it finally lets you in, that it joins the band rather than starting a second one.

Every morning, tell me the five things in my inbox and calendar that actually need me today, and draft replies for the two that are just waiting on a yes or no. Do not send anything.

Check Stripe for unpaid invoices older than 14 days and draft a polite reminder for each one for me to review. Group them by customer and show me the total outstanding.

You are my chief of staff. Each morning at 7, read my inbox and calendar, and hand anything about invoices to the Finance bot and anything about scheduling to the Calendar bot. Install Dr. Eggbot from Poteto (Lauren Tan, one of the Grok Bot leads) first and use the Dr. Eggbot assistant to create the Finance and Calendar bots.

Every weekday at 4pm, check [source] for [the one thing you care about], and post a two-line note in [channel]: what changed today, and whether anything needs me. If nothing changed, say so in one line.

This project covers [repos]. Goal: [one sentence]. Start a thread per repo. In every thread, run the tests and the linter before opening a PR, and watch the PR until CI is green. Find three things my kids and I could do in [town] this weekend, under [budget], with the times and what needs booking. Hold anything that needs a ticket or a payment until I say yes.

*The meeting place, for any of them:*

When you finish a task, post one line in [channel or repo file]: what you did, what you handed to another agent and which one, and anything waiting on me. Read the same place before you start, and do not redo anything another agent has already marked done.
