# Australia moves to mandatory AI Standards; Office of AI created

**What happened.** Albanese ended five years of voluntary governance on 15 July. An Office of
AI sits inside PM&C with cross-portfolio power; mandatory Australian Standards will cover data
centre energy and water costs and creator copyright, reversing the Productivity Commission's
2025 position. National Cabinet considers the Standards in August, legislation early 2027,
staged enforcement 2027–28.

**Why it matters.** The tell is the location. Putting it inside PM&C rather than a line agency
is the same architecture used for the Cyber Security Act 2024 — that is what a government does
when it intends to compel other portfolios, not coordinate them. The timeline also matters for
tone: enforcement is 2027–28, so anyone writing "comply now" is overselling. What is live now
is the **consultation window**, and that closes long before the legislation does.

## Observations

- **2026-07-20** — First seen, framed as the voluntary-to-mandatory pivot.
- **2026-07-21** — Detail added: PM&C location, National Cabinet in August, legislation early 2027, staged enforcement 2027–28, no text/data mining exception for Australian creative works. Also reaching the SMB audience — practitioner commentary landing on "nothing changes today, but informed AI use is no longer optional," which is the register Lars's club and SME readers actually speak in.
- **2026-08-04** — **Skeptical read from the right.** AEI's Bronwyn Howell (3 Aug, *Australia Goes Again with "World-Leading" AI Strategy?*) asks whether "world-leading" survives comparison. Three things worth keeping:
    - **The structural tell, stated by an unfriendly witness.** Coordination is centralised in PM&C, but supervision "remains with existing regulators using their general powers rather than introducing any special ex ante AI regulatory powers." That is this thread's thesis in a sentence — Australia is not building an AI regulator, it is *loading AI onto* OAIC, AUSTRAC, ACCC and the state gaming regulators. For clubs that means nothing new to register with and everything arriving through the regulators they already answer to. Lars's audience-facing line writes itself.
    - **The copyright provision finally has a precedent** (the Open question below was missing one). Howell reads creator compensation as a re-run of the **News Media Bargaining Code**: identify a foreign platform extracting local value, impose a bargaining obligation on it, call it world-first. That is what "goes again" in her title means — Australia has a repeatable playbook, and this is its third outing after the NMBC and the under-16 social media ban. She frames it as "creator compensation as part of AI's *social licence* rather than a purely IP-law question."
    - **A deflationary line worth arguing *with*, not quoting flat.** Howell: given Australia's economic scale, its incentives "position it remarkably like, say, Louisiana or Arkansas in its AI policy approach." Useful as a foil — Arkansas does not have its own privacy commissioner, its own AML regime, or the standing to refuse an EU-style text-and-data-mining exception. The comparison fails precisely on the sovereignty instruments Lars writes about, which makes it a good thing to pick up and break.
- **2026-08-04 — Live tension for [[AI Sovereignty]].** The same plan courts foreign capital to build the data centres *and* mandates they be energy-neutral (net power generators) on stringent water efficiency. Energy-neutrality raises the unit cost of Australian compute; the cheapest inference therefore stays offshore. A sovereignty policy whose own price signal pushes inference out of the jurisdiction is a piece on its own — and it is the counter-argument to "bring the model home" that nobody friendly to the thesis has made yet.
