# Humanity's Last Exam — the benchmark's own answers are contested

**What happened.** HLE was built to resist saturation — deliberately hard, expert-written,
adversarially filtered. A year on, top models are **above 50%**. That alone is the ordinary story
("benchmarks fall faster than expected"). The two findings underneath it are not ordinary.

**1. The answer key may be wrong where it matters most.** **Future House** found that roughly **30%
of the chemistry and biology answers may be incorrect** — and the mechanism is the interesting part.
The construction pipeline filtered out questions models got *right*. So the surviving set is enriched
for items where the model disagreed with the answer key, which includes every case where **the model
was right and the key was wrong.** A benchmark built to be hard was partly built to be wrong, in the
same direction, by construction.

**2. The same model scores 10–18 points apart on different leaderboards.** Version, prompt scaffold,
public-versus-private split, and who ran the eval all move the number by more than the gap between
competing models. **A single HLE score, unqualified, is not a measurement.**

**Why it matters — and this is the procurement turn, not the AI-research turn.** The safety grade
(FLI) and the capability score (HLE) are the two comparative numbers a regulated buyer actually puts
into a diligence pack, because they look like the only vendor-neutral ones available. Neither
survives being asked how it was produced.

**The four questions for any vendor citing a benchmark:**
1. **Which leaderboard** ran it — the vendor's, the benchmark authors', or a third party's?
2. **Which version** of the benchmark, and has the answer key been revised since?
3. **Public or private set** — and if private, who holds it?
4. **What is the spread** for this same model across other leaderboards?
