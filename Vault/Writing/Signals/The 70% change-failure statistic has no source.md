# The 70% change-failure statistic has no source

**What happened.** "70% of change initiatives fail" is attributed to Kotter everywhere. Kotter's
1995 HBR paper "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail" contains **no such figure**. What
it contains is *"Over the past decade, I have watched more than 100 companies try to remake
themselves"* and *"Most fall somewhere in between, with a distinct tilt toward the lower end of the
scale."* The only percentage in the whole article is *"Well over 50% of the companies I have watched
fail in this first phase"* — establishing urgency.

The chain: **1993** Hammer & Champy publish an admittedly **"unscientific estimate"** that 50–70% of
reengineering efforts fail; Hammer later clarifies there is "no inherent success or failure rate."
**2000** Beer & Nohria state 70% in HBR with no evidence. **2004** Balogun & Hope Hailey put it in a
textbook. **2005** Rune Todnem By repeats it — sourced to the textbook — inside a paper titled *A
Critical Review*, subsequently cited ~1,375 times. **2008** Kotter states his own 70% in the preface
of *A Sense of Urgency*, defining failure as a three-way disjunction that includes **over budget**
and **late**, with no sample and no method. **2009** McKinsey converts a survey of 1,546 executives'
self-ratings into the same narrative. **2013** Ron Ashkenas writes an HBR piece about why change
management is broken and cites "60-70%" in the second paragraph. **2019** it appears in the *Journal
of Continuing Education in Nursing* (PMID 30942888), sourced to a consultancy blog.

**Mark Hughes (2011)**, "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?",
*Journal of Change Management* 11(4), 451–464, reviewed all five published instances and concluded:
*"there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence to support such a narrative."*

**Why it matters.** This is the cleanest available demonstration that repetition substitutes for
evidence in management thinking — and it happens **inside the critical literature**. A critical
review launders the number. An article about the discipline being broken cites the number. A
peer-reviewed clinical journal cites a blog. Nobody lied; every step was a reasonable citation of
the previous step.

The counter-evidence nobody quotes: IBM's *Making Change Work* (2008) found **41% of projects met
objectives** — a ~59% shortfall, not 70% — and it is a real survey.

## Observations

- **2026-08-16** — first seen. Hughes's 2022 follow-up, "Reflections: How Studying Organizational
  Change Lost Its Way," *JoCM* 22(1), goes further: the failure narrative is a failure of
  scholarship, and the field's shift from "managing change" to "leading change" was a rhetorical
  move that framing change as failing was needed to legitimise.
