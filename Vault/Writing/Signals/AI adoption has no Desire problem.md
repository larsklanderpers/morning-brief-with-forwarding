# AI adoption has no Desire problem

**What happened.** The practitioner consensus is that **Desire** is the hardest ADKAR element in AI
adoption. Prosci publishes the supporting figure: 69% of webinar attendees named Desire the biggest
challenge, against Reinforcement 15%, Awareness 9%, Ability 5%, Knowledge 2%.

**That is a poll of webinar attendees about their opinions**, not a measurement of barrier points
from ADKAR assessments. It tells you what practitioners find hardest — a statement about
practitioners. And note the incentive: Desire is the element practitioners cannot be held
responsible for delivering ("no one can make an individual have the Desire to support the change" —
Prosci's own words on the same page). It is the most professionally convenient answer available.

The actual data points the other way. **78% of AI users are bringing their own tools to work**
(BYOAI), rising to 80% at small and medium companies, across all age groups; **75% of knowledge
workers use AI at work**, 46% of them having started within the previous six months (Microsoft &
LinkedIn, *2024 Work Trend Index*, n=31,000 across 31 countries, fielded Feb–Mar 2024 by Edelman
Data & Intelligence). MIT NANDA's own interview data: **"only 40% of companies say they purchased an
official LLM subscription, [but] workers from over 90% of the companies we surveyed reported regular
use of personal AI tools for work tasks."**

**Why it matters.** A workforce that installs the tool on its own credit card does not have a Desire
problem. This inverts the sponsorship model both canonical frameworks are built on — Prosci's #1
finding in every study since 1998 is active and visible sponsorship, and Kotter's steps 1–2 are
urgency and coalition. **The sponsor is not ahead of the workforce. The sponsor is late to it**, and
60% of leaders say their own leadership lacks a plan and vision for AI (same Microsoft source).

So the money goes to Awareness and Desire — comms and urgency — which were solved before the
programme existed. By ADKAR's own logic, when the upstream elements are strong and adoption still
fails, the barrier is downstream by definition. Nobody applies it.

For a security audience the sharper version: shadow adoption is a **posture** problem before it is a
change problem, and a model that tells you to create urgency is telling you to do the one thing you
do not need.
