# South Australia calls a royal commission into AI

**What happened.** Announced **today, 10 August 2026**, by Premier **Peter Malinauskas** on his return from
the US (where he met OpenAI, Anthropic and Apple, and signed an MOU with OpenAI's Greg Brockman).
**Three commissioners**, recruited by **international search**, **none named**. Budget **about $3 million**.
Commissioners **start work in October 2026**; report due **by 1 July 2027**. **Terms of reference are four to
six weeks away** — mid-to-late September.

**Why it matters.** A state government has reached for its **most coercive investigative instrument** on a
question the Commonwealth is handling by circular, letter and forward plan. That is the Ad Hoc Regulatory
Regime thread's central claim arriving from an unexpected direction: not regulation improvised *downward*
through agencies, but a sub-national government using a nineteenth-century statute because nothing else
available to it has teeth.

## Accuracy — three corrections before this goes anywhere near a draft

1. **It is "Australia's first", NOT "the world's first."** No source makes the world claim. SBS: *"Australia's
   first major inquiry into AI."* AAP/The Mandarin/Epoch Times: *"a national-first royal commission."* Royal
   commissions exist in a handful of Westminster jurisdictions, so "no royal commission into AI has been held
   before" is *probably* true — but it is an argument from absence, and it is misleading in substance: the
   EU ran a multi-year AI Act process, the US had the NSCAI, Australia's own Senate Select Committee on
   Adopting AI reported in 2024. **"World's first" is a claim about the instrument that readers will hear as
   a claim about being first to ask.**
2. **"1 October" is not corroborated.** AAP, SBS, Epoch Times and The Mandarin all say only *October* /
   commissioners "start work in October." Only techAU gives 1 October. **Write "October 2026."**
3. **The report date is specific and worth being precise about: 1 July 2027**, not "July 2027" loosely.

Also: **no official name has been gazetted.** A royal commission's formal name is fixed by Letters Patent,
not yet issued. Do not print a capitalised title. And **no primary release was retrievable** from
premier.sa.gov.au at the time of checking — the site's crawlable index is stale. Re-check in 24–48 hours,
and again in 4–6 weeks for the Letters Patent.

## Scope, as actually said (there is no document yet)

Supported by the reporting: **future of work** (his central theme), **education**, **health and public
service delivery**, **creative industries**, **state and national regulatory settings**. Overstated in the
brief: *"the grid."* Sources refer to **data centres and their energy and water consumption** — resource
use, not the grid as such.

Malinauskas, verbatim (AAP):

> "It is absolutely clear that the use of artificial intelligence is an exciting opportunity to advance the
> cause of humanity."

> "But it is also true that unchecked artificial intelligence does represent a material risk to the way our
> society operates and a risk to the future of work if not thought through carefully."

> "We can choose to set the guardrails and the policy frameworks to ensure that we use AI in a way that puts
> people first…"

He called it a **"fork in the road"** and argued AI regulation is more consequential than social media
regulation.

## The powers, and the gap that is the actual story

Under the **Royal Commissions Act 1917 (SA)** the powers are genuinely coercive: s 11 makes it an offence to
fail to attend on summons, refuse to be sworn, refuse to produce documents or refuse to answer, with the
chairman able to **commit a person to gaol for up to three months**; s 11(3) provides a warrant to compel
attendance; s 11(4) makes refusals on separate days separate offences; s 7 frees the commission from the
rules of evidence.

And then: **it investigates and recommends. It does not prosecute, and its findings bind nobody.** SA's own
precedent is the **2016 Nuclear Fuel Cycle Royal Commission**, whose central recommendation was abandoned.

**That is the piece.** Compulsory power over *evidence*, zero power over *outcome* — the most coercive
instrument available, pointed at a question whose answer will be non-binding, reporting into a federal
process it cannot direct. Against [[NSW mandates AI registration and assessment across government]], which
has no compulsory power at all and *does* bind agencies, it is a clean pair: **the instrument with teeth
cannot bite, and the instrument that binds cannot see.**

## The federal timing, verified

PM Albanese, **15 July 2026**, University of Sydney: mandatory national AI standards to be **legislated**,
with **legislation expected in parliament early 2027**; a new **Office of AI** in PM&C to draft them;
data-centre obligations on power and water; strongest-possible copyright protection for Australian
creators. **National Cabinet in August 2026** to agree the framework. So SA reports **1 July 2027**, roughly
alongside or just after the federal bill enters parliament. Carry the caveat: this is announced policy with
a drafting body attached — **not a bill, not an exposure draft, not law**. Legal trackers still record
Australia as having no wide-reaching AI statute.
