# ADKAR and Kotter — a provenance file

Everything below is traced to source. Where a claim could not be verified it is marked
`[UNVERIFIED]` rather than smoothed over. Companion to
[[Foundational research — AI adoption and change management]], which grades the 35-source
reading list this came out of.

**The three things to take away if nothing else:**

1. **The 70% change-failure statistic is unsourced.** Not disputed — unsourced. Kotter's 1995
   paper contains no percentage at all.
2. **Kotter superseded his own model in 2014.** The sequential, small-coalition, episodic version
   almost everyone teaches was replaced by its author with a concurrent, continuous,
   volunteer-army version. Twelve years ago.
3. **AI adoption has no Desire problem.** Every change programme opens with urgency and comms.
   Both were solved before the programme existed. The change dies at Ability and Reinforcement.

**Provenance flag for the deck:** Prosci markets ADKAR as "research-based" but does not publish a consistent origin date for it. That is the first crack.

### 1.2 The claimed research base

Prosci's headline claim: *"The model was developed nearly two decades ago by Prosci founder Jeff Hiatt after studying the change patterns of **more than 700 organizations**."* ([Prosci, ADKAR page](https://www.prosci.com/methodology/adkar))

Hiatt's own book gives a different and narrower account: it references **"eight years of change management research with Prosci that preceded this text,"** a **2005 study of 411 companies** undergoing major change projects, and benchmarking data from "hundreds upon hundreds of organizations." It does not name a year of model creation. ([Hiatt, *ADKAR*, 2006 — text copy](https://experianta.com/wp-content/uploads/atbdp_temp/ADKAR_-A-Model-for-Change-in-Bu-Jeff-Hiatt-1.pdf))

**The benchmarking studies themselves** (Prosci's *Best Practices in Change Management* series):

- Prosci states it has conducted **12 benchmarking studies since 1998**. ([Prosci, "Change Management Research Process"](https://www.prosci.com/about/research-process))
- **First study (fielded 1997, published 1998): 102 participants.** ([Prosci, *Best Practices in Change Management* 11th Edition Executive Summary](https://www.prosci.com/hubfs/2.downloads/research-executive-summaries/11th%20Edition-Best-Practices-Executive-Summary-11-2020.pdf))
- **11th Edition (data 2019, published 2020): 1,863 participants**, 85 countries. Regional split: US 32%, Europe 21%, Australia/NZ 16%, Canada 11%, Asia-Pacific 7%, Latin America 6%, Africa 5%, Middle East 2%. ([same source](https://www.prosci.com/hubfs/2.downloads/research-executive-summaries/11th%20Edition-Best-Practices-Executive-Summary-11-2020.pdf))
- **12th Edition (data 2023): 2,668 respondents**, 38 industries, 101 countries. ([Prosci, *Best Practices in Change Management* 12th Edition Executive Summary, PDF](https://www.prosci.com/hubfs/Website_English/2.downloads/webinars/EN-EUR%20Webinar/Best-Practices-in-Change-Management-12th-Edition-Executive-Summary.pdf))
- Prosci's own research-process page describes the most recent study as **"more than 400,000 cells of data from 3,000 participants"** — which does not reconcile with 2,668. A marketing page describes the cumulative base as "over 10,800 professionals." **Flag this inconsistency.** ([Prosci research process](https://www.prosci.com/about/research-process); [Prosci, "Best Practices in Change Management"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/change-management-best-practices))

**How participants are recruited — this is the load-bearing methodological fact:**

> "Prosci recruits participants through thousands of online media outlets plus engages directly with **90,000 members of Prosci's Change Management Learning Center**." ([Prosci, "Change Management Research Process"](https://www.prosci.com/about/research-process))

That is an open-invitation convenience sample drawn predominantly from Prosci's own customer and alumni base. It is **self-selected change practitioners self-reporting on their own projects.** There is no sampling frame, no response-rate disclosure, and no independent verification of outcomes. Prosci's participant-list page names contributing organizations for 2011, 2013, 2015 and 2017 but discloses no recruitment method. ([Prosci, "Research Participants"](https://www.prosci.com/research-participants))

### 1.3 The five elements — Prosci's own definitions, questions and failure signatures

Hiatt's book definitions (verbatim from the 2006 text):

| Element | Hiatt's definition (2006) | Prosci's "question it answers" | Failure signature when it is the barrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | "A person's understanding of the nature of the change, why the change is being made and the risk of not changing." | *Why is this change needed? What is the risk of not changing?* | Individuals **ignore the change and continue existing practice**; question the need; defend current processes; no visible evidence of change. Prosci calls lack of awareness "the greatest source of resistance for both employees and managers." |
| **Desire** | "The willingness to support and engage in a change… ultimately about personal choice, influenced by the nature of the change, by an individual's personal situation, as well as intrinsic motivators." | *What's in it for me?* | **Disengagement, distraction, absence, job-seeking, active resistance, spreading misinformation, fear, anger, poor morale.** As with Awareness, the change is visibly absent. |
| **Knowledge** | "The information, training and education necessary to know how to change… information about behaviors, processes, tools, systems, skills, job roles and techniques." | *How do I change? What do I do?* | The person **tries and struggles**: frequent questions, mistakes requiring rework, frustration. Honest effort that "often doesn't work out." |
| **Ability** | "The realization or execution of the change… turning knowledge into action… demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels." | *Can I demonstrate proficiency in the new way?* | The person **tries but works slowly, with low productivity**, constantly seeking help, disappointed in their own performance; at risk of reverting or abandoning. |
| **Reinforcement** | "Those internal and external factors that sustain a change… recognition, rewards and celebrations tied to the realization of the change." | *What keeps this from reverting?* | **Declining enthusiasm and energy; reversion to previous methods; feeling unrecognised or uncompensated** for the change effort. |

Definitions: ([Hiatt, *ADKAR*, 2006](https://experianta.com/wp-content/uploads/atbdp_temp/ADKAR_-A-Model-for-Change-in-Bu-Jeff-Hiatt-1.pdf)). Questions and failure signatures: ([Prosci, "ADKAR: Core to the People Side of Change"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/adkar-core-to-the-people-side-of-change); [Prosci, "Individual Barriers to Change and What to Do About Them"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/individual-barriers-to-change-and-what-to-do-about-them)).

**The teaching value here:** the Knowledge and Ability failure signatures are the only ones that look like *effort*. Awareness and Desire failures look like *absence*. That distinction is the diagnostic payload of the model and it is what most people who "use ADKAR" never actually operationalise.

### 1.4 Sequential and cumulative — and "the barrier point"

Prosci's claim is that the five elements are **sequential and cumulative**: each must be sufficiently achieved before the next can be realised.

> "Earlier elements must be sufficiently achieved before the proceeding elements can be realized… Building Knowledge of how to change is ineffective if an individual does not have Awareness of the need for change or Desire to participate." ([Prosci, "ADKAR: Core to the People Side of Change"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/adkar-core-to-the-people-side-of-change))

Hiatt: elements "must occur in order as cumulative building blocks." ([Hiatt, 2006](https://experianta.com/wp-content/uploads/atbdp_temp/ADKAR_-A-Model-for-Change-in-Bu-Jeff-Hiatt-1.pdf))

**Barrier point — two definitions, one operational:**
- Hiatt (book): the barrier point is *"the first element of the model that is weak or would be assessed as 'low.'"*
- Prosci (operational): *"the first ADKAR element with a score of 3 or less"* on the 1–5 ADKAR assessment. ([Prosci, "Individual Barriers to Change"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/individual-barriers-to-change-and-what-to-do-about-them))

The practical claim: **you do not fix what is downstream of the barrier.** If Desire is the barrier, more training (Knowledge) is wasted spend. This is genuinely the model's most useful idea and the one that survives most of the critique.

**Note the weakness in the sequential claim:** it is asserted, not demonstrated. There is no published test showing that interventions delivered out of ADKAR order perform worse than in-order interventions. The cumulative logic is a design assumption presented as a finding.

### 1.5 How ADKAR relates to the rest of the Prosci methodology

People routinely say "we're using ADKAR" when they mean "we're using Prosci." ADKAR is **one of three** models in the Prosci Methodology:

1. **The PCT Model (Prosci Change Triangle)** — the strategic/diagnostic frame. Four elements: **Success** at the centre, with three sides: **Leadership/Sponsorship**, **Project Management**, **Change Management**. It assesses the health of a change effort at the *initiative* level.
2. **The ADKAR Model** — the *individual* level. "Organizations don't change, individuals do."
3. **The Prosci 3-Phase Process** — the *organizational execution* engine: **Phase 1 Prepare Approach** (define success, define impact, define approach) → **Phase 2 Manage Change** (plan and act, track performance, adapt actions) → **Phase 3 Sustain Outcomes** (review performance, activate sustainment, transfer ownership).

([Prosci, "Prosci Methodology"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/prosci-methodology); [Prosci, "The Prosci Change Triangle (PCT) Model"](https://www.prosci.com/methodology/pct-model))

**Get this right on the slide:** ADKAR is the *outcome* model — it says what has to be true inside a person's head. The 3-Phase Process is the *delivery* model. The PCT is the *governance* model. Teaching ADKAR as "the Prosci methodology" is like teaching a thermometer as "medicine."

### 1.6 The empirical evidence FOR ADKAR / Prosci

The honest position: **Prosci's benchmarking evidence supports the broader claim that structured change management correlates with meeting objectives. It does not validate ADKAR's five elements, their order, or their cumulative logic.** Nothing in the published benchmarking tests ADKAR as a construct.

The actual numbers Prosci publishes:

**Change management effectiveness → meeting/exceeding objectives (12th Edition, 2023 data, n=2,668):**
- Poor change management: **13%** met or exceeded objectives
- Fair: **39%**
- Good: **73%**
- Excellent: **88%**

([Prosci, 12th Edition Executive Summary](https://www.prosci.com/hubfs/Website_English/2.downloads/webinars/EN-EUR%20Webinar/Best-Practices-in-Change-Management-12th-Edition-Executive-Summary.pdf))

**Sponsor effectiveness → meeting/exceeding objectives:**
- **11th Edition (2019 data, n=1,863):** very ineffective sponsor **29%** → ineffective **39%** → moderately effective **57%** → extremely effective **73%** ([11th Ed. Executive Summary](https://www.prosci.com/hubfs/2.downloads/research-executive-summaries/11th%20Edition-Best-Practices-Executive-Summary-11-2020.pdf))
- **12th Edition (2023 data):** extremely ineffective sponsor **27%** → extremely effective sponsor **79%** ([Prosci, "Best Practices in Change Management"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/change-management-best-practices))
- Prosci: active and visible sponsorship has been **the #1 contributor to success in every study since 1998**.

**Methodology use:** among those using a formal methodology, **59%** achieved good/excellent change-management effectiveness vs **26%** without a structured approach (12th Ed.). ([Prosci, "Best Practices"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/change-management-best-practices))

**Why these numbers are weaker than they look:**
1. **Correlational, not causal.** The design cannot distinguish "good change management causes success" from "projects that were going well got rated as well-managed."
2. **Same-respondent bias.** The same person rates both the change management effectiveness *and* whether objectives were met. The correlation is partly an artefact of one person's overall impression of the project.
3. **Self-selected sample of Prosci-affiliated practitioners.** Recruited from Prosci's 90,000-member learning centre. People who paid for Prosci training are the ones rating whether Prosci-style change management works.
4. **No independent replication.** These findings have never been reproduced by a party without a commercial interest in the answer.
5. **The derived "6x" and "7x more likely" claims** that circulate (e.g. 88 ÷ 13 ≈ 6.8) are ratio arithmetic on the above table, not separate findings. Prosci's own pages also carry an unattributed claim that effective sponsors move a project's chance of realising benefits "from 25% to 85%" — I could find **no study citation for that figure. [UNVERIFIED]** ([Prosci, "Primary Sponsor's Role and Importance"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/primary-sponsors-role-and-importance))

### 1.7 The critique of ADKAR — including the honest admission

**Say this plainly on the slide: there is essentially no serious peer-reviewed critique of ADKAR, because there is essentially no serious peer-reviewed engagement with ADKAR at all.** I searched the biomedical/arXiv/scholarly corpora directly. What exists is:

**(a) Applied case reports, overwhelmingly in nursing, healthcare QI, and — oddly — veterinary/agricultural extension.** Examples with citations:
- Balluck, Asturi & Brockman-type COVID staffing work; Glegg et al. (2019) visual-management tool in paediatric care; Wong et al. (2019) hospital facility transitions; Pawl & Anderson (2017) nursing programme consolidation — all catalogued in ([*Canadian Journal of Nursing Informatics*, "Theory applied to informatics – The Prosci ADKAR Model"](https://cjni.net/journal/?p=10076)), which itself presents ADKAR **entirely uncritically** and offers no methodological assessment.
- "Driving statewide digital transformation in oncology care: a change management case study using ADKAR methodology" (PMID 41697195) — descriptive case study.
- "Use of the ADKAR® and CLARC® Change Models to Navigate Staffing Model Changes During the COVID-19 Pandemic" (PMC7439963) — descriptive.
- "Real World Challenges in Delivering Consumer Directed Care" (PMID 35282793) — found barriers "spread across multiple stages of the ADKAR model," i.e. used ADKAR as a coding scheme, not a test of it.

**(b) One genuine quantitative test with a null result — this is the single most useful critical citation available.**

> Caekebeke, N., Ringenier, M., Jonquiere, F.J., Tobias, T.J., Postma, M., van den Hoogen, A., Houben, M.A.M., Velkers, F.C., Sleeckx, N., Stegeman, A., Dewulf, J. et al. (2021). "Coaching Belgian and Dutch Broiler Farmers Aimed at Antimicrobial Stewardship and Disease Prevention." *Antibiotics*, 10(5), 590. DOI 10.3390/antibiotics10050590.

Thirty broiler farms, 13 months. Farmers were profiled with an adjusted ADKAR instrument, then coached. ADKAR scores rose. Biosecurity improved ~6%; antimicrobial use fell ~7%. And then:

> **"Despite these improvements, no significant association could be found between higher ADKAR® scores and lower AMU."**

The authors conclude: "Further investigation into sociological models is needed." ([MDPI, *Antibiotics* 10(5):590](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6382/10/5/590))

That is: in the one study I could find that measured ADKAR scores against an independently observed behavioural outcome, **the ADKAR score did not predict the behaviour change.** n=30, so it is not dispositive — but it is the only quantitative validation attempt on the table, and it came back null.

**(c) Practitioner critique, unnamed sources.** The most-cited critical piece online is James Hull, ["Criticisms of the ADKAR Model," Change Adaptive](https://changeadaptive.com/criticisms-of-the-adkar-model/). His seven points: it is a beginner's model ("necessary, but not sufficient"); it contains no assessment or planning method ("does not offer a single word on assessment"); **leadership is entirely absent from it**; Awareness and Desire overlap; Knowledge and Ability could be merged; the linear structure lacks the agility to pivot; and a three-day certification is not adequate preparation for complex change. **Hull cites no external sources.** Treat as informed opinion, not evidence.

**(d) The structural critiques, which hold up on their own logic and which you can make yourself:**

1. **Proprietary and commercial.** ADKAR is a registered trademark attached to a certification business. The evidence base is house data, published by the vendor, in reports sold by the vendor, drawn from a sample recruited from the vendor's customers. This is not fraud — it is just not independent evidence, and it should never be cited as though it were.
2. **Individual-level focus underweights structure and politics.** ADKAR locates change failure inside individuals. It has no vocabulary for budget, org design, incentive conflict, coalition politics, regulatory constraint, or the possibility that the change is *bad*. In ADKAR terms, an employee correctly resisting a stupid initiative is a "Desire barrier."
3. **No leadership or sponsorship in the model itself.** Prosci handles this by putting sponsorship in the PCT and the 3-Phase Process — which is fine, and is exactly why teaching ADKAR alone is a mistake.
4. **The sequence is asserted, not tested.** No published study demonstrates the cumulative ordering.
5. **The barrier-point diagnostic depends on self-assessment.** A 1–5 self-rating of one's own Desire is not a measurement instrument in any psychometric sense; I found no published reliability or validity statistics for the ADKAR assessment. **[UNVERIFIED — no psychometric validation located]**

**The line for the deck:** *ADKAR's problem is not that it has been refuted. It is that in twenty years nobody outside its owner has seriously tried to test it, and the one attempt I can find came back null.*

## PART 2 — KOTTER'S 8-STEP, PROPERLY SOURCED

### 2.1 Origin

- **John P. Kotter**, Konosuke Matsushita Professor of Leadership, Emeritus, Harvard Business School.
- **Article:** "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail," *Harvard Business Review*, **March–April 1995**, Vol. 73 (No. 2), pp. 59–67. Reprint 95204. ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/01/leading-change-why-transformation-efforts-fail) — HBR reissued it in the January 2007 "Best of HBR" issue, which is why the canonical URL says 2007; [full text PDF](https://rfleadership.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/KotterArticle.pdf))
- **Book:** *Leading Change*, Harvard Business School Press, **1996**.
- **Observation base, in his own words:** *"Over the past decade, I have watched more than 100 companies try to remake themselves into significantly better competitors."* Named examples: Ford, Landmark Communications, General Motors, British Airways, Eastern Airlines, Bristol-Myers Squibb. ([Kotter 1995, PDF](https://rfleadership.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/KotterArticle.pdf))

### 2.2 The eight steps AS ORIGINALLY WORDED

**1995 HBR — the article is structured as eight *errors*, with an exhibit listing eight *steps*.**

The eight errors (section headings):
1. Not Establishing a Great Enough Sense of Urgency
2. Not Creating a Powerful Enough Guiding Coalition
3. Lacking a Vision
4. Undercommunicating the Vision by a Factor of Ten
5. Not Removing Obstacles to the New Vision
6. Not Systematically Planning for and Creating Short-Term Wins
7. Declaring Victory Too Soon
8. Not Anchoring Changes in the Corporation's Culture

The exhibit, **"Eight Steps to Transforming Your Organization"** — verbatim, with Kotter's own sub-bullets ([Kotter 1995, PDF](https://rfleadership.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/KotterArticle.pdf)):

1. **Establishing a Sense of Urgency** — Examining market and competitive realities / Identifying and discussing crises, potential crises, or major opportunities
2. **Forming a Powerful Guiding Coalition** — Assembling a group with enough power to lead the change effort / Encouraging the group to work together as a team
3. **Creating a Vision** — Creating a vision to help direct the change effort / Developing strategies for achieving that vision
4. **Communicating the Vision** — Using every vehicle possible to communicate the new vision and strategies / Teaching new behaviors by the example of the guiding coalition
5. **Empowering Others to Act on the Vision** — Getting rid of obstacles to change / Changing systems or structures that seriously undermine the vision / Encouraging risk taking and nontraditional ideas, activities, and actions
6. **Planning for and Creating Short-Term Wins** — Planning for visible performance improvements / Creating those improvements / Recognizing and rewarding employees involved in the improvements
7. **Consolidating Improvements and Producing Still More Change** — Using increased credibility to change systems, structures, and policies that don't fit the vision / Hiring, promoting, and developing employees who can implement the vision / Reinvigorating the process with new projects, themes, and change agents
8. **Institutionalizing New Approaches** — Articulating the connections between the new behaviors and corporate success / Developing the means to ensure leadership development and succession

**1996 *Leading Change* — "The Eight-Stage Process of Creating Major Change" (reworded):**

1. Establishing a sense of urgency
2. Creating the guiding coalition
3. Developing a vision and strategy
4. Communicating the change vision
5. Empowering broad-based action
6. Generating short-term wins
7. Consolidating gains and producing more change
8. Anchoring new approaches in the culture

Sourced from Rune Todnem By's verbatim reproduction of Kotter's 1996 list in a comparison table — ([By, R.T. (2005), "Organisational Change Management: A Critical Review," *Journal of Change Management*, 5(4), 369–380, Table 4](https://www.avannistelrooij.nl/wp/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Todnem-BY-2005-JoCM.pdf)). *I did not read the 1996 book directly; this is a scholarly secondary reproduction, and it matches every other reliable rendering.*

**Teaching point:** step 2 goes from "Forming a **Powerful** Guiding Coalition" to "Creating the guiding coalition"; step 5 goes from "Empowering **Others**" to "Empowering **broad-based action**"; step 8 goes from "Institutionalizing new approaches" to "Anchoring new approaches **in the culture**." The 1996 book is already softening the heroic-leader framing of the 1995 article.

### 2.3 The 2014 revision — *Accelerate (XLR8)*. This is the section most people don't know exists.

**Book:** John P. Kotter, *Accelerate: Building Strategic Agility for a Faster-Moving World*, Harvard Business Review Press, **2014** (ISBN 9781625271747). Preceded by the HBR article "Accelerate!," **November 2012**. ([HBS faculty page](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=49745); [HBR, "Accelerate!"](https://hbr.org/2012/11/accelerate))

**The eight *accelerators* as worded in *Accelerate* (2014):**

1. Create a sense of urgency around **a Big Opportunity**
2. Build and evolve **a guiding coalition**
3. Form a change vision and strategic initiatives
4. **Enlist a volunteer army**
5. Enable action by removing barriers
6. Generate (and celebrate) short-term wins
7. **Sustain acceleration**
8. **Institute change**

([George Bradt, "Leverage John Kotter's 'Dual Operating System'…", *Forbes*, 14 May 2014](https://www.forbes.com/sites/georgebradt/2014/05/14/leverage-john-kotters-dual-operating-system-to-accelerate-change-in-large-organizations/))

A contemporaneous independent book review renders them as: "Urgency on big opportunity / Guided coalition of volunteers / Change vision and strategic initiatives / More and more volunteers / Barriers knocked down / Wins celebrated / Relentless action / Changes institutionalized" ([Henny Portman, "Review: XLR8 – Accelerate," 13 July 2016](https://hennyportman.wordpress.com/2016/07/13/review-xlr8-accelerate-building-strategic-agility-for-a-faster-moving-world/)).

**Kotter Inc's current public wording** (shortened again): Create a Sense of Urgency / Build a Guiding Coalition / Form a Strategic Vision and Initiatives / Enlist a Volunteer Army / Enable Action by Removing Barriers / Generate Short-Term Wins / Sustain Acceleration / Institute Change. ([Kotter Inc, "The 8-Step Process for Leading Change"](https://www.kotterinc.com/methodology/8-steps/))

**THE ACTUAL DIFFERENCE — from Kotter Inc's own 2018 eBook, verbatim:**

| Original 8-Step (1996) | Enhanced / 8 Accelerators (2014→) |
|---|---|
| "Respond to or effect **episodic change in finite and sequential ways**." | "**Run the steps concurrently and continuously.**" |
| "Focus on doing one thing very well in a **linear fashion over time**." | *(ongoing strategic capability, not a project)* |
| "Drive change with **a small, powerful core group**." | "Form **a large volunteer army** from up, down, and across the organization to be the change engine." |

And on the dual operating system:

> "There must be clear communication and synchronization between the traditional hierarchical structure and the innovative network of volunteers… Your hierarchy will still be in place, as it's critical for day-to-day operations. But, in addition, you'll now have a constantly innovating network."

And on step 1:

> "Building urgency is all about concentrating on **a window of opportunity that is open today but may close tomorrow**."

And on step 8:

> "Accelerators 1–7 are all about building new muscles, new behaviors and new ways of working. Accelerator 8 is about sustaining it long into the future."

Kotter Inc's own framing: *"Both versions are relevant and effective today, yet are designed for different contexts + objectives."* ([Kotter Inc, "8 Steps to Accelerate Change in Your Organization" eBook, 2018, PDF](https://www.kotterinc.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/8-Steps-eBook-Kotter-2018.pdf))

The five principles of the dual operating system, from *Accelerate*: (1) many people driving change from everywhere, not just the usual few appointees; (2) a "get-to" mindset, not a "have-to" one; (3) head *and* heart-driven action; (4) much more leadership, not just more management; (5) an inseparable partnership between the hierarchy and the network. ([Portman review](https://hennyportman.wordpress.com/2016/07/13/review-xlr8-accelerate-building-strategic-agility-for-a-faster-moving-world/))

**Why this is the most valuable slide in Part 2:** Kotter himself abandoned the sequential, one-time, small-coalition model **twelve years ago**. Almost every "Kotter's 8 Steps" training deck in circulation teaches the 1996 version and presents the sequence as the point. If you teach the 1996 model as current Kotter, you are teaching a model its author has publicly superseded — and, critically, you are teaching the *wrong* model for AI adoption, because the 2014 one was built for exactly the conditions AI adoption creates.

### 2.4 THE 70% STATISTIC — the mini-investigation

**Step 1: What Kotter actually wrote in 1995/1996.**

He gave no failure rate. The relevant sentences, verbatim:

> "Over the past decade, I have watched more than 100 companies try to remake themselves into significantly better competitors…"
>
> "**A few of these corporate change efforts have been very successful. A few have been utter failures. Most fall somewhere in between, with a distinct tilt toward the lower end of the scale.**"

The only percentage in the entire 1995 article:

> "**Well over 50% of the companies I have watched fail in this first phase**" [i.e. establishing urgency].

([Kotter 1995, full text PDF](https://rfleadership.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/KotterArticle.pdf))

There is **no dataset**. No sample definition beyond "more than 100 companies I have watched." No outcome measure. No coding scheme. No statistical analysis. It is a distinguished scholar's structured recollection, published as such, in a practitioner magazine.

**Step 2: Did Kotter ever publish a 70% figure? Yes — thirteen years later, and still without a dataset.**

*A Sense of Urgency* (Harvard Business Press, 2008), preface, verbatim:

> "**Incredibly, we found that in over 70 percent of the situations where substantial changes were clearly needed, either they were not fully launched, or the change efforts failed, or changes were achieved but over budget, late, and with great frustration.** We also found that in about 10 percent of the cases, people achieved more than would have been thought possible."

([Kotter, *A Sense of Urgency*, 2008 — text copy](https://dokumen.pub/a-sense-of-urgency-hardcovernbsped-1422179710-9781422179710.html))

Note what that sentence actually does: it defines "failure" as a three-way disjunction that includes *over budget* and *late*. Under that definition, a successful transformation that ran three months long counts as part of the 70%. **No sample, no method, no data appendix is provided.**

The same claim appears elsewhere in Kotter's writing as "more than 70 percent of needed change either fails to be launched … [or] to be completed."

**Step 3: Who stated it as "70%" first, and how it laundered.**

The reengineering literature got there first and was explicitly non-scientific about it:
- **Michael Hammer & James Champy, *Reengineering the Corporation* (1993):** an admittedly "unscientific estimate" that "as many as 50 percent to 70 percent" of reengineering efforts failed to achieve dramatic results. **Hammer subsequently clarified that this was a descriptive observation, not a rate, and that there is "no inherent success or failure rate."**
- **Michael Beer & Nitin Nohria, "Cracking the Code of Change," *HBR* (May–June 2000):** stated the 70% figure with **no supporting evidence at all**.

([Enclaria, "It's Time to Abolish the 70% Change Failure Rate Statistic," 3 June 2014](https://www.enclaria.com/2014/06/03/its-time-to-abolish-the-70-change-failure-rate-statistic/))

**Step 4: The debunking literature — named, with their actual arguments.**

**(a) Mark Hughes (2011), "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?", *Journal of Change Management*, Vol. 11, No. 4, pp. 451–464. DOI 10.1080/14697017.2011.630506.**

Abstract, verbatim:

> "A 70 per cent failure rate is frequently attributed to organizational change initiatives, raising questions about the origins and supporting evidence for this very specific statistic. This paper critically reviews five separate published instances identifying a 70 percent organizational change failure rate. In conclusion, whilst the existence of a popular narrative of 70 percent organizational change failure is acknowledged, **there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence to support such a narrative**."

([University of Brighton research portal](https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/do-70-per-cent-of-all-organizational-change-initiatives-really-fa/); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14697017.2011.630506))

The **five instances** Hughes reviewed, each found to lack valid and reliable empirical evidence:
1. Hammer & Champy, *Reengineering the Corporation* (1993)
2. Beer & Nohria, "Cracking the code of change," *HBR* (2000)
3. Kotter, *A Sense of Urgency* (2008)
4. Senturia et al., "Leading change management requires sticking to the plot," Bain & Company (2008)
5. Keller & Aiken, "The inconvenient truth about change management," McKinsey & Company (2009)

([Gail Severini, "Time to kill the phantom 70% failure rate quoted on transformational strategy?", 16 Nov 2013 — her tabulation of Hughes's five cases](https://gailseverini.wordpress.com/2013/11/16/time-to-kill-the-phantom-70-failure-rate-quoted-on-transformational-strategy/))

Hughes's deeper argument, per the paper summary: beliefs in an inherent high failure rate arise from **ambiguity in what "change" and "failure" even denote** — different studies measure different things over different horizons, and the "70%" narrative flattens that into a false precision.

**(b) Mark Hughes (2022), "Reflections: How Studying Organizational Change Lost Its Way," *Journal of Change Management*, Vol. 22, No. 1, pp. 8–25. DOI 10.1080/14697017.2022.2030980.** The stronger follow-up. Hughes argues the failure narrative is **a failure of scholarship, not of practice**: the field's shift from "managing change" to "leading change" was partly a rhetorical move to diminish managerial authority and construct the authority of the change leader, and framing change as failing was the device that legitimised it. On Kotter specifically: a close reading of the 1995 article shows Kotter depicted *managing* change as failing and prescribed *leading* change as the cure. Hughes notes the "paucity of knowledge" about change success and failure — a point he attributes to Michael Beer himself. ([Taylor & Francis PDF](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/14697017.2022.2030980))

**(c) Ron Ashkenas, "Change Management Needs to Change," *HBR*, April 2013.** Ashkenas's contribution is the *paradox*, not a debunk — and note carefully that **he repeats the statistic rather than challenging it**:

> "…most studies still show a **60-70% failure rate** for organizational change projects — a statistic that has stayed constant from the 1970's to the present."

His argument is that this constancy, despite decades of tools, training and "over 83,000" books, indicts the discipline itself, and that "when change is outsourced to consultants and 'experts,' it rarely works." ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/04/change-management-needs-to-cha))

**Use Ashkenas as an exhibit, not an authority.** A senior HBR contributor citing an unsourced statistic in an article about why the field is broken is the cleanest single illustration of the problem you will find.

**(d) Rune Todnem By (2005), "Organisational Change Management: A Critical Review," *Journal of Change Management*, 5(4), 369–380.** Frequently cited as a 70%-sceptic. **He is not.** He reports "a failure rate of around 70 per cent of all change programmes initiated" — sourced to **Balogun & Hope Hailey, *Exploring Strategic Change*, 2nd edn (2004)** — and treats it as fact, twice, including in his conclusion. His genuinely valuable contribution is the *broader* indictment:

> "…theories and approaches to change management currently available to academics and practitioners are often **contradictory, mostly lacking empirical evidence and supported by unchallenged hypotheses** concerning the nature of contemporary organisational change management."

> "While there is an ever-growing generic literature emphasising the importance of change and suggesting ways to approach it, **very little empirical evidence has been provided in support of the different theories and approaches suggested**" (citing Guimaraes & Armstrong, 1998).

([By 2005, full PDF](https://www.avannistelrooij.nl/wp/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Todnem-BY-2005-JoCM.pdf))

**By is himself a link in the laundering chain.** A 1,375-times-cited critical review repeating an unsourced 70% figure from a textbook is a perfect teaching artefact: this is how a number becomes "well established."

**(e) The chain continues into peer-reviewed clinical literature.** "70% Failure Rate: An Imperative for Better Change Management," *Journal of Continuing Education in Nursing*, 2019, 50(4), 148–149 (PMID 30942888) — opens: "The McKinsey consulting group reports data that 70% of all change management efforts fail." Peer-reviewed, indexed, and sourced to a consultancy blog.

**(f) The counter-evidence nobody quotes.** IBM's *Making Change Work* study (2008) found **41% of projects met objectives** — i.e. a ~59% shortfall rate, not 70% — and it is a real survey. McKinsey's own 2009 basis was a survey of **1,546 executives** in which 30% called their programmes "completely/mostly successful"; that is a measure of *executive self-rating*, not of failure. ([Enclaria](https://www.enclaria.com/2014/06/03/its-time-to-abolish-the-70-change-failure-rate-statistic/))

**How to retire the statistic in one slide:**
> Kotter's 1995 paper — the source everyone cites — contains no 70%. It contains "a distinct tilt toward the lower end of the scale." The 70% first appears in a 1993 reengineering book whose author called it "unscientific" and later disowned it as a rate. Kotter's own 70%, from 2008, defines failure to include "over budget" and "late" and publishes no data. Hughes reviewed the five published sources in 2011 and found no valid and reliable empirical evidence behind any of them. The statistic is not disputed. It is **unsourced**.

### 2.5 Other substantive critique of Kotter — named only

**(a) Appelbaum, S.H., Habashy, S., Malo, J.-L. & Shafiq, H. (2012), "Back to the future: revisiting Kotter's 1996 change model," *Journal of Management Development*, Vol. 31, No. 8, pp. 764–782. DOI 10.1108/02621711211253231.** A conceptual review that went looking for empirical support for each of the eight steps in the intervening literature. Its conclusion, as summarised on the paper's own hosting page: **"Kotter's model remains popular due to its practicality for managers and ease of understanding, despite lacking rigorous empirical foundations."** It found supporting evidence for *individual* elements (e.g. position power aiding implementation; short-term wins reducing resistance) but **not for the eight steps as a validated causal sequence**, and it recommends flexibility on the grounds that "not all steps might apply universally." ([Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/02621711211253231/full/html) — paywalled/CAPTCHA'd; content via [Academia.edu hosted copy](https://www.academia.edu/83152050/Back_to_the_future_revisiting_Kotters_1996_change_model) and [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/scholarly-journals/back-future-revisiting-kotters-1996-change-model/docview/1033808435/se-2))

**(b) McLaren, T.A.S., van der Hoorn, B. & Fein, E.C. (2022/2023), "Why Vilifying the Status Quo Can Derail a Change Effort: Kotter's Contradiction, and Theory Adaptation," *Journal of Change Management*. DOI 10.1080/14697017.2022.2137835. Published online 30 Oct 2022. CC BY-NC-ND.** University of Southern Queensland. The sharpest *theoretical* attack on step 1. Abstract, verbatim:

> "The Kotter eight-step change model has a pervasive influence in the practice of change management; founded on building urgency towards the desired change through convincing employees that the status quo is more dangerous than the future state. **We critique this positioning of the current state as dangerous, the proposition of using it to drive urgency, and the resultant employee anxiety and stress that is synonymous with organizational change. We reveal that positioning the current state as dangerous is a combative (and futile) strategy given the inescapable nature of status quo bias.** To address this complication, we propose a theory adaptation that appreciatively leverages status quo bias and recommends invoking approach motivation based on communicating the pressing importance of the future state."

([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364981583_Why_Vilifying_the_Status_Quo_Can_Derail_a_Change_Effort_Kotter's_Contradiction_and_Theory_Adaptation))

This one is directly usable in Part 4: burning-platform urgency is *exactly* what AI adoption programmes reach for, and it is exactly what this paper says backfires against status quo bias.

**(c) The structural critiques, which the above support:**

1. **Top-down / heroic-leader bias.** The 1995 model puts change in the hands of "a group with enough power." Hughes (2022) argues the very framing of "leading change" was constructed to install the change leader as the authoritative figure.
2. **No causal validation of the sequence.** Appelbaum et al. (2012). Nobody has run the counterfactual: does doing step 4 before step 3 actually degrade outcomes?
3. **Derived from retrospective observation, not controlled study.** Kotter's own framing — "companies I have watched" — with obvious survivorship and hindsight problems. He observed which firms succeeded and then reasoned backwards to what the successful ones had in common.
4. **Cultural-context limits.** By (2005) documents the broader field's Anglo-American, planned-change assumptions; the planned approach "presumes that all stakeholders in a change project are willing and interested in implementing it, and that a common agreement can be reached," which "clearly ignores organisational politics and conflict" (citing Burnes 1996, 2004; Bamford & Forrester 2003).
5. **Planned-change assumptions are contested at root.** By's review sets out the case that planned models assume organisations "operate under constant conditions" and "can move in a pre-planned manner from one stable state to another" — assumptions that a fast-changing environment "increasingly weakens." Note this is precisely the critique Kotter himself conceded in 2014.

## PART 3 — HEAD-TO-HEAD

### 3.1 The rigorous comparison

| Dimension | **ADKAR (Prosci)** | **Kotter 1996** | **Kotter 2014 (Accelerate)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unit of analysis** | The individual. "Organizations don't change, individuals do." | The organization, via its leadership. | The organization, via a hierarchy+network dual system. |
| **Origin** | Commercial. Prosci, a for-profit training and certification business; ADKAR is a registered trademark. | Academic-adjacent: HBS professor, published in a practitioner magazine and a trade press book, based on personal observation. | Same author, consultancy-embedded (Kotter Inc). |
| **Evidence base** | House benchmarking data, self-selected practitioner sample recruited from Prosci's own 90k-member list, self-reported outcomes, 12 studies 1998–2023. | ~100 companies "watched" over a decade. No dataset published. | No new empirical base published for the accelerators. **[UNVERIFIED — I found no published validation of the dual operating system]** |
| **Independent replication** | None located. One null-result quantitative test (Caekebeke et al. 2021, n=30). | None located for the eight steps as a causal sequence (Appelbaum et al. 2012). | None located. |
| **Sequence** | Sequential and cumulative; explicitly cannot be skipped or reordered. | Sequential; skipping steps "creates only the illusion of speed." | **Concurrent and continuous.** Kotter Inc: "Run the steps concurrently and continuously." |
| **Time horizon** | Per-change, per-person, episodic. | Episodic; a transformation with an end. Kotter's own 1995 note: "The peak came in year five, fully 36 months after the first set of visible wins." | Permanent operating capability, no end state. |
| **Who drives it** | The individual, coached by their people manager; sponsorship sits outside ADKAR in the PCT. | "A small, powerful core group" (Kotter Inc's own retrospective phrasing). | "A large volunteer army from up, down, and across the organization." |
| **What it is genuinely GOOD at** | **Diagnosis and triage.** Locating *where* an individual or population is stuck and preventing the classic waste of training people who don't want the change. It is the best cheap instrument for "should we spend this money on comms, training, or incentives?" | **Narrative and sequencing at the top.** Urgency, coalition, vision, communication. It is a leadership speech-and-structure model, and it is excellent at telling executives what they personally must do and in what order. | **Continuous strategic change under uncertainty.** Explicitly designed for change that never resolves. |
| **What it cannot see** | Power, politics, budget, org design, incentive conflict, whether the change is a bad idea, and any group- or system-level dynamic. Also: leadership — ADKAR has none in it. | Individual variation. Kotter's model treats the workforce as a mass to be moved. It has no diagnostic for *why a specific person* isn't moving. Also: emergent, bottom-up change. | Individual variation, same as 1996. Also: job redesign. |
| **Key failure mode when misused** | Treating legitimate objection as a "Desire barrier." | Manufacturing a burning platform, which McLaren et al. (2022) argue actively backfires against status quo bias. | Volunteer armies decaying into unmanaged shadow initiatives. |

### 3.2 Does the standard practitioner claim hold?

The standard claim: **Kotter = org-level narrative; ADKAR = individual-level diagnostic; use both.**

**It holds, but weakly, and with two caveats that matter for the deck:**

**Caveat 1 — the "complementarity" is partly an artefact of Prosci's product architecture.** ADKAR is not a change *methodology*; it is one of three Prosci models, and the two that would overlap with Kotter (the 3-Phase Process and the PCT) get quietly dropped from the comparison. A fair head-to-head is **Kotter's 8 steps vs. the Prosci 3-Phase Process + PCT**, at which point they look far more like competitors than complements — both are sequential, sponsor-centric, initiative-level delivery frameworks. Compare the phase lists: Kotter urgency/coalition/vision ≈ Prosci "Prepare Approach"; Kotter communicate/empower/wins ≈ "Manage Change"; Kotter consolidate/anchor ≈ "Sustain Outcomes". They are the same model with different marketing.

**Caveat 2 — the complementarity claim is nearly always made by people selling one or both.** I found no independent study testing whether combined ADKAR+Kotter deployment outperforms either alone. **[UNVERIFIED]**

**The defensible version of the claim:** ADKAR gives you a *measurement* layer that Kotter lacks entirely, and Kotter gives you a *leadership action* layer that ADKAR lacks entirely. That is real. But "we use ADKAR and Kotter" most often means "we ran a comms plan and did some training," which is neither.

### 3.3 Other models, correctly attributed — one line each

- **Lewin, unfreeze–change–refreeze (1947).** Attributed to Kurt Lewin, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics," *Human Relations* 1(1), 1947, and *Field Theory in Social Science* (1951, posthumous). **But: Cummings, S., Bridgman, T. & Brown, K.G. (2016), "Unfreezing change as three steps: Rethinking Kurt Lewin's legacy for change management," *Human Relations*, 69(1), 33–60** — forensic historical analysis showing Lewin never formulated a three-step change model. The idea occupies **131 words in a 338-page book**; it appears as a brief aside in the 1947 article; the model "took form after his death," assembled in the 1950s–60s by Lippitt, Schein and others to buttress their own emerging OD theories; **Lewin never wrote "refreezing"** — the term was introduced by Leon Festinger in 1950. The linear prescriptive tool in the textbooks contradicts Lewin's emphasis on continuous group dynamics. ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018726715577707)) *This is the second-best provenance story in the whole field after the 70%.*
- **Bridges Transition Model (1991).** William Bridges, *Managing Transitions: Making the Most of Change* (Addison-Wesley, 1991). Three phases: **Ending, Losing, Letting Go → The Neutral Zone → The New Beginning.** Bridges' core distinction: **change is situational and external; transition is psychological and internal.** ([William Bridges Associates](https://wmbridges.com/about/what-is-transition/)) Caveat: Holdsworth & Bridgman (2025) note Bridges' influential work was "not firmly grounded in empirical research."
- **McKinsey 7-S.** Robert H. Waterman Jr., Thomas J. Peters & Julien R. Phillips, **"Structure Is Not Organization," *Business Horizons*, 23(3), June 1980, pp. 14–26.** Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff. A diagnostic of internal alignment, not a change process — and it is routinely mis-taught as one. ([Tom Peters, "A Brief History of the 7-S Model"](https://tompeters.com/a-brief-history-of-the-7-s-mckinsey-7-s-model/))
- **Satir Change Model.** Derived from Virginia Satir's family-therapy work (1916–1988) and popularised in the software/organizational world largely through **Gerald M. Weinberg, *Quality Software Management, Vol. 4: Anticipating Change* (1997)**. Stages: Late Status Quo → Foreign Element → Chaos → Transforming Idea → Practice and Integration → New Status Quo. Its distinctive and useful claim is that **performance drops during Chaos and that this is expected, not failure.** Provenance is loose: it is an extrapolation from clinical family therapy to organizations, with no organizational validation I could find. **[UNVERIFIED as an organizational model]**
- **Kübler-Ross "change curve" — the misapplication.** Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, *On Death and Dying* (1969), described five responses observed in **terminally ill patients**: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. She never proposed it as a model of organizational change. The best scholarly account of how it got there: **Holdsworth, L. & Bridgman, T. (2025), "From coping with dying to coping with organizational change: the bricolage of the change curve's evolution," *Journal of Organizational Change Management*, 38(8), 254–269.** They trace three phases — **originators** (Kübler-Ross, 1969), **transitioners** (1975–1980, consultants extending it to volunteer morale and life transitions), and **translators** (1986–2002, change practitioners linking emotional stages to employee performance metrics) — and conclude the curve is **"bricolage" driven by commercial interests rather than scientific validation**, with utility prioritised "over scientific validity." They note Kübler-Ross's original research itself "lacks empirical validation" and "does not account for individual uniqueness," that Adams et al. (1976) made generalisations "not put to the test," and that Bridges' work was "not firmly grounded in empirical research." ([Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/jocm/article/38/8/254/1318694/From-coping-with-dying-to-coping-with))

## PART 4 — MAPPING TO AI AND AGENT ADOPTION

### 4.1 Which ADKAR element breaks first in AI adoption?

**The practitioner consensus says Desire. The practitioner consensus is wrong, and its provenance is worse than you'd think.**

**Where the "Desire" claim comes from.** Prosci publishes it: *"Over two-thirds of webinar attendees (69%) identified Desire as posing the biggest challenge"* — with Reinforcement 15%, Awareness 9%, Ability 5%, Knowledge 2%. ([Prosci, "Challenges You May Encounter in Building A, D, K, A and R"](https://www.prosci.com/blog/challenges-you-may-encounter-in-building-adkar))

**That is a poll of webinar attendees about their opinions. It is not a measurement of barrier points from ADKAR assessments.** It tells you what change practitioners *find hardest*, which is a statement about practitioners, not about changes. And note the incentive: "Desire" is the element practitioners cannot be held responsible for delivering ("no one can make an individual have the Desire to support the change" — Prosci's own words on the same page). It is the most professionally convenient answer available.

**The evidence points at Ability and Reinforcement.** Consider the AI-specific facts:

**Desire is demonstrably not the bottleneck.** Employees are adopting AI *without being asked to*:
- **78% of AI users are bringing their own AI tools to work** (BYOAI), rising to 80% at small and medium companies, across all age groups; **75% of knowledge workers use AI at work**, 46% of them having started within the previous six months. (Microsoft & LinkedIn, *2024 Work Trend Index Annual Report*, "AI at Work Is Here. Now Comes the Hard Part," 8 May 2024; n=31,000 knowledge workers, 31 countries, fielded 15 Feb – 28 Mar 2024 by Edelman Data & Intelligence — [Microsoft WorkLab](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part))
- MIT NANDA's own interview data: *"While only 40% of companies say they purchased an official LLM subscription, workers from over 90% of the companies we surveyed reported regular use of personal AI tools for work tasks."* ([MIT Project NANDA, *The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, PDF §3.3](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf))

A workforce that installs the tool on its own credit card does not have a Desire problem. **When Desire is unusually strong and adoption still fails, the barrier is downstream by definition — which is exactly what ADKAR's own logic predicts and exactly what nobody applies.**

**Ability is the real barrier, and there is measured evidence for it.**

- **METR RCT (Becker, J., Rush, N., Barnes, E. & Rein, D., "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity," arXiv:2507.09089, July 2025).** 16 experienced developers, 246 real tasks on repos they averaged 5 years working in, randomised to AI-allowed or AI-forbidden. Result: **AI access increased completion time by 19%.** Before the study, participants forecast a 24% *speed-up*; afterwards, having actually been slowed down, they still estimated AI had made them **20% faster**. Expert forecasters predicted 38–39% speed-ups. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)) **This is the single most important number in the deck**: people who use AI badly cannot tell that they are using it badly. That is an Ability barrier that is invisible to self-report — and ADKAR assessments are self-report.
- **Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D. & Raymond, L., "Generative AI at Work," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 140(2), 2025, pp. 889–942.** n=5,172 customer-support agents, staggered rollout. Overall **+15%** issues resolved per hour. But: **the lowest-skill quintile gained ~36%; the most-skilled agents saw no significant productivity change and a small *decline* in conversation quality.** ([QJE](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658); [author PDF](http://danielle.li/assets/docs/GenerativeAIatWork.pdf))
- **Dell'Acqua, F., McFowland III, E., Mollick, E.R., Lifshitz-Assaf, H., Kellogg, K., Rajendran, S., Krayer, L., Candelon, F. & Lakhani, K.R., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier," HBS Working Paper 24-013 / SSRN 4573321, 15 Sept 2023.** Preregistered field experiment, **758 BCG consultants**. Inside the AI's capability frontier: **+12.2% tasks completed, 25.1% faster, higher quality.** Outside it: **subjects using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions than those without AI.** ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321); [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700))

Read together: AI compresses the skill distribution at the bottom and *degrades* the top; and the required competence is not "operate the tool" but **"know which side of the jagged frontier you are standing on."** That is Ability, and it is not trainable by demonstration.

**Reinforcement is the barrier that decides whether adoption sticks — and organisations don't measure it.**

- **"Workslop."** Niederhoffer, K., Rosen Kellerman, G., Lee, A., Liebscher, A., Rapuano, K. & Hancock, J.T., "AI-Generated 'Workslop' Is Destroying Productivity," *Harvard Business Review*, 22 Sept 2025 (BetterUp Labs + Stanford Social Media Lab). Survey of **1,004 full-time US desk workers, September 2025**. **40%** report receiving workslop in the last month; workers estimate **15.4%** of the work they receive is workslop; **54%** of managers vs 38.5% of individual contributors report receiving it; **53%** admit some of what they send may be workslop; each instance costs **1 hour 51 minutes**; an estimated **$186 per employee per month**, extrapolated to **>$9M/year for a 10,000-person organisation**. ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity); [BetterUp methodology](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop))

That is what "adoption" without Reinforcement produces: measured usage goes up and organisational output quality goes down, and nobody's dashboard shows it because the dashboard counts seats and prompts.

**The verdict for the deck:** *Desire is the answer practitioners give because it is the answer that absolves them. In AI adoption, Desire is unusually strong, Knowledge is unusually cheap, and the change dies at **Ability** — where competence is judgment, not operation, and self-assessment is provably wrong — and at **Reinforcement** — where the organisation measures logins instead of output quality.*

### 4.2 Why AI adoption is structurally different — five arguments, made concretely

**(a) The technology changes under you every few months, so "Reinforcement" and "anchor in the culture" aim at a moving target.**

Both models' final step assumes a stable end state to which behaviour can be anchored. ADKAR's Reinforcement is "those internal and external factors that sustain a change"; Kotter's step 8 is "anchoring new approaches in the culture." Both presuppose that the *approach* is fixed. In AI adoption the correct behaviour at 12 months is often the *wrong* behaviour at 18 — the prompting discipline that produced good output on one model generation is unnecessary overhead on the next; the human review step that was essential becomes theatre; the task you correctly refused to delegate becomes the one you should. **Reinforcing last quarter's practice is actively harmful.** This is the strongest single argument for teaching Kotter-2014 rather than Kotter-1996: Kotter's own answer to this problem, arrived at for different reasons, was to stop treating step 8 as a terminus and to run all eight concurrently and continuously.

**(b) The change is bottom-up and already underway before any programme exists — which inverts the sponsorship model.**

Both models are sponsorship-first. Prosci's #1 finding in every study since 1998 is active and visible sponsorship; Kotter's steps 1–2 are urgency and coalition. But **90% of surveyed companies had employees regularly using personal AI tools while only 40% had bought a subscription** ([MIT NANDA §3.3](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)); **78% of AI users brought their own** ([Microsoft WTI 2024](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part)); and **60% of leaders worry their organisation's leadership lacks a plan and vision to implement AI** (same source). The sponsor is not ahead of the workforce. The sponsor is *behind* the workforce, arriving to lead a change that has already happened in shadow. Neither ADKAR nor Kotter-1996 has any move for "the change is already adopted, badly, and un-governed." Kotter-2014's volunteer army is the closest thing either author offers — and note that the shadow AI economy **is** a volunteer army that nobody enlisted.

For your audience specifically: shadow AI is a security-posture problem before it is a change-management problem, and the change model that tells you to "create urgency" is telling you to do the one thing you don't need.

**(c) The required competence is judgment about output quality, which is far harder to train and certify than tool operation.**

This is the point where ADKAR's Knowledge/Ability distinction earns its keep and then breaks. Knowledge ("how to use the tool") is nearly free — the interface is a text box. Ability, in Hiatt's own definition, is "demonstrated capability to implement the change **at the required performance levels**." For AI the required performance level is: *can you tell when the output is wrong?* The evidence says people cannot self-assess this: METR's developers were 19% slower and believed they were 20% faster ([arXiv:2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)); BCG's consultants were 19 points less likely to be correct on the one task past the frontier ([SSRN 4573321](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321)); the most skilled support agents got *worse* on quality ([QJE 140(2)](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658)).

You cannot certify this with a training completion rate. There is no LMS module for "calibrated scepticism." And the barrier-point diagnostic that would flag it — the ADKAR assessment — is a self-rating, and self-rating is precisely the faculty that fails.

**(d) The change threatens identity and status, not just workflow.**

ADKAR's Desire is framed as "what's in it for me" — a motivational, incentive-shaped construct. But the resistance in AI adoption is frequently about what the tool *implies about you*: that your judgment is a bottleneck, that your craft is a commodity, that the thing you are known for is now a subscription. Note the asymmetry in the QJE data: the workers who gained most were the least experienced; the most skilled gained nothing and lost quality. **The people with the most status to lose are the people the evidence says benefit least.** Their resistance is not a Desire deficit; it is an accurate read of their position. And McLaren, van der Hoorn & Fein (2022) is directly on point: the standard remedy — manufacture urgency by making the status quo look dangerous — is precisely the move they show backfires against status quo bias, producing "employee anxiety and stress." Telling a senior expert that their current way of working is a burning platform is a strategy their paper calls "combative (and futile)."

**(e) Agentic systems shift the human role to verification and delegation — a job-redesign question no change model addresses.**

ADKAR asks whether a person can perform the new behaviour. Kotter asks whether the organisation has anchored the new approach. **Neither asks whether the job should still exist in its current shape.** With agentic systems the human's work becomes specifying intent, setting boundaries, and verifying output — a supervisory role, at scale, over work they did not do. That is not a "new behaviour"; it is a different job, with different competencies, different throughput, different accountability, and different failure modes (approval fatigue, automation bias, responsibility diffusion). Both models sit downstream of job design and assume it is settled. It is not. **This is the honest gap, and it should be a slide: the models tell you how to get people to do the new job. They do not tell you what the new job is.**

The workslop data is what this gap looks like empirically: volume of output up, verification burden displaced onto colleagues, 1h51m per instance, $186/employee/month. Nobody redesigned the receiving end of the workflow.

### 4.3 The AI-adoption failure statistics — what survives scrutiny and what does not

**(i) MIT NANDA "95%" — full provenance.**

- **Title:** *The GenAI Divide: STATE OF AI IN BUSINESS 2025*
- **Authors:** Aditya Challapally, Chris Pease, Ramesh Raskar, Pradyumna Chari. Reviewer: Pradyumna Chari, Project NANDA.
- **Publisher:** MIT NANDA (Project NANDA, MIT Media Lab). **Cover date: July 2025**; circulated and widely covered from **18 August 2025** (Fortune). Distributed as **"v0.1."**
- **Research period:** January – June 2025.
- **Methodology, verbatim:** *"a multi-method research design that includes a systematic review of over 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, structured interviews with representatives from 52 organizations, and survey responses from 153 senior leaders **collected across four major industry conferences**."*
- **The claim, verbatim:** *"Despite $30–40 billion in enterprise investment into GenAI, this report uncovers a surprising result in that 95% of organizations are getting zero return."*
- **What was actually measured:** a funnel for *task-specific/embedded* GenAI tools — 60% investigated → 20% piloted → **5% successfully implemented**, where "successfully implemented" is defined as *"ones users or executives have remarked as causing a marked and sustained productivity and/or P&L impact."*
- **The report's own limitations note, verbatim:** *"**These figures are directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting.** Sample sizes vary by category, and success definitions may differ across organizations."*

([MIT Project NANDA, *The GenAI Divide*, full PDF](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf))

**Does it survive scrutiny? Partly. Use it only with the caveats attached.**

*In its favour:* unlike most numbers in this space, **it discloses its sample and method**, states its own limitations in the body text, and its qualitative findings (shadow AI, the pilot-to-production chasm, learning gap) are corroborated elsewhere.

*Against it:*
1. **Not peer-reviewed; explicitly preliminary ("v0.1"); a working paper from a lab, not an MIT institutional finding.** The report carries a disclaimer that views "do not reflect the positions of any affiliated employers."
2. **The 153 survey responses were "collected across four major industry conferences"** — a convenience sample of AI-conference attendees, which is roughly the worst possible frame for estimating population-level AI outcomes.
3. **The headline "95% of organizations are getting zero return" is not what the funnel measures.** The funnel measures the fate of *custom/task-specific* tools; the same report says general-purpose LLMs show **~83% pilot-to-implementation** and that generic tools are widely successful. The executive summary generalises a narrow measurement into a universal claim.
4. **Internal inconsistencies within the document.** The executive summary says *"Only 2 of 8 major sectors show meaningful structural change"*; §3.1 says *"only two industries (Tech and Media)"* out of **nine**, and §3 says *"Seven of nine sectors show little structural change."* On budgets, §3.4 says *"50% of GenAI budgets go to sales and marketing"* in its takeaway and *"approximately 70 percent of AI budget allocation"* two paragraphs later. **A report that cannot hold its own denominators steady across a page is not a report to put a load-bearing number on.**
5. **Structural conflict of interest.** The diagnosis is that AI fails because systems "do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time." NANDA is MIT's agentic-AI/decentralised-agent architecture project. The problem identified is the problem the authors' own project addresses. This has been noted in practitioner critique ([Álex Martínez Corriá, "The 95% AI Failure Rate: What MIT Got Right, and Why They're Still Dangerously Wrong," Medium/Shift Signal, 1 Sept 2025](https://medium.com/shift-signal/the-95-ai-failure-rate-what-mit-got-right-and-why-theyre-still-dangerously-wrong-d2544559b528) — note that this author also discloses he is building a competing AI infrastructure platform, so treat as a conflicted critique of a conflicted report).
6. **Six-month observation window** (Jan–Jun 2025) for judging return on multi-year transformations.

**Verdict:** it is a *contested* number with a disclosed method — categorically better than an *unexamined* one, but not a number to put unqualified on a slide.

**(ii) IDC/Lenovo "88% of POCs" — this one collapses, and it collapses in a way that is genuinely useful for teaching.**

- **The claim, verbatim as circulated:** *"88% of POCs monitored didn't advance: Surveyed companies launched 33 AI POCs, of which only four made it into production."*
- **Source:** Lenovo, *CIO Playbook 2025: It's Time for AI-nomics*, with research insights by IDC, **February 2025**. ([Equinix blog, 13 Nov 2025, citing the Lenovo/IDC playbook](https://blog.equinix.com/blog/2025/11/13/from-proof-of-concept-to-production-advancing-ai-adoption/); [Lenovo/BusinessWire release, 5 Feb 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250205462134/en))
- **Sample size and methodology for the 2025 edition: not disclosed in the public materials I could reach. [UNVERIFIED]**

**And then the same vendor, the same research partner, the same annual series, one year later:**

- *Lenovo CIO Playbook 2026: The Race for Enterprise AI*, research by IDC, **3,120 IT and business decision-makers**, fieldwork **16 Sept – 17 Oct 2025**: *"Nearly half (**46%**) of AI proof-of-concepts have already progressed into production."* ([BusinessWire, 27 Jan 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260127255538/en))

**88% didn't advance → 46% have advanced, in twelve months, from the same source.** Either the world changed radically, or the two figures measure different things with different denominators over different horizons and neither is a stable fact about AI adoption. Both readings disqualify the 88% as a slide number. **Do not use it.**

**(iii) Gartner "over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by 2027" — the worst-sourced number in the category.**

An independent investigation obtained and read the primary Gartner research note in full — *Emerging Tech: AI Vendor Race: Avoid Agentic AI Failure: Build Success Using Right Use Cases*, analyst Anushree Verma, published **15 May 2025** — and reports that the note **contains no survey, no sample size, no case count, no citation and no calculation** for the 40% figure. It is filed under Gartner's own label "Strategic Planning Assumption," i.e. a forward-looking prediction. Gartner's June 2025 press release restates the figure alongside a January 2025 poll of **3,412 webinar attendees** that measures *investment posture*, not project cancellation, and nothing connects the two. The same investigation found the identical no-disclosed-derivation pattern across eight other Gartner Strategic Planning Assumptions spanning four years. ([Johan Locke, "Gartner Says 40% of Agentic AI Projects Will Fail. Nobody Can Show the Math.," LightAge AI, 13 July 2026, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21482265](https://airi.com.au/gartner-40-percent-no-shown-math))

**Caveat on that source:** it is an independent, AI-produced, human-directed analysis, explicitly **not peer-reviewed**, and it is the only piece I found that claims to have read the primary note. Its central factual claim — that the note shows no derivation — is checkable only by a Gartner subscriber. Treat it as a well-documented allegation, not a settled fact. But note it draws the distinction that matters: NANDA's 95% is a *contested* number (disclosed method, visible critique); Gartner's 40% is an *unexamined* one.

**(iv) What actually survives scrutiny — the numbers with real methodology.**

- **US Census Bureau, Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS).** A biweekly, **nationally representative** federal survey. AI use hovered between **17% and 20%** of businesses (Dec 2025 – May 2026), with 20–23% expected within six months. As of 3 May 2026: **37%** for firms with 250+ employees, **32%** for 100–249, under 20% for firms with fewer than 20. By sector: Information **39.7%**, Finance & Insurance **33.9%**, Retail Trade **14%**. Question wording changed in **November 2025** from AI use "in producing goods or services" to "in any business function" — a material break in the series that you must disclose if you trend it. ([US Census Bureau, "Large Firms With at Least 20 Employees Biggest AI Users," May 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html); [BTOS programme page](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/btos.html))
- The three experimental studies in §4.1 (Brynjolfsson et al. QJE 2025; Dell'Acqua et al. 2023; METR 2025).

### 4.4 Peer-reviewed work applying ADKAR or Kotter specifically to AI/ML adoption

**It is thin. Say so.** I searched the biomedical and arXiv research corpora directly. What exists:

**ADKAR + AI:** essentially nothing at the level of evaluation. The nearest items:
- "Change Management in Radiology: A Contemporary Primer for Effective and Sustainable Practice" (PMID 41410171) — a primer noting radiologists have limited exposure to structured change approaches as AI enters practice. Narrative review.
- "Driving statewide digital transformation in oncology care: a change management case study using ADKAR methodology…" (PMID 41697195) — digital health, not AI proper; single descriptive case study.
- The Canadian Journal of Nursing Informatics piece references an ADKAR-and-AI application (Todd, n.d.) with **no date and no citable publication** — a dangling reference. ([CJNI](https://cjni.net/journal/?p=10076))

**Kotter + AI:** case reports only.
- "AI-Powered Voice Documentation: A Nurse-Led Pilot Program" (PMID 42487193) — "guided by Kotter's eight-step change management model." Single-site pilot, no comparison group.
- "Creating a Practical Transformational Change Management Model for Novel Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Technology Implementation in the Operating Room" (PMC9618851) — derives a *new* model because existing ones were found wanting.
- "Critical activities for successful implementation and adoption of AI in healthcare: towards a process framework for healthcare organizations," *Frontiers in Digital Health*, 2025 (PMC12122488) — opens by noting the **"absence of structured guidelines"** for AI implementation in healthcare, i.e. an explicit statement that the existing change models are not doing the job.

**Emerging non-peer-reviewed frameworks that explicitly say the classic models don't fit:**
- "A Framework for the Adoption and Integration of Generative AI in Midsize Organizations and Enterprises (FAIGMOE)" (arXiv:2510.19997) — argues TAM, TOE and Diffusion of Innovations "lack the specificity required for GenAI implementation."
- "From Challenge to Change: Design Principles for AI Transformations" (arXiv:2512.05533); "Human-Centered AI Transformation: Exploring Behavioral Dynamics in Software Engineering" (arXiv:2411.08693); "Making the Invisible Visible: Understanding the Mismatch Between Organizational Goals and Worker Experiences in AI Adoption" (arXiv:2605.03078).

**The honest summary for the deck:** *There is no peer-reviewed evaluation of ADKAR or Kotter applied to AI adoption. There are single-site healthcare case reports that used them as a framing device and reported that the project went acceptably. The most telling signal in the literature is that researchers working on AI implementation keep building new frameworks rather than using the existing ones — which is itself the finding.*

## Appendix — the two provenance stories worth telling in full

**Story A: the 70%.** 1993, Hammer & Champy publish an "unscientific estimate" that 50–70% of reengineering efforts fail; Hammer later says there is "no inherent success or failure rate." 1995, Kotter publishes an article with no percentage in it, titled "Why Transformation Efforts Fail." 2000, Beer & Nohria state 70% in HBR with no evidence. 2004, Balogun & Hope Hailey put it in a textbook. 2005, Rune Todnem By repeats it — sourced to the textbook — inside a paper called *A Critical Review*, which goes on to be cited 1,375 times. 2008, Kotter states his own 70% in a book preface, defining failure to include "over budget" and "late." 2009, McKinsey converts a survey of executives' self-ratings into the same narrative. 2011, Mark Hughes reads all five and finds nothing. 2013, an HBR contributor writes an article about how change management is broken, and cites the 60–70% figure in the second paragraph. 2019, it appears in a peer-reviewed nursing journal, sourced to McKinsey. **Nobody lied. Every step was a reasonable citation of the previous step.**

**Story B: Lewin's three steps.** Cummings, Bridgman & Brown (2016) show the model attributed to Lewin occupies 131 words in a 338-page posthumous book, appears as an aside in a 1947 article, was assembled into a prescriptive framework by Lippitt, Schein and others in the 1950s–60s to buttress their own theories, and **that Lewin never wrote the word "refreezing"** — Festinger introduced it in 1950. The most-taught change model in the world is a posthumous reconstruction its named author would not recognise.

Both stories teach the same lesson your audience already believes and will pay to have confirmed with citations: **in management thinking, repetition substitutes for evidence, and the citation trail is the only defence.**
