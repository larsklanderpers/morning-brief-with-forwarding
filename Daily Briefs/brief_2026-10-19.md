## 🚨 Government Advisories

**JPCERT/CC and the vendor confirm attacks on a SiteGuard web application firewall flaw that runs commands as root or SYSTEM**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hackers-exploit-siteguard-waf-flaw-to-run-commands-as-system-root/)
CVE-2026-106931 is an OS command injection in the statistics feature of SiteGuard Server Edition, a host-based WAF from Japan's E-Guardian group, sold through EG Secure; the feature is on by default, and the vendor's alert says "abuse has already been confirmed" and JPCERT/CC lists it as critical. Affected: Apache, Nginx and IIS editions from 2.20 up to before 8.00 Update 5, and FreeBSD editions before 7.00 Update 6; a file-replacement patch exists even for end-of-life versions. The vendor warns that patching may erase attack traces and asks admins to download diagnostic information first; direct-support customers were told on 29 September, roughly three weeks before public disclosure. Attacker, victims and scale are not disclosed.
💡 Strategic take: A WAF is the control you buy so that the web server does not have to be perfect, and here the control is the way in, with the highest privilege on the box. The instruction to save evidence before patching is the detail to copy into your own runbooks: a fast fix that wipes the only record of what happened turns an incident into a guess. The three-week gap between private and public notice is also worth asking your own security vendors about, because it decides who learns first.

**CISA and ACSC both quiet over the weekend; ACSC's 14 October OT alert is still its latest for a fifth day**
Source: Australian Signals Directorate's ACSC | [Link](https://www.cyber.gov.au/alerts-and-advisories)
CISA's alerts page shows nothing newer than its 16 October BeyondTrust alert and KEV additions, both covered on Saturday. The ACSC alerts and advisories page still leads with the 14 October alert on operational technology and edge devices, including refrigeration and cooling.
💡 Strategic take: A quiet weekend from both agencies means the to-do list is already written: BeyondTrust, the Fortinet path traversal below, and contractor-managed OT. Use the gap to close those rather than wait for the next bulletin.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Unit 42 ties the Fortinet flaw CISA added to KEV on Friday to a memory-only FortiGate implant inside a telecom network**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/exploited-fortinet-bug-linked-to-stealthy-fortigate-implant-in-telecom-hack/)
This brief noted the KEV addition of CVE-2025-61624 on 17 October. What moved: Palo Alto Networks reports that UNC5820, a suspected state-sponsored actor, used the FortiJump FortiManager flaw (CVE-2024-47575) in September 2024 to reach a telecom's central FortiManager, register a rogue virtual FortiGate and take a configuration archive covering more than 500 managed firewalls, then "appears to have exploited" CVE-2025-61624 as a zero-day to get root on the firewall OS. The resulting implant, PeerStitch, lives only in memory, opens no ports, makes no outbound connections and talks through the traffic of legitimate FortiOS processes; the actor also impersonated FortiManager to issue commands and worked to erase forensic artifacts.
💡 Strategic take: The prize was not one firewall, it was the console that manages five hundred of them, which is the same lesson as last week's ConnectWise app: compromise the manager and you inherit the fleet. An implant with no disk footprint and no network signature of its own defeats most of what edge-device monitoring checks for. If you run central management for firewalls, treat that console as tier-zero, on the same footing as your domain controllers, and ask your vendor what integrity checking of running memory it actually offers.

**Microsoft confirms October Windows Server updates are breaking Exchange sign-in and mail flow on some servers**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/microsoft/microsoft-october-windows-server-updates-cause-exchange-issues/)
Microsoft says this month's Windows Server security updates introduced a default cap of 512 simultaneous Endpoint Mapper calls that may be too low under heavy load, causing Outlook on the web sign-in and mail delivery failures on Exchange running on Windows Server 2019 and later. Not all servers are affected; Microsoft offers a registry workaround with a reboot and asks customers not to uninstall the October Exchange updates.
💡 Strategic take: This is the cost on the other side of "patch faster": every update is also a change, and on-premise Exchange is where those changes bite hardest. The useful posture is not to slow down, it is to have a tested rollback and a workaround path ready before the patch window opens. Organisations still running Exchange on premises for sovereignty reasons should price this recurring operational tax honestly against the alternatives.

**MITRE ATT&CK v20 adds AI abuse, MFA bypass and water utility techniques, plus 15 new detection strategies**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/mitre-attck-v20-catalogs-ai-abuse-mfa-bypass-water-utility-attacks/)
MITRE released version 20 of the framework last week, adding Enterprise techniques covering AI prompt injection and MFA bypass, OT coverage drawn from attacks on more than 30 water and wastewater utilities, and new campaign entries naming groups including Salt Typhoon, Nimbus Manticore and APT38. Defenders get 15 new detection strategies, six Enterprise and nine ICS, backed by 38 analytics. SecurityWeek notes some attributions in the underlying reports remain unconfirmed.
💡 Strategic take: ATT&CK is the shared vocabulary auditors, insurers and SOC vendors all map to, so when prompt injection gets a technique ID it stops being a research topic and becomes a line item someone will ask you to evidence. Expect your next pentest report and your next cyber insurance questionnaire to reference v20. The quick win is asking your detection provider which of the 15 new strategies they already cover, and in writing.

**Langflow flaw was under attack more than two weeks before its CVE was published**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/in-other-news-ics-sniper-attack-hacking-xworm-malware-operators-250k-chrome-exploit/)
Honeypot operator Previdian reports exploitation attempts against CVE-2026-105740, a critical Langflow flaw that lets authenticated users run code on the server, starting on 19 September, ahead of the CVE's 5 October publication; it logged more than 1,800 attempts from 28 IP addresses in 16 countries. Langflow is a popular open-source builder for AI agent workflows. The figures are Previdian's own sensor data, reported in SecurityWeek's 16 October roundup.
💡 Strategic take: AI workflow builders hold exactly the things attackers want, model API keys, cloud credentials and connections into internal data, and they are often stood up by a data team outside normal change control. Two weeks of exploitation before a CVE exists means scanners keyed to CVE numbers were blind for the whole window. Inventory every Langflow, Flowise or similar instance you have, and get any that face the internet behind authentication today.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**XBOW used a public OpenAI model to build a full-chain Chrome exploit in six days for about $40,000 of compute, and Google paid $250,000**
Source: XBOW | [Link](https://xbow.com/blog/xbow-built-a-chrome-full-chain-exploit)
XBOW disclosed on 13 October a three-bug chain, in V8's Maglev compiler, the animated WebP decoder and a handler outside the renderer sandbox, that took a malicious web page to code execution on Windows. The team says it built the final chain between 2 and 7 August with OpenAI's publicly available GPT-5.6 Sol, with researchers choosing targets, splitting tasks and checking the model's output, at roughly $40,000 in compute. Google fixed all three bugs and paid its top full-chain reward; there is no report of the exploit being used against users.
💡 Strategic take: Put the two numbers side by side: $40,000 of compute for an exploit Google values at $250,000, against a browser on about a billion machines. That ratio is the whole story of this year, because the same arithmetic works for anyone with a credit card and fewer scruples. XBOW also sells AI pentesting, so read the economics as a vendor's own claim, but the bounty is Google's independent verdict that the chain was real.

**Bank of America says it added 1,000 contractors to 3,000 staff to patch flaws that frontier AI models like Mythos exposed**
Source: CNBC | [Link](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/anthropic-mythos-bank-of-america-hiring-cost.html)
CEO Brian Moynihan told CNBC on 14 October that the bank brought in about 1,000 third-party consultants in the second quarter to help 3,000 internal staff patch proprietary and vendor software flaws surfaced by advanced AI models including Anthropic's Mythos. The remediation work, combined with anti-money laundering compliance, added what he called "a couple hundred million plus" to third-quarter costs, which he expects to decline over time. The bank did not break out the patching cost on its own.
💡 Strategic take: This is the first big-bank number on what AI-speed discovery costs the defender, and it lands the same week as the $40,000 Chrome exploit. Finding flaws has become cheap; fixing them is still people, change windows and testing, and it still costs what it always did. For a club or casino operator without 4,000 people to throw at it, the lesson is to push that remediation cost back up the supply chain: contracts should say who pays when a vendor's code is found wanting.

**SecurityWeek reports Anthropic has acquired Opaque Systems and that Opaque is winding down, as Intel drops SGX from future Xeons**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/anthropic-acquires-confidential-ai-startup-opaque-systems/)
The 17 October AI brief carried Constellation Research's account of a licence and partial hire, not a full acquisition, and flagged that it was unclear whether Opaque would keep serving customers. What moved: SecurityWeek, citing Opaque, now describes it as an acquisition and says Opaque is winding down operations; financial terms are undisclosed, and Opaque raised a $24 million Series B at a $300 million valuation this year. Separately, SecurityWeek reported on 16 October that Intel will stop including SGX after the Diamond Rapids Xeon generation and shift its confidential computing roadmap to TDX, with SGX platforms supported into the early 2030s. Anthropic has still not published its own statement.
💡 Strategic take: Confidential computing is the technical answer to the regulated buyer's question, "can the AI provider see my data?", and both stories show that answer consolidating into a few hands. Opaque customers now have their answer on continuity, and it is the one to plan around. Anyone with an SGX-based architecture has a decade of runway but a clear direction of travel; the time to design for TDX or its equivalents is before the next hardware refresh, not during it.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The thread this morning is that finding flaws has become cheap and fixing them has not. XBOW spent about $40,000 of compute to build a Chrome exploit worth $250,000 to Google; Bank of America spent a couple of hundred million in a quarter, and hired 1,000 contractors, to fix what models like Mythos found. Meanwhile the fixes themselves bite back, as Microsoft's October update shows on Exchange, and the security tools sit squarely in the attack path: a WAF that runs attacker commands as root, a firewall manager that handed over 500 firewall configurations. For a decision-maker the asymmetry is the budget question of the year. Discovery now scales with compute; remediation scales with people, testing and change windows. The organisations that come out ahead will be the ones that shorten the remediation side deliberately, through tier-zero treatment of management consoles, tested rollback, and contracts that make vendors carry the cost of their own flaws.
