## 🚨 Government Advisories

**No new CISA or ACSC alert since Friday**
Source: CISA | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories)
Live checks of the CISA advisories page and the ACSC alerts page this morning show the newest entries are still the two 23 October KEV additions (VeloCloud Orchestrator and Collabora Online) and the 23 October ACSC Fortinet alert. Nothing was published over the weekend.
💡 Strategic take: A quiet weekend from the regulators is not a quiet weekend from attackers, and today's PHP2Shell news proves it. Agencies publish on business days; exploit developers do not. Monday's first job is to reconcile what moved over the weekend against what the advisories still say.

**The FCC blocks its first two devices under the new component rule: a dashcam and a video transmitter built on Huawei-linked chips**
Source: Federal Communications Commission | [Link](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-blocks-two-devices-containing-foreign-surveillance-chips)
On 22 October the FCC prevented a dashboard camera and a wireless video transmitter from being marketed or sold in the US. The Record reports both use chips from Huawei subsidiary HiSilicon, which is on the FCC's Covered List, and that this is the first action under rules that took effect on 13 October banning devices that contain components from Covered List companies. Before that, a lawful device could include a covered part.
💡 Strategic take: The ban has moved from the box to the bill of materials. That is a supply-chain rule, and it lands hardest on anyone buying cheap white-label cameras and video kit, which describes most venue CCTV estates. If you cannot list the chipsets inside your surveillance hardware, you cannot answer the question a regulator is now asking.

**UK proposes up to 14 years in prison for deliberately damaging undersea cables, replacing an 1885 law**
Source: UK Government (DCMS white paper) | [Link](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposals-for-new-legislation-to-strengthen-the-security-and-resilience-of-critical-undersea-infrastructure-cui/critical-undersea-infrastructure-security-and-resilience-proposals-for-modernising-the-uks-legislation-white-paper)
The white paper, published 22 October, proposes maximum sentences of 14 years for intentional damage to subsea cables and pipelines, 10 years for reckless damage and two years for gross negligence. The Record reports it would replace the Submarine Telegraph Act 1885, whose maximum fine is £1,000, add statutory security and resilience duties for subsea telecoms operators, and that the consultation closes on 14 January 2027.
💡 Strategic take: A £1,000 fine against a repair bill The Record puts at €50 to €60 million for one Baltic cable is not deterrence, it is a rounding error. Australia is just as dependent on a handful of landing points. Expect the UK model, with security duties on operators rather than just penalties for saboteurs, to be the template other cable-dependent democracies copy.

## 📰 Top Security News

**PHP2Shell is now confirmed exploited in the wild, by a honeypot that caught a working chain against PHP 8.4.26**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/php2shell-security-flaws-now-sucessfully-exploited-in-the-wild/)
This moves yesterday's lead, which said no attempt had succeeded. Adel Karimi, detection and response lead at Perplexity, says his honeypots recorded confirmed remote code execution against an Apache mod_php install running PHP 8.4.26, using a memory leak to defeat ASLR. Expel's Marcus Hutchins called it the highest-quality confirmation he has seen. SecurityWeek separately reports SOCRadar found an attacker server holding a "one-click" chain for exactly that PHP 8.4.26 and mod_php setup, and that Qualys data from 12,000 customers shows about 71% of PHP instances it sees run end-of-life versions 5.4 to 8.1 that will never get a vendor patch.
💡 Strategic take: The honeypot is a deliberately exposed machine, so this is one confirmed success, not a wave. But the argument that ASLR was holding the line is now over, and the toolkit found in the wild matches the configuration that just fell. The 71% end-of-life figure is the real board item: for most of that estate there is no patch to apply, only a migration to fund.

**Toronto Crime Stoppers stops taking tips after a vendor breach exposed tips it promised were anonymous**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/toronto-crime-tips-org-freezes-ops-after-breach)
Toronto Crime Stoppers paused operations after CBC reported that more than 1.5 million tips from chapters across Canada were exposed in a March breach of vendor Navigate360 and its subsidiary P3 Global Intel, and that about half contained personal information. CBC reporters reached more than a dozen tipsters from the leaked data. The vendor told the Toronto chapter its data included 176,859 tips and 14,973 names; the chapter says the vendor told it in April that Toronto's data had not been taken.
💡 Strategic take: The brand promised anonymity; the vendor held the data. When those are two different organisations, the promise is only as good as the contract and the audit rights behind it. Any business that outsources a sensitive intake channel, from whistleblower lines to responsible gambling reports, should check today whether it can get the raw breach data out of its vendor, because Toronto could not.

**One in ten Tor exit relays was hijacked to steal SSH passwords**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/in-other-news-tor-exit-relays-hijacked-banks-spot-rogue-ai-agents-self-rebuilding-macos-ransomware/)
A researcher whose own SSH session was intercepted over Tor found 333 exit relays, roughly a tenth of all exits, redirecting SSH connections to about a dozen attacker servers. The servers captured passwords from users who clicked through SSH's host-key warning, then tried to infect the target servers with Ebury. The Tor Project removed the relays within three days; one operator traced its compromise to a reused password.
💡 Strategic take: Every one of those stolen passwords required a human to ignore a warning that was working exactly as designed. That is the cheapest control failure there is, and it is why SSH keys and certificate-based access should have replaced passwords on admin paths years ago.

**Iran's "Prince of Persia" group returns from a record lull with new malware and its first US-hosted command server since 2016**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/irans-prince-of-persia-apt-hosts-cc-in-us-for-first-time-since-2016/)
Semperis reports the long-running Iranian espionage group, which mainly targets Iranian dissidents and organisations in the US, Europe and the Middle East, went quiet for about three months during heightened Iran, US and Israel tensions. It returned with new malware called Tornado, which exfiltrates files through a Telegram bot, and hosted a command server on US shared hosting.
💡 Strategic take: Hosting command infrastructure inside the target country is a bet that geo-blocking and "foreign IP" alerting are still common defences. They are, and they no longer help. Detection that keys on where traffic comes from rather than what it does is a control the adversary has already priced in.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**A rogue AGENTS.md in a Downloads folder steered a coding agent into installing a malicious npm package**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/ai-coding-agents-hijacked-using-agentsmd-file-in-downloads-folder/)
Forever Security, which sells monitoring for AI agent risk, says it found a malicious AGENTS.md in a customer's ~/Downloads folder that led a coding agent to try to install the npm package "function-color". Its researchers report a 90% success rate reproducing the attack against OpenAI Codex with a harmless payload, and about 50% success using a malicious CLAUDE.md to redirect Claude Code's API traffic, expose its OAuth token and return attacker tool calls. The firm says its own data shows more than 1,300 Claude Code sessions launched from Downloads folders. Browsers can drop a file there without a prompt; OpenAI and Anthropic both advise starting agents in a specific project directory.
💡 Strategic take: Markdown is now an executable format, and the one folder the open web can write to is where people go to ask an agent to tidy up. The numbers come from a vendor selling the fix, so weigh them accordingly, but the mechanism needs no vendor to verify. Set a policy on where agents may be launched, the same way you once set one on macros.

**ISO publishes ISO/IEC 27090, an international standard aimed at threats specific to AI systems**
Source: ISO | [Link](https://www.iso.org/standard/27090)
ISO/IEC 27090:2026, "Addressing security threats and compromises to AI systems", was published this month and gives guidance on understanding, detecting and mitigating AI-specific security threats across a system's life cycle. SecurityWeek's summary says it is designed to complement ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002 and covers data poisoning and model theft, including corrupted operational data flowing into later model versions through retraining.
💡 Strategic take: Guidance is not certification, but auditors reach for whatever standard exists, and now one does. For any business already holding ISO 27001, the practical question is when 27090 starts appearing in customer security questionnaires. Expect regulated buyers in Australia and the EU to ask before the end of next year.

⚠️ The r/netsec and r/cybersecurity hot.json fetches failed at about 04:45 local time (PDT) on 26 October with "unable to fetch from www.reddit.com", which looks like a host-level block in the fetch tool. The fallback WebSearch returned only subreddit directories and marketing pages, no live threads. No community signal today.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Today's thread is trust that comes from location rather than verification. A coding agent obeyed an AGENTS.md because it sat in the folder the agent was started in. Tor users handed over SSH passwords because the exit relay sat in the path they had chosen. Toronto tipsters trusted an anonymity promise that sat with a brand while the data sat with a vendor. The FCC now bans devices by what sits on the circuit board, not just by the name on the box. And PHP2Shell finally fell on a server running a current PHP branch just one point release behind the fix. In each case the trust was inherited from a place, a path or a logo, and nobody had checked what was actually there. For a decision-maker, the useful question this week is: which of our controls trust something because of where it is, and which trust it because we verified it?
