## 🚨 Government Advisories

ACSC: nothing new in the last 24h. Its latest alert is still the 9 Sep Adobe Commerce and Magento exploitation alert.

**EU Cyber Resilience Act Reporting Went Live Friday: 24 Hours to Flag an Exploited Flaw**
Source: European Commission | [Link](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cra-reporting)
Since 11 Sep 2026, manufacturers of products with digital elements must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through ENISA's new Single Reporting Platform: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days of a fix being available (one month for severe incidents). Open-source stewards come under the same duty from 11 Dec 2027; the Commission's page does not state penalties, so none are quoted here.
💡 Strategic take: The clock now starts on the day you learn of an attack, not the day you ship the fix. Twenty-four hours is shorter than most vendors take to convene a triage meeting, so the vulnerability inbox has become a regulated function that needs a named owner and an on-call rota. A US or Australian vendor selling software into Europe is on the same clock, and European buyers will start asking for proof of it in procurement.

**Dutch NCSC Expects Large-Scale Exploitation of the Check Point VPN Flaws**
Source: NCSC-NL | [Link](https://www.ncsc.nl/alerts/kritieke-kwetsbaarheden-in-check-point-vpn-producten-met-actief-misbruik-verwacht-update-nu)
The Dutch National Cyber Security Centre rates both likelihood and impact as high for CVE-2026-85102 and CVE-2026-85103 (both CVSS 9.8, unauthenticated code execution during VPN setup) and says it expects active exploitation at scale soon; it advises patching now and, for Site-to-Site VPN, disabling implied rules and restricting VPN access to known IP addresses. What moved: Saturday's brief carried Check Point's "no indication" of exploitation; no public proof of concept or attack has been reported yet, but a national CERT is now forecasting one (alert dated 10 Sep, reported 12 Sep).
💡 Strategic take: When a national CERT forecasts exploitation before any proof of concept is public, the forecast is the warning, and there may not be a second one. The GitLab flaw in Saturday's brief went from disclosure to probing within hours. The IP restriction is worth doing even on a patched gateway, because it shrinks the target for the next VPN bug as well as this one.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Revolut Hands KYC Files to a Fake Government Request Sent From a Real Government Domain**
Source: TechCrunch | [Link](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/revolut-confirms-customer-data-breach-through-fake-government-requests/)
Revolut confirmed on 12 Sep that an unauthorised third party used a legitimate government agency email domain to send fraudulent information requests, which it treated as genuine and answered with identity and contact details, passport and driver's licence copies, and possibly verification selfies, account statements and transaction histories. It says "a limited number" of customers were affected and declines to name the agency or the market; coverage suggests the targets appear to have been wealthy individuals.
💡 Strategic take: A lawful-access request is a skeleton key the law obliges you to honour, and this one was cut from a real government mailbox. Domain authentication proved where the email came from, not whether the request was real, so the check has to sit outside email: a contact at the agency you already hold. Australian clubs and casinos field police and regulator requests for patron data too, and that step belongs in the written procedure.

**Microsoft: Passkey Lures Are the Pretext, Your Microsoft 365 Identity Is the Target**
Source: Microsoft Security Blog | [Link](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/09/09/passkey-themed-social-engineering-leads-identity-cloud-compromise/)
Microsoft says crews including Storm-3121 (linked to ShinyHunters and Falcon extortion) and Storm-3032 (the Helix extortion brand) have, since May 2026, phoned or texted staff posing as the IT help desk with urgent passkey, MFA or SSO "updates", then steered them into adversary-in-the-middle phishing pages or device-code sign-in flows. Once in, they register their own MFA methods to keep access and pull mail, SharePoint and OneDrive data through Microsoft Graph at volume to support extortion; the post ran 9 Sep and drew wider coverage this weekend.
💡 Strategic take: The passkey rollout has become the cover story: the more a company talks about going passwordless, the more believable a call about "updating your passkey" sounds. Device-code sign-in is a door most organisations never use and never closed, and blocking it in Conditional Access is an afternoon's work. The harder control is a help desk that never starts an authentication change by phone, and staff who know that rule by heart.

**Florida Traces DMV Database Breach to One Police Officer's Login on a Personal Device**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/florida-confirms-dmv-database-breached-via-stolen-police-account/)
Florida's Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles says the attacker who reached its DAVID driver database used credentials of a single Plant City Police Department user that were "improperly stored on the employee's personal electronic device"; it learned of the breach on 4 Sep. ShinyHunters, the extortion brand Microsoft ties to Storm-3121 above, claims more than 200,000 driver records and says it used a password-reset flaw; the agency has not said how many records were taken and attributes the access to the stolen credentials.
💡 Strategic take: A state register shared across hundreds of agencies is only as strong as the weakest device that can sign in to it, and here that was one officer's personal machine. Read beside Revolut, the pattern is plain: government credentials are now a product criminals acquire and reuse. Any organisation that grants partner or agency logins into its data should be able to say how many exist, whether each needs phishing-resistant MFA, and how fast one can be revoked.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**Microsoft Finds AI Fingerprints on a Million-Email CEO Invoice Scam, and Says It Cannot Measure Them**
Source: Microsoft Security Blog | [Link](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/09/10/protecting-organizations-ai-assisted-executive-impersonation-invoice-fraud/)
Microsoft says a campaign sent more than a million emails between 3 and 5 Aug impersonating target companies' own CEOs alongside a fake ServiceNow annual-subscription invoice chain, asking accounts payable for an ACH payment of nearly $50,000; 87.7% of the volume targeted US organisations. It reads "extensive HTML comments, structured section labeling, and highly uniform template construction" as signs of generative AI, while saying those indicators do not establish how much of the content AI actually wrote.
💡 Strategic take: Business email compromise used to be a craft trade, one careful letter at a time; templates that look machine-built let one crew mail a million tailored ones in three days, and the return still needs only one clerk who pays. Microsoft's caveat is the honest part of the report and should travel with the headline whenever it is repeated. The event to verify is a new or changed payee, whatever the email looks like, because the email will only get better.

**Intezer: AI Alerts Are 0.43% of the SOC Queue, and the Fastest-Growing Slice**
Source: The Hacker News | [Link](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/when-whole-company-adopts-ai-what-it.html)
In a sponsored piece, Intezer, which sells an AI SOC platform, says AI-related alerts across roughly 16.9 million SOC alerts it analysed grew 685% between February and June 2026 while still making up 0.43% of the total. It traces the growth to developers' coding agents running shells and touching credentials, and to staff granting OAuth consent to third-party AI apps, and concludes that so far the cost is alert volume rather than breaches.
💡 Strategic take: Name the interest first: a vendor selling AI triage has found that AI generates alerts that need triage. The finding is still useful because it shows where the new noise comes from, and both sources are permission grants: an agent holding a shell and an app holding an OAuth token. The cheaper fix sits upstream of any SOC tool, in an allow-list of OAuth scopes for AI apps and a separate, logged identity for every coding agent.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Today's pattern is borrowed authority. Revolut answered a request sent from a real government domain; Florida's driver register was opened with a real police login; Microsoft's crews pose as your own help desk and then enrol their own MFA; the invoice scam wore the CEO's name and a real vendor's brand. None of these breaks anything technical. Each borrows a trusted identity and lets the organisation's own process finish the job. The regulators are working the other end: the EU now gives vendors 24 hours to say an attack is under way, and the Dutch CERT is calling exploitation before it happens. The practical move for decision-makers is to list every request the organisation obeys because of who it appears to come from (legal requests, help-desk resets, payment changes, partner logins) and give each one a check that does not travel through the channel the request arrived on.
