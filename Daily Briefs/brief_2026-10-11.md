## 🚨 Government Advisories

**CISA's fix-or-stop deadline on the five Integrity Technology Group flaws lands today**
Source: Security Affairs | [Link](https://securityaffairs.com/200734/security/u-s-cisa-adds-proftpd-onlyoffice-docs-strapi-apache-struts-and-isc-bind-flaws-to-its-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog.html)
Moved since yesterday's brief: the federal remediation deadline of 11 October for the ProFTPD, ONLYOFFICE Docs, Strapi, Apache Struts and ISC BIND entries in the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is now today. Security Affairs ties the additions to the joint advisory naming China-based Integrity Technology Group, and lists CVSS scores from 7.2 to 10.0. No new ACSC advisory has been published since the 9 October joint advisory on the same company (checked cyber.gov.au alerts page this run).
💡 Strategic take: A deadline only binds federal civilian agencies, but it is the cleanest external benchmark a board has. If your patch team cannot say today whether any of these five are in your estate, the gap is asset inventory, not patching speed.

## 📰 Top Security News

**FBI arrests a ransomware negotiation firm founder in the ShinyHunters investigation**
Source: CyberScoop | [Link](https://cyberscoop.com/edward-dubrovsky-cypfer-arrested-fbi-extortion-charges/)
Edward Dubrovsky, 54, a founder and former COO of Canadian negotiation firm CYPFER, was arrested in Pennsylvania on a federal charge of conspiracy to commit extortion, linked to the ShinyHunters breach of FBI IT systems. KrebsOnSecurity reports, citing sources, that charges against principals at other negotiation firms may follow. Neither Dubrovsky nor CYPFER has commented, and the charge is an allegation, not a finding.
💡 Strategic take: Ransom negotiators sit inside your worst week with your board, your insurer and your attacker all on the line, and there is no licensing regime for them. Whatever this case proves, the procurement lesson stands now: vet the negotiator the way you vet the forensics firm, and make sure your incident retainer says who picks them, you or the insurer.

**SonicWall SMA 1000 SSRF flaw drew exploitation attempts within 24 hours of an advisory saying there were none**
Source: Forkast | [Link](https://forkast.news/sonicwall-sma-1000s-latest-ssrf-door-was-being-walked-through-within-24-hours-of-disclosure/)
SonicWall's 6 October advisory SNWLID-2026-0017 said there was no evidence of in-the-wild exploitation. Honeypot data from threat intelligence firm Previdian shows attempts against CVE-2026-102255, an unauthenticated SSRF rated CVSS 10.0, began within a day. The report counts more than 400 internet-exposed SMA 1000 appliances. The exploitation evidence comes from a single vendor's sensors.
💡 Strategic take: "No known exploitation" in a vendor advisory is a statement about the vendor's visibility on publication day, not a forecast. Edge appliances should be patched on a clock that starts at disclosure, because the attacker's clock already has.

**Ransomware crews are using a Palo Alto GlobalProtect authentication bypass to log in as ordinary VPN users**
Source: Cyber Security News | [Link](https://cybersecuritynews.com/palo-alto-globalprotect-flaw-vpn-access/)
Cyber Security News reports that Qilin and Settra affiliates are exploiting CVE-2026-0257, an authentication bypass in PAN-OS GlobalProtect and Prisma Access, to obtain VPN sessions that blend into legitimate traffic. Advice is to upgrade, disable unneeded authentication override cookies, and review past VPN sessions, because access obtained before patching may persist. This is secondary reporting; Palo Alto's own advisory is the primary.
💡 Strategic take: A front door that lets the attacker in as a valid user turns every downstream control into a guess. Patching closes the door but does not evict whoever already walked through, so the real work this week is reviewing VPN logs back to the disclosure date.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**BleepingComputer: the South Korean bank intrusions used Claude agents alongside ARTEX, and ARTEX has gone closed-source**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hacker-used-artex-ai-and-claude-agents-to-target-south-korean-banks/)
Moved since Friday's brief: BleepingComputer reports the Chinese-speaking operator behind the bank attacks used Claude agents as well as the ARTEX agentic pentest suite, citing CrowdStrike, which says it has identified possible personal details of the operator. Following the attacks, the ARTEX project switched from open source to closed source. The operator reportedly sought buyers for the stolen data but showed no direct monetisation plan.
💡 Strategic take: Closing the source after the fact is the agentic equivalent of locking the stable door, because the code that ran the attacks is already forked and mirrored. The durable control is not the tool, it is the exposed WebLogic server it found; defenders cannot regulate the agent, only the attack surface it reads.

**Korean banks sat through an AI hacking briefing weeks before the breaches, and lawmakers now want to know why nothing changed**
Source: Aju Press | [Link](https://m.ajupress.com/view/20261011150451696)
Aju Press reports that the Financial Security Institute ran a seminar on AI hacking techniques weeks before the attacks, attended by security staff from some of the institutions later breached. About 40,000 Yegaram Savings Bank and 25,000 Shinhan Bank customers were affected. Lawmaker Han Chang-min said the upcoming National Assembly audit will focus on the banks' failure to fix known internet-facing vulnerabilities.
💡 Strategic take: This is what liability looks like once AI attacks stop being novel: the question shifts from "could you have known" to "you were told, what did you do". For regulated firms in Australia and the UK, minutes of a threat briefing with no follow-up action are now evidence against you, not for you.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Today's thread is trust in the intermediaries. A vendor advisory said "no exploitation" and was wrong within a day. A VPN bypass lets attackers arrive as trusted users. A ransomware negotiator, the person a victim hands the keys to in a crisis, is in federal custody on an extortion charge. And South Korean banks were warned about AI-driven attacks by their own sector body and still left the door open. None of these is a new technique; each is a party you relied on to tell you the truth or act on it. For a decision-maker, the useful question this week is not "what new threat is coming" but "which third party's word am I treating as a control", and then writing down how you would know if that word was wrong.
