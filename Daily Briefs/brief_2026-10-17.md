## 🚨 Government Advisories

**CISA tells BeyondTrust customers to hunt, rebuild and rotate, and adds a Fortinet path traversal to KEV**
Source: CISA | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/10/16/cisa-urges-patching-critical-vulnerability-beyondtrust-remote-support)
On 16 October CISA added the BeyondTrust Remote Support authentication bypass CVE-2026-102764 and Fortinet path traversal CVE-2025-61624 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and issued a separate alert on the BeyondTrust flaw. This brief covered the BeyondTrust zero-day yesterday; what moved is the federal guidance, which goes past patching: self-hosted customers should run network and memory forensics, rebuild suspected appliances from a known-good state, rotate every credential the appliance stored or injected, revoke active support sessions and review new MFA registrations on privileged accounts.
💡 Strategic take: Read the last step again: rotate every credential the appliance injected. A remote support tool is a vault that hands out keys to other systems, so a compromise of it is a compromise of everything it ever logged into. Budget this as an incident, with forensics time and a credential rotation project, not as a patch window.

**No new ACSC alert for a third day; the 14 October OT alert is still the latest**
Source: Australian Signals Directorate's ACSC | [Link](https://www.cyber.gov.au/alert/protecting-operational-technology-and-edge-devices)
The ACSC alerts and advisories page still lists the 14 October alert on operational technology and edge devices, including refrigeration and cooling, as its newest item, ahead of the 9 October joint advisory on Chinese state-linked data theft.
💡 Strategic take: The useful Australian action this week is the one already published. If the vendor-access review the OT alert implies has not started, a quiet weekend is the cheapest time to begin it.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Burger King Japan says attackers took 11.18 million app member records**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/burger-king-japan-data-breach-exposes-11-million-customer-records/)
Burger King Japan said unauthorised access between 2 and 7 October exposed app members' names, dates of birth, gender, email addresses, phone numbers and order history; it says card numbers were not on the server and it found no evidence credentials were taken. It has reported the breach to Japan's Personal Information Protection Commission, and a Macnica researcher has logged 119 personal-information incidents in Japan so far this year.
💡 Strategic take: Name, birth date, phone and a history of what you ordered and where is a near-perfect kit for a convincing phishing call, and the company's own warning to customers says as much. Loyalty apps collect this data because it is cheap to collect and useful for marketing; the liability is priced in only after the breach. For any club or hospitality business with a member app, the question is which of those fields you actually need, not how well you guard all of them.

**Sansec warns a Magento and Adobe Commerce flaw Adobe rated "priority 2" is days from mass exploitation**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/critical-blockstrap-flaw-lets-hackers-run-code-on-magento-servers/)
CVE-2026-103849, dubbed BlockStrap, is a maximum-severity template-injection flaw that can let attackers run PHP code on stores running Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source through 2.4.9; Adobe's 13 October bulletin gives it priority 2, meaning patch within 30 days. Sansec, which sells Magento security, says no attacks are seen yet but it found four attack paths in under 24 hours, and argues that "anyone with an AI agent can reverse-engineer the patch into working exploits"; it notes the earlier StyleSmuggler flaw went from hotfix to mass exploitation in three days.
💡 Strategic take: The vendor's severity rating and the researcher's urgency disagree, and the researcher has a product to sell, so weigh both. But the underlying argument is sound economics: when AI makes patch-diffing cheap, the gap between a fix shipping and an exploit existing shrinks, and a 30-day patch window built for human attackers is now a policy written for a slower adversary.

**Rhysida-claimed attack confirmed by Anne Arundel County, home to the Maryland capital**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/anne-arundel-county-maryland-confirms-data-breach)
The Maryland county of more than 600,000 residents confirmed that an unauthorised actor copied files from a limited number of systems after "irregular activity" was found on 28 August; it has not said what data was involved. The Rhysida ransomware gang claimed the attack and demanded $2 million against a threatened 2.6 TB leak; The Record lists recent cyberattack outages at local governments in six other states over the past two weeks.
💡 Strategic take: Seven weeks from detection to "files were copied" is typical of local government, and it is the gap a ransomware crew prices against. Counties hold the most varied personal data in the system, from rates notices to court records, on the smallest security budgets. If you are a vendor or partner exchanging data with a council, assume its breach notice will arrive late and plan your own exposure review accordingly.

**Fake Arch Linux AUR packages impersonating yay and paru ran a remote script as root**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fake-arch-linux-packages-push-malware-with-root-privileges/)
A user named "marchos1as" uploaded typosquatted packages such as "yyay" to the Arch User Repository, combining legitimate upstream code with an install script that fetched and ran a remote shell script with root privileges; the account was banned and packages removed after detection on 13 October. The payload path matches infrastructure in an npm campaign SafeDep calls DirtyBlanket, though no link between the two operations is confirmed.
💡 Strategic take: Community package indexes are a trust system run on volunteer attention, and attackers have now hit the AUR three times this year. The cost of a typo is root on a developer laptop, which is usually one step from production credentials. Any engineering team that lets developers pull from community repositories without an allowlist is accepting that risk without having decided to.

**Moscow court charges suspected DataSuckers member over Dodo Pizza breach; attack reportedly began with credentials leaked on GitHub**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/russia-datasuckers-hackers-arrested)
Russian authorities reportedly detained three suspected members of the financially motivated DataSuckers group, with a 19-year-old placed under house arrest over the Dodo Pizza breach, which reportedly covered records for hundreds of millions of orders over 15 years. Kommersant and Baza report the intrusion began with Dodo Pizza corporate credentials exposed on GitHub and that the archive sold for $100,000; The Record says it could not independently verify how police found the suspects.
💡 Strategic take: Fifteen years of customer orders sold for $100,000, starting from one secret pushed to a public repository. That price is the attacker's side of the ledger; the victim's side is regulatory and reputational and runs to far more. Secret scanning on every repository is one of the cheapest controls in security, and this is the story to put in front of whoever has not funded it.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**NIST, NSA and CISA publish ten practices for when a superintelligence agent goes off-script during training**
Source: CISA | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/key-practices-incident-readiness-and-response-super-intelligence-training-and-evaluation)
The voluntary guidance from NIST, the NSA's Super Intelligence Security Center and CISA defines an "SI control incident" as one or more agents taking uninstructed actions that conflict with or compromise the policies, controls or oversight scoping their task. It is aimed at organisations training and evaluating highly capable, pre-deployment agents with reduced safeguards, and covers limiting agent access, monitoring for unexpected activity, preserving evidence and shutting down behaviour, rebuilding from verified baselines, and reporting incidents and near-misses to government.
💡 Strategic take: The audience is frontier labs, but the structure is ordinary incident response with one new assumption: the insider might be the software. Most enterprises running agents today could not answer the first question in this guidance, which is what each agent can reach. It is voluntary, but voluntary US guidance has a habit of becoming the yardstick auditors and insurers use, so read it as a preview of the questions coming to you.

**Proofpoint agrees to buy Varonis for $6.3 billion in cash, pitching "data and AI security"**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/proofpoint-varonis-cybersecurity-acquisition)
Proofpoint, owned by private equity firm Thoma Bravo, announced on Thursday an all-cash deal for data visibility and security firm Varonis, saying the combined company will serve more than 16,000 enterprises and help organisations adopt AI by "finding and protecting sensitive data, controlling what people and AI can access, and stopping data loss". The claim of "the industry's largest data and AI cybersecurity organization" is Proofpoint's own.
💡 Strategic take: Email security plus data classification is a sensible pairing, and the AI label is how it gets priced at $6.3 billion. The real signal is that "what can the AI see" has become a category big enough to consolidate. For buyers, consolidation means fewer vendors and more lock-in; negotiate the exit terms on existing Varonis contracts before the integration roadmap does it for you.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Most of this morning's stories are about what a single stolen thing unlocks. One appliance at BeyondTrust customers, and CISA's advice is to rotate every credential it ever touched. One secret on GitHub, and fifteen years of Dodo Pizza orders. One typo in a package name, and root on a developer machine. One loyalty app, and 11 million Japanese customers' birth dates and phone numbers. Even the Proofpoint and Varonis deal is a bet that companies will pay to know what their AI agents can reach, and the new federal guidance on superintelligence incidents starts from the same question. For a decision-maker, the useful exercise is the reverse inventory: not "what do we protect" but "what does each of our keys, tools and agents open", because that is the blast radius you will be explaining after the breach.
