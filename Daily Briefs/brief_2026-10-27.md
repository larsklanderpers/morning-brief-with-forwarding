## 🚨 Government Advisories

**CISA puts both PHP2Shell flaws on its exploited list, a day after a honeypot caught a working chain**
Source: CISA | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/10/26/cisa-adds-two-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)
Moved since Monday's brief. On 26 October CISA added CVE-2026-109618 (PHP memory corruption) and CVE-2026-109619 (PHP stale internal request-state) to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and updated its 21 October PHP alert. Under BOD 26-04, federal civilian agencies must prioritise KEV entries on publicly exposed assets that grant total control, and check whether they were compromised before the patch went on.
💡 Strategic take: Yesterday the evidence was one honeypot; today it is a federal listing, and that changes the conversation with your auditors and insurers. The BOD 26-04 clause worth stealing for the private sector is the second one: patching is not the end of the job, you also have to ask whether someone got in during the gap. For a PHP estate that has been exposed since 22 October, that is a compromise assessment, not a change ticket.

**Ukraine's CERT-UA warns that Dahua cameras and recorders are being taken over through the Easy4IP cloud feature**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/ukraine-cert-ua-warning-chinese-dahua-cameras-hacked)
CERT-UA says it found cases where attackers compromised internet-connected Dahua cameras and video recorders, changed their settings and created unauthorised user accounts. The Record reports the access ran through Easy4IP remote access, with predictable serial numbers, weak passwords and two old critical flaws (CVE-2021-33044 and CVE-2021-33045). CERT-UA did not say how many devices were affected.
💡 Strategic take: A camera is a sensor that the attacker gets to aim. The weak point here is not exotic: a vendor cloud relay, a guessable identifier and a five-year-old bug. Any venue that runs CCTV on a manufacturer's remote-access service should ask who else can reach that relay, and the rogue accounts mean a firmware update alone does not evict anyone.

**No new ACSC alert since Friday's Fortinet notice**
Source: Australian Cyber Security Centre | [Link](https://www.cyber.gov.au/alerts-and-advisories)
The latest items on the ACSC alerts page are critical Fortinet vulnerabilities (23 October) and critical PHP vulnerabilities (21 October). Nothing has been added since.
💡 Strategic take: A quiet ACSC page is not a quiet threat picture: the PHP alert it already published is the one CISA just confirmed is being exploited. Australian teams that took the 21 October notice as "patch soon" should now read it as "patch and hunt".

## 📰 Top Security News

**Microsoft ships emergency .NET patches for the Brotli flaw, now going by three names**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/microsoft/microsoft-releases-emergency-net-patches-for-critical-brotli-flaw/)
Moved since Saturday's brief, when the flaw was a Node.js and Bun problem. Microsoft has released out-of-band updates for .NET 8, 9 and 10 and a release candidate of .NET 11 for CVE-2026-111497, an out-of-bounds write in the Brotli decoder, tracked as Broccoli2Shell, Node2Shell and HTTP2Shell. BleepingComputer reports it can lead to crashes, memory leaks or remote code execution in clients and servers that accept Brotli-compressed data. Where updates cannot go on straight away, Microsoft advises blocking Brotli-compressed requests or turning off Brotli decoding.
💡 Strategic take: The bug is in a compression library, which means it is everywhere you did not list as a dependency. Node, Bun and now .NET all carry it, and BleepingComputer reports Python HTTP clients may too. The useful question this week is not "are we on .NET?" but "where in our stack does something decompress data a stranger sent us?", and most software inventories cannot answer it.

**University of Copenhagen says hackers reached a test environment holding data on 525,000 people**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/university-of-copenhagen-data-breach-exposes-data-of-525-000-people/)
The university detected the intrusion on 24 October and disclosed it two days later. The exposed data includes full names, Danish national identity numbers, phone numbers and email addresses, and PIN codes for about 20,000 staff and students were also exposed. It is working with the Danish Data Protection Agency and says daily operations were not disrupted.
💡 Strategic take: It was a test environment, and it held half a million real national ID numbers. That is the pattern worth naming to your developers: test systems get production data because synthetic data is work, and then they get production-grade breaches with development-grade controls. Under GDPR, the regulator will not care which environment it was.

**ENGIE confirms customer data was stolen through a partner's marketing platform**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/energy-giant-engie-discloses-data-breach-affecting-customers/)
The French energy group says attackers breached a marketing platform used by its business partners, not its internal systems, and took customer contact details and some energy contract information. It says no banking data was involved, the incident is limited to customers in France, and it has notified authorities. It has not said how many customers are affected.
💡 Strategic take: "Our systems were not breached" is now the standard first line, and it is technically true and practically irrelevant. The customer gave their data to ENGIE; where ENGIE then put it is ENGIE's problem. Every marketing tool your partners log into is part of your attack surface, and it usually has the weakest access controls of anything holding customer records.

**DreamHost forces email and FTP password resets in an "incident" it has not explained**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/dreamhost-forces-email-ftp-sftp-password-resets-in-ongoing-incident/)
The hosting provider has forced password resets for email and FTP/SFTP users after connection problems with its File Manager that began on 23 October and spread to other services. Affected account owners cannot log in until they reset. BleepingComputer reports DreamHost has not said what happened, and advises users to check their email forwarding rules and change any reused passwords.
💡 Strategic take: A forced reset with no explanation tells you the provider suspects credentials are out but will not yet say so. The forwarding-rule advice is the tell: an attacker with mailbox access sets a quiet forward and keeps reading after the password changes. If any of your domains, marketing sites or vendor mailboxes sit on DreamHost, check the rules, not just the passwords.

**Apple backports an exploited CoreGraphics zero-day fix to older iPhones and iPads**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/apple/apple-backports-coregraphics-zero-day-patches-to-older-iphones/)
Apple has brought patches released last month for CVE-2026-86950, an out-of-bounds write in CoreGraphics exploited in what Apple calls "extremely sophisticated" targeted attacks, to older iPhones and iPads.
💡 Strategic take: Targeted iOS exploits are aimed at people, not fleets: executives, journalists, officials. The older device in a senior person's drawer, still signed in to email, is exactly the kind that waits a month for its fix. If you have a mobile policy for leadership, this is the week to check whether it covers the second phone.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**South Korean police say a former pentester's gang used the ARTEX AI framework to breach more than 90 organisations**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/former-pentester-used-artex-ai-to-steal-82-million-data-records/)
Seoul Metropolitan Police identified seven suspects in an operation that hit schools, hospitals, large companies and golf courses, stole 8.2 million records and demanded about 360 million won (about US$270,000) in ransom, per BleepingComputer on 26 October. Police say the 32-year-old leader moved to AI tools in August 2026 and used the ARTEX agentic pentesting suite in most of the attacks; only the leader is in custody. ARTEX is the same framework Microsoft tied to the ConnectWise attack covered on Sunday.
💡 Strategic take: Last week ARTEX was a nation-state's tool; now it is a seven-person crime gang's. That is the price curve of offensive capability in one week: what needed a state budget now needs a former contractor and a laptop. The ransom total is the telling number, because about $270,000 across 90 victims is small-business extortion run at enterprise scale, and that only pays when the labour is automated.

**LiteLLM says the supply-chain attack came through a master key it found exposed in May and never revoked**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/litellm-hackers-ai-malware)
Moved since Friday's brief. LiteLLM said on Friday that a configuration file containing the master key to an internal tool it uses to run AI agents had been exposed months earlier; it spotted and removed the file at the time but did not revoke the key. Attackers used that key to push the malicious 1.107.0 release. Wiz says the compromised package was downloaded about 49,000 times, and users of that version are told to assume their credentials were stolen.
💡 Strategic take: Deleting a leaked key is tidying up; revoking it is the security control, and five months separated the two here. The irony is sharp: the key belonged to the tool that runs LiteLLM's own AI agents, so the agent's keyring became the attacker's way in. Every team has a "we removed it from the repo" incident somewhere in its history; the audit question is whether anyone rotated what was in it.

**Texas sues OpenAI and names Sam Altman personally, over ChatGPT safety claims**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/texas-sues-openai-deceiving-customers)
Missed on Friday and worth catching up. Texas filed suit under its deceptive trade practices law, seeking civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation and injunctions that would bar minors from ChatGPT, require a court-appointed expert to approve new models, and stop the chatbot giving tips on suicide, eating disorders and violence. The suit seeks to hold Altman personally liable. OpenAI says it works with experts on safeguards and wants AI policy that applies to the whole industry, not one company.
💡 Strategic take: This is a consumer-protection suit, not an AI law, and that is the point: states do not need new AI statutes when existing deceptive-practices law already covers marketing that says "safe". Any business putting a chatbot in front of customers should read its own safety claims as a regulator would. A court-appointed expert approving new models would be an outside party in the release process, and every AI vendor will be watching whether a judge grants it.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The thread today is the gap between knowing and acting. LiteLLM knew its key had leaked in May and removed the file without revoking the key. The University of Copenhagen knew what a test environment was for and still loaded it with half a million national ID numbers. CISA's PHP listing comes with an instruction to check for compromise during the patch gap, because the patch only closes the door and says nothing about who already came in. Meanwhile ARTEX went from state tool to street tool in a week, which shortens every one of those gaps from the attacker's side. For a decision-maker, the question for this week is not "what do we know is wrong?" but "of the things we already knew, which ones did we tidy up instead of fixing?"
