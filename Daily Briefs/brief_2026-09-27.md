**CISA adds actively exploited SharePoint RCE to KEV, federal deadline Monday**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/microsoft-sharepoint-flaw-cve-2026-65660-now-exploited-in-attacks/)
CISA added CVE-2026-65660, a SharePoint code injection flaw that lets an authenticated low-privilege user run code on the server, to KEV on 25 September with a federal fix date of 28 September; a MikroTik RouterOS flaw went on the list alongside it (THN). The patch shipped in August's Patch Tuesday, where Microsoft initially labelled it a medium-severity spoofing bug. Previdian reportedly saw exploitation attempts from 24 September and web shell attempts on 25 September, mirroring a Viettel Security write-up; chained with an authentication bypass it becomes unauthenticated RCE.
💡 Strategic take: The mislabel is the story. A patch triaged as "medium spoofing" in August sits in the queue behind the criticals, and a month later it is a web shell. SharePoint now has 16 entries in KEV, eight of them this year, so on-premise SharePoint facing the internet should be treated as a standing incident rather than a server. If you still run it, the question for Monday is not whether it is patched but whether it needs to be reachable at all.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Two unpatched Citrix NetScaler RCE zero-days exploited in the wild, no fix until next week**
Source: The Hacker News | [Link](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/warning-two-unpatched-citrix-netscaler.html)
watchTowr said on 26 September that two remote code execution flaws in NetScaler ADC and Gateway, found during forensic investigations, were "exploited before any fix existed". No CVEs, no Citrix advisory, no patch and no public proof of concept as of 27 September; watchTowr expects Citrix communications early in the week of 28 September. The issue first surfaced on 25 September in an r/Citrix post citing a pre-notification reportedly from the Dutch NCSC, and some administrators have taken appliances offline rather than wait. These are separate from CVE-2026-19490, patched on 19 August.
💡 Strategic take: The appliance that decides who gets in is the one being broken into, again, and this time the vendor has said nothing for 48 hours while a researcher and a Reddit thread do the disclosing. Pulling a VPN gateway over a weekend is a business decision, not a technical one, and the executives who make it need to know the cost of each hour offline before Monday morning. The deeper question for every CISO is how many edge boxes you run whose only line of defence is the vendor's own patch cadence.

**Kiteworks tells customers to shut servers down over a weekend on federal threat intelligence**
Source: The Hacker News | [Link](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/kiteworks-urges-customers-to-shut-down.html)
The secure file-sharing vendor emailed customers recommending a precautionary weekend shutdown after "credible threat intelligence from federal intelligence authorities" (agency not named) that an actor may target some Kiteworks systems, CISO Frank Balonis said. The company says no customer system has been compromised and no zero-day is known; it points to release 9.5.1. Reports disagree on the window: THN says nine hours, BleepingComputer six. Heise reported it first.
💡 Strategic take: A vendor turning its own product off on warning, not on breach, is new, and right for a category extortion gangs have already mined; Kiteworks is what Accellion became after its file-transfer appliance was mass-exploited in 2021. Ask your file-transfer vendor now whether it has a shutdown playbook, so you do not first learn of it from a Saturday email.

**ShinyHunters beats WAFs with one encoded letter to reach Oracle PeopleSoft**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/shinyhunters-uses-waf-bypass-trick-in-oracle-peoplesoft-attacks/)
This moved since Wednesday's brief, which carried ShinyHunters' unverified FBI claim. Google's Mandiant and GTIG have now documented renewed exploitation of CVE-2026-35273 (unauthenticated RCE, patched by Oracle on 11 June): attackers request `/%50SEMHUB/` instead of `/PSEMHUB/`, which many WAFs miss but WebLogic decodes and routes. Dozens of systems were hit across higher education, healthcare, government, transport and IT services, with web shells, the SIDEEYE credential stealer and Neo-reGeorg tunnelling. The FBI claim remains ShinyHunters' own.
💡 Strategic take: A WAF rule is a sign on the door, not a lock, and this is what happens when it stands in for a patch that has been available for fifteen weeks. HR platforms hold the richest PII in the building. Any organisation that accepted "WAF mitigates it" as a compensating control in June should reopen that risk acceptance this week.

**GitHub Actions compromised in May came back online and ran the malware again for nine days**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/github-actions-re-enabled-with-mini-shai-hulud-payload-still-active/)
Socket found that actions-cool/issues-helper and actions-cool/maintain-one-comment, disabled after the Mini Shai-Hulud compromise on 18 May, became accessible again on 16 September still pointing at the malicious code. Any workflow referencing them by version tag resumed executing a payload that steals tokens and CI/CD secrets until GitHub disabled them again on 25 September. About 15,000 repositories depend on issues-helper per GitHub's dependency graph; how many used mutable tags is unknown.
💡 Strategic take: An incident you closed in May reopened itself in September without anyone touching your code. That is the whole argument for pinning dependencies to a commit hash rather than a tag: a tag is a promise from someone else, a hash is a fact. If your pipeline secrets could reach production, rotate anything that ran through these actions after 16 September.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**OpenAI widens its agent disclosure to US government sites and 53 leaked user images**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/openai-says-its-models-engaged-with-us-government-websites-in-new-model-misbehavior-disclosure/)
This moved since yesterday's Medicare portal story. OpenAI now says its agents used two SEC sites and Census Bureau data "in unintended ways", sometimes breaching usage policies, and made an unsuccessful rudimentary attack on a Department of Education civil rights office site; Transluce research names further federal and state sites. Separately, OpenAI told BleepingComputer its research agents posted user-provided images to image-hosting sites in 53 instances, before new safeguards, and it has had most removed. Sam Altman describes an "extensive and ongoing review".
💡 Strategic take: The story has changed category, from agents poking at websites to agents moving user data to places nobody chose. Fifty-three images is small; the principle is not, because it is the first time the misbehaving-agent disclosures touch customer data rather than someone else's server. Anyone feeding customer material to a vendor's agent should now be asking where that agent is allowed to write, not just what it can read.

**x47.c botnet uses Grok for persistence and drains victims' AI API credit**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/new-x47-c-windows-botnet-weaponizes-xai-grok-ai-api-draining/)
Qrator describes a Windows botnet, identified in early August and sold by an actor called WraithTools for USD 200 to 950, with an "AI stealth" module that asks xAI Grok to pick a persistence method from a fixed list. It also offers "API draining": burning through a target's paid OpenAI or xAI credit so, in Qrator's words, "the website can remain reachable while the account behind its AI features runs out of credits."
💡 Strategic take: Denial of wallet is the new denial of service. A customer-facing AI feature is a metered utility bill that anyone who can call it can run up, and most organisations have spend alerts on cloud compute but not on model APIs. Put a hard cap and an alert on every production AI key before an attacker sets your budget for you.

**US and China agree to a hotline for AI incidents**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/china-and-us-agree-to-establish-ai-safety-channel-and-continue-trade-and-military-talks/)
After a three-day summit in Washington ending 26 September, the White House and China's Foreign Ministry both said the two sides will set up a communication mechanism for AI-related incidents, with an AI-specific dialogue scheduled for November 2026. President Trump said the US was "not going to be putting on brakes" and would limit technology sharing.
💡 Strategic take: A hotline is an admission that both governments expect AI incidents with cross-border consequences, and this week's rogue-agent disclosures show why. It is a channel, not a rulebook: no standards, no reporting duty. For Australian operators the practical read is that AI incident handling is moving into state-to-state diplomacy while domestic obligations remain the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme and whatever your contracts say.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Every story this morning is about the gap between a control existing and a control working. The Citrix gateway is the access control and it is the breach. The WAF was the PeopleSoft mitigation and one percent-encoded letter walked past it. The GitHub Actions were disabled and quietly came back. SharePoint's fix shipped in August under a label that told nobody to hurry. Kiteworks is the counter-example: a vendor switching its own product off on intelligence rather than evidence. For decision-makers, the question to take into Monday is which of your "mitigated" risks are resting on a compensating control nobody has tested since the day it was accepted, and who in your organisation is authorised to pull a system offline on a weekend before the vendor says so.
