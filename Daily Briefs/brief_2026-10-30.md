## 🚨 Government Advisories

**CISA lists the max-severity GitLab flaw and Thursday's Cisco firewall zero-day as exploited**
Source: CISA | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/10/29/cisa-adds-two-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)
On 29 October CISA added CVE-2026-97229 (GitLab CE and EE integer overflow) and CVE-2026-76418 (Cisco ASA and FTD) to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The Cisco entry moved since Thursday's brief, when the evidence was Cisco's own statement. SecurityWeek reports federal agencies have three days to fix the GitLab flaw under BOD 26-04, and that it is rated CVSS 10 and reachable by an unauthenticated attacker through a crafted regular expression in a CI/CD configuration.
💡 Strategic take: Your source control server is the one machine that can write to everything you ship, so a pre-auth code execution bug there is a supply chain incident waiting for a date. Three days is the federal clock, and it is a fair clock for anyone else. Treat GitLab with the same urgency as the firewall on the same list.

**CISA says the Fragnesia Linux bug can be chained with PHP2Shell, and a vendor report shows that chain reaching a firewall management server**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/cisa-warns-of-fragnesia-linux-flaw-chained-in-php2shell-attacks/)
Moved since Tuesday's PHP2Shell listing. CISA added CVE-2026-46300 (Fragnesia, local root on Linux kernels released before 13 May 2026) to KEV on 28 October and says it "can be chained" with the PHP flaws to run commands as root. Security firm Dream says a campaign rooted 25 servers across the US, Japan, Taiwan and South Korea on 23 and 24 October, then took a firewall management server. Outlets differ on the US government-affiliated servers (three in BleepingComputer, two in SecurityWeek). Dream calls it Chinese-nexus and names no actor.
💡 Strategic take: A May kernel patch would have stopped this chain at the web tier, so the five-month-old bug did more damage than the five-day-old one. Most teams are watching the PHP patch and not asking what the web server user can become. Rank the local privilege escalation bugs on internet-facing boxes alongside the remote ones, because the pair is what the attacker actually buys.

**36 state attorneys general settle with GM for $220 million over selling driver data**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/general-motors-lawsuit-privacy)
GM agreed on Thursday to pay $220 million and to obtain affirmative express consent before collecting or sharing driving behaviour and location data, The Record reports, citing Washington Attorney General Nick Brown's release (the release itself was not read for this brief). The states alleged GM sold data from its Smart Driver programme to data brokers, who passed it to insurers, despite promising not to; dealers were allegedly paid bonuses to enrol customers. GM says the programme ended in 2024. A separate Texas suit continues.
💡 Strategic take: The dealership bonus is the detail to hold on to: consent was a sales target, not a customer decision. Anyone running a loyalty programme should ask the same question of their own sign-up desk, because a staff member paid per enrolment is writing your consent records. At $220 million, that is an expensive place to leave unsupervised.

**No new ACSC alert since last Friday's Fortinet notice**
Source: Australian Cyber Security Centre | [Link](https://www.cyber.gov.au/about-us/view-all-content/alerts-and-advisories)
The ACSC alerts page, checked this morning, still shows "Critical vulnerabilities in Fortinet products" (23 October) as its most recent alert, after PHP (21 October). Nothing material from ACSC in the last 24 hours.
💡 Strategic take: The PHP alert on that page predates the Fragnesia chain, so a team reading only the ACSC notice is working from a smaller picture of the risk than the one CISA now describes. Local teams on PHP should add kernel patching to the same ticket.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Binance loses about $6.3 billion in the largest crypto theft on record, reportedly starting from one engineer's laptop**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/6-billion-binance-hack-bears-hallmarks-of-north-koreas-tradertraitor/)
Binance puts the loss at roughly $6.3 billion; TRM Labs traced about $6.36 billion leaving on 29 October, mostly in about 20 minutes. Binance suspects a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop and reports no evidence of a private key leak, though it has "uncovered additional points of breach." TRM assesses the attacker could make the hot wallet signing system sign chosen transactions, and with Chainalysis attributes the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor; Binance has named no one. TRM says about $2.4 billion is beyond any issuer's reach. Binance says it will cover all losses.
💡 Strategic take: Nobody stole the keys, which is the unsettling part: the attacker reportedly just got the machine that uses the keys to say yes. A vault is only as strong as the clerk who can ask it to open, and most key management programmes spend their budget on the vault. In any business that moves money or loyalty points on a signed instruction, the real asset to protect is the service allowed to request the signature.

**Polymarket briefly halts trading after a monitoring script on its site started draining users, with researchers pointing to DebugBear**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/2-million-drained-from-polymarket-traders-in-new-supply-chain-attack/)
Polymarket suspended trading on its international platform on 29 October, saying a third-party provider was compromised and that those affected will be made whole. Researchers tie the code to DebugBear, a UK website performance monitoring firm, which confirmed an ongoing incident and told customers of its real user monitoring product to pull its snippet. Hudson Rock says the script reused the authority of logged-in sessions to sell positions and move proceeds. Loss estimates run from about $2.19 million to $2.71 million and none is verified. Polymarket disclosed a similar third-party script compromise in June.
💡 Strategic take: A performance monitor sits inside your logged-in page with the same powers as your own code, and this one was there to measure load times. Twice in five months at one company says the lesson did not reach the script inventory. Any tag running on a page where customers hold value should be self-hosted or pinned, because "it only collects metrics" is a description of intent and not of capability.

**Japan Airlines ties its breach to Amadeus, and three other Asian carriers report impact**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/japan-airlines-suffers-third-party-data-breach-exposing-passenger-info/)
Moved since Thursday's brief, which flagged a Japanese cluster with no known link. JAL says Amadeus told it on 23 October of unauthorised access to its Passenger Service System between 10 August 2023 and 23 September 2026, exposing PNRs, names and routes for about 90,000 customers, plus passport numbers and contact details for about 2,000 more. BleepingComputer reports ANA, Asiana and Korean Air have also linked customer data exposure to Amadeus; ANA's report comes from local media. Amadeus has not said how many airlines are affected. Nothing links this to the Sony ISP or GMO incidents.
💡 Strategic take: Three years and six weeks is the number that matters. A reservation system used by more than 400 airlines is the kind of supplier nobody can replace and few can audit, and the airline learns of the breach when the supplier decides to say so. Ask how far back your critical suppliers' access logs go, because you can only tell customers what your supplier managed to detect.

**Drupal warns a highly critical Commerce Core flaw is being used against online stores**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/drupal-commerce-core-vulnerability-exploited-against-online-stores/)
Drupal says CVE-2026-118539, an access bypass in the Commerce Core contributed module used on more than 42,000 sites, is being exploited by unauthenticated attackers, and that it "may financially impact the vendor." Fixes are in 3.3.13 and 8.x-2.41. Drupal has not said how it is exploited or by whom, and tells sites to audit orders for inconsistencies.
💡 Strategic take: "Audit your orders" is a rare thing to read in a vulnerability advisory, and it tells you the bug sits in the money path rather than the server. Patching closes the door, but only finance can tell you what already walked out, so this one needs a reconciliation and not just a change ticket.

**Oracle E-Business Suite flaw draws probes a week after patching, on one firm's decoys**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/hackers-start-probing-fresh-oracle-ebs-vulnerability/)
Threat intelligence firm Defused says its decoys recorded exploratory exploit attempts on 27 October against CVE-2026-101767, an unauthenticated SQL injection in EBS (CVSS 9.8) patched in Oracle's October update that can lead to remote code execution. There are no other reports of in-the-wild exploitation so far.
💡 Strategic take: Decoy traffic is a smoke alarm, not a fire, and the evidence is one vendor's sensor. But Cl0p turned EBS into a mass extortion campaign last year, so finance systems that sit on a quarterly patch cycle should not wait for a second data point.

**Belgium takes its main tax portal offline after a cyberattack, days before a filing deadline**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/cyberattack-disrupts-belgian-tax-services-before-deadline)
The Federal Public Service Finance suspended MyMinfin on Wednesday ahead of a 31 October deadline for some taxpayers, who will get extra time. It says there is currently no indication of a data breach, while investigators continue to examine that. Authorities have not named an attacker or said how access was gained, nor established any link to this month's PHP-driven website shutdowns.
💡 Strategic take: Pulling a national tax portal days before a deadline is a costly decision, and a sensible one if you do not yet know what you are looking at. The cost of containment is now part of the attack's return, whether or not any data left. Boards should know in advance who is authorised to switch off the revenue-critical system, and on what evidence.

**US bankruptcy court notices restart after a two-week outage at BAE-run system, and BAE says no data was taken**
Source: The Record | [Link](https://therecord.media/us-bankruptcy-courts-it-contractor-says-no-data-stolen-cyberattack)
The Bankruptcy Noticing Center, maintained by BAE Systems, is coming back with a backlog dating to 7 October. BAE says "no data was extracted." Court officials declared a Bankruptcy Rules emergency allowing judges to extend deadlines, which they expect to keep in place through restoration. No group has claimed the attack.
💡 Strategic take: Nobody needs to steal anything to do damage to a system whose whole job is telling people about deadlines. A notice that arrives late can cost someone their rights, and "no PII breached" does not capture that harm. Availability of an unglamorous service is its own risk category.

**319 Chrome extensions with 13.4 million reported installs hijacked searches, and could swap the destination at will**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/chrome-extensions-with-134-million-installs-caught-hijacking-searches/)
Socket's John Tuckner says the unlisted extensions posed as PDF converters and security tools, routed searches through operator-controlled proxies and collected Bing and Yahoo referral payments. Some needed no code at all, only a manifest setting that replaces the default search provider. Socket warns the proxy could send users to phishing pages without any extension update, and says most have been removed. Socket sells software supply chain security.
💡 Strategic take: An extension with no permissions and no code passed every instinct a reviewer has, and still owned every search that user made. Search queries from staff are a rich record of what a company is working on. If browser extensions are not on an allow list on managed machines, this is a one-line policy change worth making today.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**Malware on Hugging Face returned within days of takedown, and a fake repo is reportedly positioned for AI coding agents to find**
Source: BleepingComputer | [Link](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hugging-face-malware-campaign-returns-to-hijack-gpus-for-crypto-mining/)
Pluto Security found 31 malicious repositories across seven accounts abusing the trust_remote_code option to install a cryptominer and, in some versions, a reverse shell on GPU servers. The top model reached 28,412 downloads (downloads, not confirmed infections), scanned clean, and a copy was still live when BleepingComputer published. Separately, Air Security says a fake Solana starter kit ranks first when AI coding agents search for one, and that its README's only instruction installs NetSupport RAT. Both researchers sell security products; BleepingComputer independently confirmed the live copy and the payload.
💡 Strategic take: The first story is an old trick on a new shelf: a model that is really a program, and scanners that check the label. The second is the more interesting one, because a README is now an instruction to a machine that will not squint at it. Local model hosting is the right call for PII, but it only keeps data in the building if the model files get the same review as any other code you run.

**Google says Gemini 4 Carbon beats rivals at finding, exploiting and fixing flaws, on benchmarks it reported itself**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/google-says-gemini-4-carbon-leads-in-finding-exploiting-and-fixing-vulnerabilities/)
Google says Carbon scored 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro, 54.8% on ExploitGym and 71.0% on CWE-bench, ahead of OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1 in its own comparison, with no guardrails in place. Access starts with vetted defenders through its Fairwind programme, which Google says has more than 650 organisations. Pricing is $2 and $10 per million input and output tokens at introductory rates. No independent verification of these scores was found for this brief.
💡 Strategic take: Exploitation at 54.8% for that price means the argument about whether AI can do this work has moved on to who gets it first. Staged release to vetted defenders buys time, not safety, and the window is set by the next open-weights release. Budget for the fix side now, because finding has become the cheap half of the job.

**Cisco open-sources Quarry, a rule language for AI agent attacks that only appear across a sequence of harmless steps**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/cisco-open-sources-quarry-to-catch-ai-agent-attacks-hidden-in-harmless-steps/)
Project Quarry combines machine learning judgment with exact rule checks across multi-turn conversations, tool use and MCP interactions, for runtime guardrails, analytics and incident response. It ships with a scanner, a Python SDK and a plugin that lets coding agents write rules. Cisco wants it to become a shared standard in the way Snort, YARA and Sigma did. Cisco sells AI security products, and adoption is so far an aim rather than a fact.
💡 Strategic take: Bank fraud teams learned long ago that no single purchase looks criminal; the pattern does. Agent security has just arrived at the same place, and that is the useful idea here whether or not this project wins. If you run agents, start by keeping the full action trace, because no rule language can help with steps you never recorded.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The thread today is authority borrowed rather than stolen. Binance reports no leaked private key: the attacker reportedly got its systems to sign what it wanted. The Polymarket script did not need passwords, because it reused the sessions customers had already opened. Fragnesia turns a web server user into root, and the Dream chain turned one firewall into the console for all of them. Even the Amadeus access, three years long, ran through a system airlines trust by default. Credential theft is a problem security teams know how to measure; this is harder, because every action looks valid to the system recording it. The question for a decision-maker this week: which of our systems will do whatever a trusted caller asks, and what would have to be true for us to notice that the caller had changed?
