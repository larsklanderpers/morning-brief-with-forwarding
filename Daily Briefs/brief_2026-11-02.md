## 🚨 Government Advisories

**Federal deadline for the first of Friday's two Ivanti Connect Secure flaws falls today, with no new CISA notice found over the weekend**
Source: Security Affairs | [Link](https://securityaffairs.com/202175/security/u-s-cisa-adds-ivanti-connect-secure-flaws-to-its-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog.html)
Moved since Saturday's brief only on the calendar. CISA gave federal agencies until 2 November to fix CVE-2026-111357 and until 13 November for CVE-2026-118527, both chained with the earlier authentication bypass CVE-2026-104935 in attacks reported since early October. Searches this morning turned up no KEV additions or alerts after Friday; the cisa.gov catalog itself was not read directly.
💡 Strategic take: A two-day federal deadline over a weekend tells you how CISA rates this chain. Anyone running Connect Secure who treated Friday's notice as a Monday job is already behind the government's own clock. The second flaw's longer runway is not a reason to wait, because the attackers use the two together.

**ACSC alerts page still tops out at the 23 October Fortinet notice**
Source: Australian Cyber Security Centre | [Link](https://www.cyber.gov.au/alerts-and-advisories)
A live check this morning shows the same order as last week: Fortinet (23 October), PHP (21 October), operational technology and edge devices (14 October), and the Chinese state-linked tooling advisory (9 October). Nothing on BeyondTrust, Ivanti or the Amadeus breach, even though Qantas says it has reported the Amadeus matter to the ACSC.
💡 Strategic take: A quiet alerts page is not a quiet week. The reporting is flowing into the ACSC rather than out of it, which is how it should work, but it means Australian operators are getting this week's signal from airlines and vendors first.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Hackers push fake radiation and chemical alerts through Ukraine's and Russia's emergency apps on the same night**
Source: Associated Press via The Washington Times | [Link](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/nov/2/ukraine-russia-report-fake-emergency-alerts-cyberattack-targets-apps/)
Ukraine's air raid alert app sent false warnings late Sunday of high radiation, a chemical threat and public disturbances in Kyiv, in poor English and mixed Russian and Ukrainian, and was suspended and restored overnight. Russia's Emergency Ministry says its app also sent fake alerts. A Russian Telegram channel linked to the security services says a group called Hitcore claimed the attack; AP could not verify that. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said a growing number of attacks on state agencies use AI.
💡 Strategic take: The target was not data, it was trust in the one notification people are told never to ignore. Both governments fell back on sirens, SMS and broadcast within hours, which is the real lesson: an alerting channel without an independent backup is a single point of panic. Any operator that pushes safety or fraud alerts to members through an app should ask what happens the night that app lies.

**Qantas joins the Amadeus disclosures, says its own systems were not touched, and does not yet know about its customers**
Source: Newstalk ZB | [Link](https://www.newstalkzb.co.nz/news/business/airlines-including-qantas-cathay-pacific-impacted-in-amadeus-cyber-attack/)
Moved since Sunday's brief, which covered Malaysia Airlines and Cathay Pacific. Qantas says Amadeus told it of unauthorised access to one of its systems, that Amadeus says the issue was contained in September, and that Qantas is still working out whether any Qantas bookings were affected. It has reported the matter to the ACSC and the national cyber security office. Amadeus says it has no evidence payment data was accessed.
💡 Strategic take: This is now an Australian Privacy Act story, not a foreign one. Qantas will carry the notification and the reputational cost for a system it does not run, a year after its own breach made it the country's reference case. Every Australian business that hands customer data to a global platform should check its contract for who notifies, how fast, and who pays.

**Fresh probes hit BeyondTrust Remote Support decoys, as a public Metasploit module raises the odds of wider attacks**
Source: Cyber Security News | [Link](https://cybersecuritynews.com/critical-beyondtrust-vulnerability/)
Moved since the 20 October brief, when exploitation was attributed to the suspected China-linked espionage group ARMORY CASTLE. Previdian reports two probes on 1 November against decoy appliances in the US and Europe, both from one Hong Kong IP, resembling a Metasploit module still under review. It saw no successful exploitation. Self-hosted appliances need the BT26-05 patch; BeyondTrust says hosted customers were patched by 9 October.
💡 Strategic take: Two requests from one address are a weather vane, not a storm. But the shift matters: a flaw once worth spending only on espionage targets now has tooling anyone can run. Remote support tools sit on top of everything else, so treat an unpatched appliance as a set of master keys left at the front desk.

**BTCPay Server tells operators to switch off their instances after reports of drained hot wallets**
Source: CoinDesk | [Link](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/11/02/btcpay-a-bitcoin-payment-tool-tells-users-to-switch-off-their-servers-after-reports-of-drained-wallets)
On 1 November the open-source Bitcoin payment processor told operators to stop their servers while it investigates. Reports say older releases appear to be involved, with outlets giving different version cutoffs, and a co-founder reportedly said attackers gained administrator access. BTCPay has given no root cause, victim count or total loss.
💡 Strategic take: "Turn it off" is the most honest instruction a vendor can give and the most expensive one a merchant can receive. Self-hosting buys control and costs you the patch discipline a provider would otherwise carry. The safe hot-wallet balance is the one you can afford to lose tonight.

## 🤖 AI & Security

**Copilot in Visual Studio could be talked into rewriting its own config with a trailing dot, and the fix shipped on 13 October**
Source: SecurityOnline | [Link](https://securityonline.info/cve-2026-103572-visual-studio-copilot-rce-disclosed/)
Not covered here before. CVE-2026-103572 (CVSS 8.8, rated Important by Microsoft) let a prompt-injected Copilot agent write to `.mcp.json.`; Windows drops the trailing period, so the protected `.mcp.json` file was changed and an attacker-chosen MCP server could be launched. Researcher Johann Rehberger published the details and a proof of concept. Fixed builds are Visual Studio 2022 17.14.42 and Visual Studio 2026 18.10.4; reports say no exploitation has been confirmed, and the SSMS fixed version was not confirmed for this brief.
💡 Strategic take: The guard rail checked a string, and the operating system disagreed about what the string meant. That is the shape of most agent security failures this year: the model is not the weak point, the plumbing around it is. If your developers run Copilot in Visual Studio, check the build number today rather than trusting auto-update.

**Attackers posted 83 booby-trapped issues and comments across 61 GitHub projects in 43 minutes, including Google's LangExtract and MLflow**
Source: Cyber Security News | [Link](https://cybersecuritynews.com/hackers-target-61-github-repositories/)
Radware says the campaign ran on 1 September and relied on GitHub Actions workflows that pass user-written text from issues, pull requests and comments into shell commands. Named targets include LangExtract, MLflow, Trivy and Brave. Command execution was reportedly confirmed in two workflows; Radware found no conclusive evidence of secrets stolen or code changed. Radware's own report was not read for this brief.
💡 Strategic take: An issue comment is now an input to someone's build server, and AI projects are where the automation is thickest. The two-month gap between the attack and the report is the uncomfortable part, because a stolen CI secret is used long before anyone writes it up. Audit every workflow that runs on comments from strangers.

## 📌 Today's Signal

Every story this morning is about a channel people trusted to speak for them: the emergency app that tells a city to shelter, the booking platform that holds an airline's customers, the remote support box that holds the admin keys, the payment server that holds the float, the CI bot that reads strangers' comments, the coding agent that edits its own config. None of these attacks needed to break the thing at the centre. They went for the intermediary, because the intermediary is trusted by default and watched by nobody in particular. For a decision-maker the question this week is not "are we patched" but "which of our trusted messengers could lie to us, or for us, and how would we know?" Map them, give each one an owner, and make sure each has an independent fallback, the way Kyiv still had sirens.
