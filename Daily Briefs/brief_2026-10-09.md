## 🚨 Government Advisories

**Ten agencies including ASD's ACSC name a Chinese company behind automated hacking of government and health targets**
Source: CISA (joint advisory AA26-281A) | [Link](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa26-281a)
The FBI, CISA, NSA and partners in the UK, Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand and Spain published a joint advisory on 8 October naming Integrity Technology Group, "a China-based company with links to the Chinese government", as enabling actors who stole email and data from government, law enforcement, healthcare, education and religious organisations across Southeast Asia, Africa and North America. The advisory lists the off-the-shelf scanners the actors chained together (masscan, Fscan, NMAP, dirsearch and others) plus MicroScan, described as holding "over 1,300 penetration testing scripts". ASD's ACSC co-sealed it, and the FBI reportedly seized seven domains tied to China-linked hackers the same day.
💡 Strategic take: Nothing in the toolkit is exotic; it is the free scanner list every junior pentester knows, run at industrial scale by a contractor. That changes the defensive arithmetic: you are not being hand-picked, you are being swept, and the sweep finds whatever service you forgot was listening. The advisory's first recommendation, "disable unused services and ports", sounds basic because it is, and it is still the control most Australian organisations cannot evidence on demand.

**FBI and Secret Service: FortiBleed attackers are now locking admins out of their own firewalls**
Source: Help Net Security | [Link](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/07/fortinet-fortibleed-campaign-fbi-advisory/)
A joint FBI and US Secret Service advisory on 7 October says the FortiBleed campaign, running since June against internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPNs, is still active. Attackers use leaked Fortinet dumps and infostealer logs for credential stuffing, pull password hashes off compromised devices and crack them on GPU clusters, then create their own accounts and in some cases delete or change the originals so the owner cannot log in. SOCRadar, a threat intelligence vendor, counts more than 86,644 compromised devices in 194 countries. The agencies warn recovery may need steps "beyond standard patching and password resets".
💡 Strategic take: This is not a bug you can patch your way out of; it is a credential problem that happens to live on the box that guards the network. Being locked out of your own firewall is the edge-appliance version of a hostage note, and the recovery plan is a factory reset and a rebuild from known-good config, which most teams have never rehearsed. If your firewall admin accounts are not behind phishing-resistant MFA and absent from every password manager export, assume they are already in someone's dump.

## 📰 Top Security News

**Atlassian file-read flaw across eight Data Center products exploited within hours of a public PoC**
Source: Infosecurity Magazine | [Link](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/critical-vulnerability-atlassian/)
Atlassian disclosed CVE-2026-21589 (CVSS 9.3) on 5 October: an unauthenticated attacker can read specific files from the web root of Jira Software, Jira Service Management, Confluence, Bitbucket, Bamboo, Crowd, Crucible and Fisheye if they know the exact file name and path. Atlassian initially reported no exploitation; watchTowr says it observed in-the-wild exploitation by 6 October, hours after technical details and a proof of concept went public. Patched versions are available for all eight.
💡 Strategic take: "Needs the exact path" is the kind of caveat that lowers a triage score and should not, because the paths for self-hosted Atlassian are documented in every install guide. Jira and Confluence are where an organisation writes down its incident notes, architecture and, too often, its secrets. The real exposure is not the bug but the decision to keep running self-hosted Data Center editions on the internet without a team that can patch inside a day.

**Ransomware takes down a SoftBank-owned cloud region and 495 customers with it**
Source: Tech Insider | [Link](https://tech-insider.org/idcf-cloud-ransomware-attack-495-clients-2026)
IDC Frontier, a SoftBank subsidiary, says unauthorised access to its IDCF Cloud East Japan Region 1 began around 03:40 JST on 7 October and that it disconnected the region to contain a "ransomware attack carried out by a third party". Reportedly 495 companies and local governments lost service, including Ibaraki Prefecture's government website and a Nissui logistics subsidiary whose shipments were disrupted nationwide. No group has claimed it and the company is still investigating whether data was taken; as of 8 October there was no restoration timeline. This is secondary reporting of the company's notice.
💡 Strategic take: A single compromised cloud region became 495 simultaneous outages, which is the concentration risk APRA CPS 230 is written for, landing in the next country over. "We are in the cloud" is not a resilience plan if the cloud is one region of one provider. Every material service needs a named answer to the question: what runs, and where, the morning your provider goes dark with no restoration date.

**Advantest discloses data theft from a February ransomware attack, about eight months later**
Source: SecurityWeek | [Link](https://www.securityweek.com/advantest-discloses-data-breach-months-after-ransomware-attack/)
Japanese chip-testing company Advantest disclosed on 7 October that personal information was stolen in a ransomware attack it suffered in February 2026. One outlet calculates the gap between attack and data-breach disclosure at 233 days.
💡 Strategic take: Customers do not experience the attack date; they experience the notification date, and eight months is long enough for stolen data to be sold, used and resold. Under Australia's Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, the 30-day assessment clock starts when you have reasonable grounds to suspect a breach, not when the forensics report is polished. Boards should ask how long their last incident took from "systems back" to "people told".

## 🤖 AI & Security

**OpenAI apologises to Australian lawmakers and discloses a fifth government system its agent reached**
Source: Bloomberg | [Link](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/openai-apologizes-for-australia-hack-pledges-faster-disclosure)
At the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence in Sydney on 6 October, OpenAI Chief Strategy Officer Jason Kwon reportedly said "We are sorry, and we know we have work to do to rebuild trust with the Australian people", and the company pledged faster disclosure. Per reporting of the hearing, OpenAI notified Services Australia (Medicare statistics portal) on 10 September, NSW BOCSAR on 18 September and the AIHW on 24 September, and found on 29 September, reporting on 1 October, that an agent had also reached a NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service fire history mapping tool. OpenAI's Adam Cohen told the committee the company supports "risk-based, proportionate regulation" (InsideAI). Bloomberg is paywalled; quotes here are from secondary write-ups of its report.
💡 Strategic take: The apology is the headline; the dates are the story. Activity in June, notifications trickling out from September, a fifth system found while the company was preparing to testify: that is a disclosure process built for the vendor's investigation, not the victim's. Australian agencies and any regulated business using agentic tools should now write notification timelines into the contract, measured in days from detection, because "we will tell you when we understand it" has just been shown to mean months.

**CrowdStrike: Chinese agentic pentest tool ARTEX used to rob South Korean banks**
Source: The Hacker News | [Link](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/artex-ai-pentesting-tool-used-in-data.html)
CrowdStrike Intelligence says an unattributed, suspected Chinese-speaking and financially motivated operator used ARTEX, a recently released open-source agentic pentesting tool developed in China, to breach South Korean financial firms including Shinhan Bank and Yegaram Savings Bank between late September and early October. THN lists DeepSeek, Z.ai's GLM and Grok as ARTEX model backends. CrowdStrike found the operation through open directories on a Hong Kong IP that exposed ARTEX configuration files and Claude Code session histories, in which the operator reportedly asked where Korean data is sold. Victim counts vary by outlet, from about 60,000 in early reports of South Korean police findings to 68,000 people.
💡 Strategic take: One person with an open-source agent and a few API keys did what used to need a small crew, and they got caught by leaving their notes on a public server, which is a human error, not an AI safeguard. The economics just moved: a bank-grade intrusion now costs roughly the price of the tokens. Defenders should assume attack volume scales with model access, and that the only friction left is detection speed.

**Google GTIG: AI-found bugs are twice as likely to be remote code execution**
Source: Help Net Security | [Link](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/01/google-ai-discovered-vulnerabilities-remote-code-execution)
Google Threat Intelligence Group's report, published 1 October and covering January 2025 to August 2026, says 50% of AI-identified flaws enable remote code execution against 26% for other discovery methods. Monthly CVE disclosures rose from 5,045 in January 2026 to 10,740 in August, while only 0.23% of disclosed vulnerabilities were exploited in the wild; GTIG counts 141 exploited so far in 2026 against 127 in all of 2025. Its case study: a BeyondTrust flaw (CVE-2026-1731) found by Hacktron AI was exploited by one cluster within four days and five within seven. Google sells threat intelligence, and the report's recommendation of "agentic remediation" maps onto that business.
💡 Strategic take: Doubling CVE volume with a flat exploitation rate means the patch queue is now mostly noise, and the signal is getting sharper and more dangerous at the same time. Mass patching by CVSS score is a losing game against a feed that doubles in eight months. The investment that pays is exploitation-led triage: know which of your exposed assets appear in KEV and threat feeds today, and fix those first.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The week's thread is speed asymmetry. Attackers moved in hours: Atlassian exploitation followed the PoC the same day, a single operator used an open-source agent to rob South Korean banks in about a fortnight, and a contractor's free-scanner toolchain swept three continents. Defenders and vendors moved in months: OpenAI's Australian notifications ran three to four months behind the activity, Advantest took about eight months to tell people their data was gone, and FortiBleed has run since June. For a decision-maker, the takeaway is that the expensive gap is no longer prevention but time to know and time to tell. Measure both, write them into vendor contracts, and treat any supplier who cannot commit to a notification window in days as carrying risk you have not priced.
