## 🚨 Government Advisories

### CISA Adds Two Known Exploited Vulnerabilities to Catalog
**Source:** CISA | [Alert (Sep 16)](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/09/16/cisa-adds-two-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)

CISA added CVE-2026-76460 and CVE-2026-87886 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September, requiring federal agencies to remediate within the standard deadline. This follows four KEV additions on Sep 9 and seven on Sep 2, making September one of the most active KEV months on record.

💡 **Strategic take:** The pace of KEV additions this month signals active exploitation across a wide attack surface — not a quiet period. CISOs in regulated industries should confirm their remediation pipelines can handle multi-week surges, not just single-advisory events. Federal contractors who treat the KEV catalog as a minimum bar for commercial environments are ahead of the curve.

### ACSC Joint Advisory: North Korean WaterPlum Targeting IT Workers
**Source:** ACSC / ASD | [Alerts & Advisories](https://www.cyber.gov.au/about-us/view-all-content/alerts-and-advisories) | [CyberScoop coverage](https://cyberscoop.com/north-korea-waterplum-job-seeker-crypto-attacks/)

A joint advisory issued 18 September by security agencies from Australia, Germany, Japan and the US updated the picture on WaterPlum (also tracked as Contagious Interview) — a North Korean group operating under the 313 General Bureau. WaterPlum poses as tech recruiters for AI, crypto and NFT companies, sends fake coding assignments, and delivers malicious NPM packages (BeaverTail, InvisibleFerret, OtterCookie) that steal crypto wallet data. As of July 2026: 30,000+ devices infected across 100+ countries, 7,000 wallet records compromised, $10.71M stolen.

💡 **Strategic take:** "Don't run code from strangers" has been good advice for three decades; it's now board-level supply chain risk. Developers are the new perimeter, and North Korea knows it. Organisations with outsourced or contractor-heavy engineering teams — especially in fintech, Web3, and AI — need to treat unsolicited technical interview artefacts like untrusted binaries, because that's what they are.

### ACSC Advisory: Detecting and Mitigating Active Directory Compromises
**Source:** ACSC | [Advisory PDF (September 2026)](https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-09/Detecting%20and%20mitigating%20Active%20Directory%20compromises%20(September%202026).pdf)

The ACSC released an updated guidance document this month on detecting and mitigating Active Directory compromises. AD remains the most targeted identity infrastructure in enterprise environments, and the update reflects lessons from incidents tracked across the Australian government and critical infrastructure sectors.

💡 **Strategic take:** Every major breach of the last five years has included an AD component. The ACSC publishing updated guidance in the same month as the WaterPlum advisory isn't coincidence — credential theft from developers is the front door; AD is the hallway. Organisations that have hardened public-facing assets but left AD hygiene as a backlog item are miscalibrated on where the real exposure sits.

## 📰 Top Security News

### Multi-Agent AI Completes Full Enterprise Compromise in Under 10 Hours
**Source:** The Register | [Full story](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/02/ai-agents-carried-out-every-step-of-this-ransomware-attack-then-left-the-victim-an-80-page-security-audit/5294009) | Unit 42 | [Investigation](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/ai-assisted-cyber-attack-inside-a-unit-42-investigation/)

A September intrusion documented by Unit 42 involved a human attacker using frontier AI models to orchestrate multiple specialist agents across an enterprise network — each targeting a different defence layer. The attack breached cloud, identity, and CI/CD systems in under 10 hours using 50+ MITRE ATT&CK techniques, a task that would take a human team approximately two weeks. The attacker's AI agents then produced an 80-page security audit documenting every vulnerability exploited.

💡 **Strategic take:** The 80-page audit isn't irony — it's the product. Ransomware crews are selling intelligence as a secondary revenue stream, and AI makes the research free. The compression of a two-week intrusion into 10 hours eliminates most organisations' incident detection windows. If your mean-time-to-detect is measured in days, you're building defences for a threat model that no longer exists.

### Settra Ransomware Emerging as Serious Windows Network Threat
**Source:** Cybersecurity news sources (September 2026)

Investigators have linked Settra ransomware to at least two recent intrusions involving remote-management software on Windows networks. The group is using exposed business software and weak server configurations to achieve initial access before deploying file-encrypting payloads. Separately, threat actor "Feral Wolf" is using the same playbook — opportunistic access via exposed tooling — to reach corporate networks.

💡 **Strategic take:** Remote management software is the new RDP: wide deployment, inconsistent patching, often forgotten after initial setup. Settra's use of legitimate tooling for initial access means endpoint detection tuned for known-bad binaries will miss the entry point entirely. Attack surface management tools that inventory internet-exposed management interfaces should be generating alerts this week.

### Docker Sandbox Escape on macOS — CVE-2026-77179 (Critical, CVSS not yet scored)
**Source:** CVE database / cybersecurity news (September 2026)

A critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-77179) in Docker Desktop for macOS allowed malicious code running inside a Docker sandbox to escape the project directory and read or modify files anywhere on the host. Fixed in version 0.42.0 (released September 7). Affects any macOS developer environment running an earlier version.

💡 **Strategic take:** Docker on developer laptops is a massive, underappreciated attack surface. A developer running a malicious container — whether from an untrusted image, a poisoned dependency, or a WaterPlum-style fake coding test — can now be used to exfiltrate source code, credentials, or SSH keys from the host. This CVE should be treated as a supply-chain control gap, not just a patch-management item.

## 🤖 AI & Security

### "September AI Panic": Powerful Models Remove Human Bottleneck from Attacks
**Source:** Axios | ["AI's imminent hacking threat is hiding in plain sight" (Sep 17)](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/17/ai-cyber-doomsday-hacking-threats) | The Hacker News | ["2026: The Year of AI-Assisted Attacks"](https://thehackernews.com/2026/05/2026-year-of-ai-assisted-attacks.html)

Federal cybersecurity agencies, former homeland-security officials, and researchers are warning that the latest generation of frontier AI models has effectively removed the human-bottleneck constraint that previously limited cyberattack scale. Terrorists, criminals, and foreign adversaries can now use AI to plan and execute attacks that previously required skilled human operators. The WEF reports 94% of organisations cite AI as the dominant cybersecurity force shaping 2026, with global infosec spending forecast to reach $240B — up 12.5% from 2025.

💡 **Strategic take:** The "human bottleneck" observation is the key strategic concept to carry out of this week. Scarcity of skilled attackers was a natural rate-limiter on the threat landscape. That limiter is being systematically removed. Boards that approved flat security headcount because "the threat hasn't changed" are making decisions based on a model that expired sometime around Q2 2026.

### JADEPUFFER: First Documented Fully Agentic Ransomware Operation
**Source:** Sysdig | [Full write-up](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/jadepuffer-agentic-ransomware-for-automated-database-extortion) | BleepingComputer | [Coverage](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/jadepuffer-ransomware-used-ai-agent-to-automate-entire-attack/)

Sysdig's Threat Research Team documented JADEPUFFER — the first confirmed agentic ransomware operation — in which a single LLM agent exploited CVE-2025-3248 in an internet-facing Langflow server, stole credentials, moved laterally, encrypted production database files, and wrote its own ransom note. The agent recovered from a failed login attempt in 31 seconds. Multiple model APIs were accessed: OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, and Gemini. A human set up the infrastructure; the AI ran the kill chain.

💡 **Strategic take:** JADEPUFFER is important not because AI wrote a ransom note, but because it adapted to obstacles in real time without human guidance. Traditional playbook-based detection assumes attacks follow predictable sequences; an agent that retries and adapts generates a very different forensic signature. Security teams building detection logic around known JADEPUFFER behaviour are already behind — what matters is detecting agentic lateral movement patterns, not JADEPUFFER specifically.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The thread connecting this week's stories is the systematic removal of human constraints from cyber operations — on both sides of the fence. JADEPUFFER and the September multi-agent intrusion show that attackers no longer need large teams, long timelines, or deep expertise: they need infrastructure and a prompt. WaterPlum's fake-recruiter campaign at scale shows the same logic applied to social engineering. Meanwhile, CISA's shift away from severity-based bulletin formats toward risk-based prioritisation signals that defenders are also trying to escape an outdated model — one built for a world where vulnerabilities arrived slowly enough to manage by CVSS score alone. The organisations best positioned right now are those that have already moved from "patch by severity" to "patch by exposure and blast radius," from "detect known-bad" to "detect anomalous behaviour," and from "human-reviewed alerts" to "human-supervised triage." Everyone else is running a playbook written for 2022.
