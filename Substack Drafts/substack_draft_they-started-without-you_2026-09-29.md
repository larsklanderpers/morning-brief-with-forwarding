# They Started Without You

Your people did not wait for the program. They have been using this for a year, on their own accounts, and most of them have never told you.

There is a slide deck going around every large company right now, and I have seen enough versions of it to describe yours without seeing it. Slide four is the burning platform. It explains, with a stock photo, why the organization must change and what the risk of standing still is. Slide seven is the communications plan. Slide nine has a timeline with a kickoff on it.

The deck is competent. The person who built it knows what they are doing. And it is aimed at a problem that, in this particular case, you do not have.

## The canon was built for a change nobody wanted

The change-management field has two dominant frameworks and almost everyone reaching for one is reaching for a version its own author replaced.

John Kotter published "Leading Change" in 1996, out of a 1995 article, and the first two of his eight steps are establishing a sense of urgency and forming a guiding coalition. That ordering is not an accident. It is the whole theory. The model assumes the change starts at the top, that the organization does not want it, and that leadership's first job is to manufacture enough discomfort with the present that people will move.

You have probably been told that some large share of change initiatives fail, and been told that Kotter is the source. He is not. There is no failure rate in the 1995 article. Mark Hughes went back in 2011 and read every published source behind the figure people quote and found no valid, reliable evidence behind any of them; the trail runs back to a 1993 book whose authors described their own estimate as unscientific, and whose lead author later said there was no inherent success or failure rate at all. Nobody lied at any point. Every step was a reasonable citation of the step before it, which is how this happens.

What IS in the 1995 paper is one number, and I find it much more interesting than the one everybody quotes. Kotter wrote: "Well over 50% of the companies I have watched fail in this first phase." The first phase is establishing urgency.

Hold that thought, because the first phase is the one AI adoption does not need.

Then there is ADKAR, which is Jeff Hiatt's model and Prosci's product: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement, in that order, each one supposedly required before the next. Two honest things about it. The first is that it contains a genuinely excellent idea, which I will come back to and which is the reason this piece exists. The second is that its evidence base is house data. The benchmarking is drawn from a self-selected sample recruited out of the vendor's own ninety-thousand-member learning center, the same respondent rates both the quality of the change management and whether the project succeeded, and it has never been independently replicated.

I want to be careful here, because there is a sloppy version of this criticism and it is wrong. ADKAR has not been refuted. It has barely been tested. In twenty years, the only quantitative external test I can find that measured ADKAR scores against an independently observed outcome was a 2021 study of thirty broiler farms in Belgium and the Netherlands, which coached farmers on antimicrobial stewardship, watched their ADKAR scores rise and their actual practice improve, and then reported that "no significant association could be found between higher ADKAR® scores and lower AMU." Thirty farms is not dispositive of anything. It is just that it is the only attempt on the table, and it came back null.

And the part nobody mentions: Kotter replaced his own model in 2014. "Accelerate" moved from sequential and episodic to, in Kotter Inc's own words, "Run the steps concurrently and continuously," and from driving change with "a small, powerful core group" to enlisting "a large volunteer army from up, down, and across the organization." Twelve years later, essentially every eight-steps deck in circulation still teaches the 1996 sequence.

That matters more than it sounds, because the 2014 model is the one built for a change that never finishes. Which is the one you have.

## The barrier is not desire, and it has not been for two years

ADKAR's best idea is the barrier point: the first element that is weak is the one you fix, and spending on anything after it is wasted. If people do not want the change, more training will not help. That is a sharp, useful, genuinely operational idea and it survives most of the criticism of the model.

Almost nobody applies it. Ask a change practitioner which element usually breaks and you will be told desire, near-universally. The figure behind that belief is a poll of webinar attendees about which element they personally find hardest, which is a statement about practitioners, not about changes. Notice also that desire is the one element the practitioner cannot be held responsible for delivering, on Prosci's own reasoning. It is the most professionally convenient answer available.

Now go and look at your own organization.

Microsoft's own survey of thirty-one thousand knowledge workers across thirty-one countries found that 75% were using AI at work, that 78% of those users had brought their own tools in, and that 60% of leaders said their leadership lacked a plan and vision for implementing it. That is vendor-sponsored research by a company selling AI, so take it for direction rather than to two decimal places. The direction is not subtle, and it matches what every manager I talk to describes: the workforce got there first, paid for it themselves, and did not file a ticket.

Read that against the model. A workforce that installs the tool on a personal credit card and uses it in the dark does not have an awareness problem or a desire problem. Those two elements were solved before your program existed, by the people you were about to go and persuade. And the money, the comms plan, the kickoff, the urgency slide -- all of it is aimed squarely at the two elements that were never the barrier.

There is a worse version. When you build a burning platform for people who are already running, you are not motivating them, you are telling experienced professionals that the way they currently work is dangerous. There is a 2022 paper by McLaren, van der Hoorn and Fein on exactly this move against status quo bias, and it describes manufactured urgency as combative and futile, producing anxiety and stress rather than movement. That is the standard remedy, working backwards.

So if desire is not the barrier and awareness is not the barrier, apply the model's own logic. The barrier is somewhere after them.

## Ability, and ability here means judgment

ADKAR splits knowing how from being able to do it. Knowledge and Ability. In most changes that distinction feels academic. In this one it is everything.

Knowledge is free. The interface is a text box. Anybody in your organization can be taught to use these tools to a working standard in an afternoon, and the evidence that they have already taught themselves is that they did.

Ability, in Hiatt's own definition, is "demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels." So what is the required performance level here? It is not operating the tool. It is knowing when the output is wrong.

That competence is measurably harder than anyone budgets for, and worse, it is invisible to the person who lacks it.

METR ran a randomized controlled trial with sixteen experienced open-source developers across two hundred and forty-six real tasks in repositories they had worked in for about five years on average. Sixteen people, and I say that out loud because the number is small and anybody quoting this at you without it is selling something. With AI access, those developers took 19% longer. Afterwards, having actually been slowed down, they estimated that AI had made them 20% faster.

That is the whole problem in one result. Not that they were slower. That they could not tell.

It is not one study. A preregistered field experiment with seven hundred and fifty-eight BCG consultants found large gains inside the AI's capability range and, on the task that sat outside it, subjects using AI were nineteen percentage points less likely to produce the correct answer than those without it. And the "Generative AI at Work" study in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, five thousand one hundred and seventy-two customer support agents on a staggered rollout with firm-recorded outcomes rather than self-report, found about 15% more issues resolved per hour on average -- with roughly 36% for the least skilled quintile, and no significant gain plus a small decline in conversation quality for the most skilled.

Put those three together and a manager's picture falls out of them. The tool lifts your junior people substantially. It does roughly nothing for your best people and may make their work slightly worse. And nobody in either group can reliably tell you which of those two things is happening to them.

Now notice what that does to your diagnostic. An ADKAR assessment asks a person to rate their own ability from one to five. The METR result says that self-rating is not merely noisy, it is biased in the flattering direction, and biased hardest exactly where the change is failing. You cannot self-report your way to knowing whether your judgment about AI output is any good. There is no learning module for it and there is no completion certificate that means anything, whatever the person selling you the certificate says.

There is also a thing here that no framework will name for you, so I will. The people with the most status to lose are the people the evidence says gain least. Your most experienced person, the one whose resistance you have quietly filed under "not on board," is looking at a tool that helps the newest member of the team more than it helps them, and drawing an accurate conclusion about their own position. That is not a desire deficit. That is a correct read, and treating it as an attitude problem is how you lose them.

## Reinforcement, which you are measuring wrong

The last element is the one that decides whether any of it holds, and the standard advice for it is to anchor the change in the culture. Anchor it to what, exactly? The correct practice at twelve months is frequently wrong at eighteen. The prompting discipline that produced good work on one model generation is unnecessary overhead on the next. The review step that was essential becomes theater. The task you were right to refuse to delegate becomes the one you should. Reinforcing last quarter's practice is not sustainment; it is setting it in amber.

That is the single best argument for teaching Kotter's 2014 model rather than his 1996 one. He arrived at concurrency and continuity for entirely different reasons, and it happens to be the right shape for a change whose target keeps moving.

But there is a more immediate problem, which is what you are counting. Most organizations measure AI adoption in seats and prompts, because those are the numbers the platform hands you. Both go up. Neither one tells you anything about whether the work got better.

The number that does is in a piece of research from BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab, published in Harvard Business Review, on a survey of a thousand and four full-time US desk workers. They named the thing workslop: AI-generated work that looks finished and is not, passed to a colleague who then has to do the actual thinking. Forty percent of workers said they had received some in the previous month. They estimated each instance cost them an hour and fifty-one minutes, and the authors put it at roughly $186 per employee per month.

That is what adoption without reinforcement looks like from the inside. Volume up, verification quietly displaced onto whoever is downstream of the sender, and the dashboard showing green the entire time because the dashboard counts logins.

And note who is absorbing it. In that survey, managers reported receiving workslop at a considerably higher rate than individual contributors did. The cost of everybody's unverified output lands on the person with the least time to check it, which is you.

## What to do on Tuesday afternoon

Here is the act, and it is small on purpose.

**Cancel the next adoption push and book a one-to-one instead.** Whatever is queued -- the awareness campaign, the lunch-and-learn, the champions network -- it is aimed at awareness and desire, and you have both already. Spend that hour on one person instead.

**Bring one real piece of work that already shipped.** Not a demo, not a sandbox exercise. Something that went to a customer, a board, a colleague. Something with consequences that have already happened.

**Ask how they knew it was right.** That is the entire conversation. Not whether they used AI, which is a question that teaches people to hide things. What did they check, what did they not check, and how did they decide which was which. If the answer is "it looked right," you have just found your barrier point, and you have found it on a real artifact rather than on a self-assessment form.

**Then do it again next week with somebody else.** Ten of those conversations will tell you more about where your organization actually stands than any survey instrument will, because you are measuring the output rather than the confidence.

**And change one number on your dashboard.** Retire a usage metric and put up something about quality: rework rates, rejected deliverables, how often work comes back. You will not get it right the first time. Having the wrong quality metric still beats having a perfect count of logins.

None of this requires a program, a budget line, or a slide deck. It requires an hour a week and the willingness to hear an uncomfortable answer.

## The good news is the part you think is bad news

I have spent this entire piece telling you that your change program is aimed at the wrong thing, so let me finish by pointing out what that actually means, because I think managers are reading this situation as worse than it is.

The expensive part of organizational change has always been getting people to want it. That is the part that takes eighteen months, burns political capital, and fails quietly. You got it for free. Your people did the hardest element of the model on their own time, with their own money, before anyone asked them to. That is not a governance failure to be cleaned up, whatever the shadow-IT conversation says. That is a workforce that went and found the thing.

What is left is the tractable part: helping capable adults work out when to trust the output, and building the habit of checking. That is a coaching problem, and coaching problems are what management is actually for. The platform is not burning. It never was. They already jumped, and they are waiting to see whether you are going to come over and look at what they built.
