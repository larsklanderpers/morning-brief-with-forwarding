Somewhere there is a person with my partner's name whose credit file has been tangled with hers at least twice (not mine, my name is sufficiently strange that this does not happen).

Nothing about it was a lie. A clerk matched a name and a partial date. A system inherited the match. A second system inherited it from the first, and by the time anybody noticed, the error had been repeated by enough independent-looking sources that it had the texture of a fact. Unpicking it took a long time, and at no point in the process was there anybody to be angry at.

That is the shape of the thing I want to talk about this morning, because it happens to the security record constantly, and last month I caught it happening three times in a single week. Not in somebody else's newsletter. In my own.

## The part that made me want to write this down

I run a pipeline (I actually run several because, you know, engineers need to over-engineer) that reads a lot of security and AI sources every morning and hands me a short brief. It is useful and I would not give it up. It is also, structurally, a machine for doing exactly what happened to my partner's credit file -- taking things that are individually almost right and welding them into something confidently wrong.

None of the three failures below involved anybody making anything up. There is no villain. That is the entire point, and it is why the fix is a skill rather than an accusation.

I mentioned a small version of this on Monday in passing. This is the version that actually earns its own space.

## One: two things that share a word

An identity-protection company called Aura disclosed a breach. Around the same time, a group called ShinyHunters was running a campaign against a Salesforce product -- specifically against an API endpoint in Salesforce Experience Cloud with the string `aura` in its path.

Two entirely unrelated things. One word in common.

What happened next is that Wikipedia's article on the Aura breach acquired citations attributing the intrusion to ShinyHunters -- citations which, read carefully, are all about the Salesforce campaign. And the security press, quite reasonably, inherited the attribution from there.

**Aura named no actor.** Aura never stated an intrusion date. Neither of those facts is hidden; they are in the company's own statement, which is short and careful and boring and which almost nobody read, because a confident attribution was available one click earlier.

I want to be fair about how this happens, because the temptation is to treat it as sloppiness and it mostly is not. If you are writing up a breach and a well-cited encyclopedia entry names the actor, you have done a reasonable amount of diligence by checking it. The failure is not laziness. It is that the check *passed*.

## Two: a survey wearing somebody else's masthead

There is a report called *State of AI Cybersecurity 2026*. It is cited constantly, and it is cited as the Cloud Security Alliance's -- an industry body, vendor-neutral, the kind of source you quote precisely because it is not selling you anything.

It is **Darktrace's** report. The CSA republished it, and the CSA's own page says so, in a line directly above the text: *"Originally published by Darktrace."*

Read as the CSA's, it is neutral industry research about what security leaders are worried about. Read correctly, it is a survey conducted by a company that sells both the detection tooling and the agentic security-operations product whose adoption the survey measures. Which does not make it false. It makes it a different kind of document, and the difference matters when you are quoting it in a board paper -- or anywhere else for that matter.

And there is a second layer under that one, which is about numbers rather than mastheads. The report is usually cited as finding that fifty percent of security leaders identify hyper-personalized phishing as a top threat. What it actually says is that fifty percent *"mentioned hyper-personalized phishing campaigns as one of their biggest **worries**."*

Worries. Not observations. Not threat measurement. Nobody measured any phishing. They measured what a group of people said keeps them up at night, which is a real and interesting thing to measure, but which is clearly not the same as incidence. The same report's frequently-quoted sixty-seven percent is not "have deployed agentic AI for security operations" either -- it is respondents who *"report that theirs uses agentic AI,"* which for a lot of them means a vendor shipped a feature.

Here is the part I did not expect to have to write, or to be surprised by. I worked all of this out on the fourteenth of August. **On the twentieth, my own brief served it back to me** -- the same report, attributed to the CSA again, the worries presented as findings again. And when I went and looked properly, the report was published in April. It had arrived in my inbox as that morning's signal, four months late, with the attribution error intact, six days after I had personally established that it was wrong.

I am not telling you that to be self-flagellating about it. I am telling you because it is the strongest evidence I have that this is structural rather than careless. A thing I had specifically caught, in writing, came back within a week -- because the wrong version is what is circulating, and anything reading the open web will keep finding the wrong version until the wrong version stops being the loudest one. There is a whole broader implication about AI-driven content here, but that is a topic for another time.

## Three: the right number in front of the wrong noun

Back to Aura, because the same breach produced a second failure of a completely different kind.

The number in circulation is nine hundred thousand customer records. Aura's own statement says *"approximately 900,000 records"* -- and then keeps going, which is the part that got dropped. Those records came *"almost exclusively from a contact list from a company that Aura acquired five years ago."* The identifiable customer exposure is *"less than 20,000 active"* plus *"less than 15,000 former."*

Call it thirty-five thousand.

So the number is right and the noun after it is wrong, by a factor of twenty-five. Nobody misquoted the figure. Somebody attached "customer" to a count of "records," which is the sort of thing that happens in a headline where there is no room for the qualifier, and after that it was in the record.

But I think the genuinely interesting story is the one that got buried underneath the wrong version. A marketing contact list, acquired with a company five years ago, was sitting in the blast radius of a breach in 2026 -- and it was roughly twenty-five times larger than the active customer base it was sitting next to. That is a much better story than the one everybody ran. It is also a considerably more uncomfortable one, because most organizations of any age are carrying something similar and could not tell you what is in it.

The compromise itself, for what it is worth, was one employee, one phone call, one hour.

That acquired contact list is not an exotic failure. In regulated industries like gaming you must keep records for years after the fact, and advanced AML regimes like AUSTRAC's can make you keep data even longer -- data about people who in many cases are not even customers any longer, but which is nevertheless exceedingly private. Amalgamation can bring over lots of these records, and they usually just come across with the systems. Especially if the customer is inactive, little is actually done at that point, or even during regular reviews. The potential exposure far exceeds the potential value, and regulation will not let you simply delete the problem. But it is worth being precise about what the obligation actually is. You are required to **retain** those records. You are not required to keep them **reachable**. Nothing in any of those regimes says the data has to sit somewhere a live query can walk into it, and that distinction is the whole difference between a retention obligation and a standing exposure. In practice almost nobody separates the two, which is the genuinely odd part.

Now that probably feels like a thought exercise. But consider it in the context of, say, a BI program mining historic data for potential reactivation customers. That data can easily become part of an active set, and if the records are not marked as confidential *but the AML decision about them is*, you can end up in a complete self-own. This happened to me a few years back. We assumed the suppressed records had been duplicated into another, active system with their flags carried across; they had not, so we never pre-processed the data with those exceptions in mind, and the mining pulled them straight into the active set. Nobody realized until the business was already exposed.

## Four questions, four minutes

Last Sunday I gave you four questions to point at your own controls. These are four questions to point at everybody else's sentences, and the rhyme is on purpose -- it turns out the same posture works in both directions.

These take about four minutes, which is the only reason I use them and keep coming back to them. A check that takes an hour is a check you will do twice and then quietly abandon -- or at least I will, and I suspect most people are the same.

**Who published it, rather than who republished it?** This catches the masthead problem on its own. Follow the link. Then look for the line at the top of the page that says where it came from before it came here. That single habit would have caught case two, and it takes about forty seconds.

**What do they sell?** Not as an accusation -- as a fact about what the sentence is for. A firewall company characterizing the severity of a vulnerability is not lying to you. It is doing its job, and its job is not the same as your job. The useful move is not to discount the claim; it is to notice when the only people characterizing the risk are the people selling the response to it, and to go and find out what the quieter party said.

**Is the actor or the author named by the primary, or only by a summary?** This is case one, and it is the question that would have caught it. If the company's own statement does not name an attacker, then the attacker is not named, no matter how many well-cited pages say otherwise. An attribution that appears everywhere except in the primary source has a shape, and the shape is that somebody guessed early and everybody inherited it. I have found this one to be particularly true of late, enough that I have changed my research agents to always go back to the primary source. The problem is systemic enough that I have taken to tracking wrong instances alongside right ones, which is what turned into the basis for this piece.

**Does the denominator match the noun?** Nine hundred thousand *records* is not nine hundred thousand *customers.* Sixty-seven percent whose *tooling includes* a thing is not sixty-seven percent who *deployed* it. Ninety-five percent who *delayed or canceled something* over twelve months is a floor of one incident, not a portrait of an industry in crisis. Read the number, then read the noun immediately after it, and ask whether they are actually describing the same population. This, by the way, if it was not intuitively obvious, is where most people go wrong. It is a discipline, a muscle that requires constant practice, and it can be exhausting even with that.

Four questions. Say four minutes, generously. They will not make you right, and they are not a substitute for expertise. They will stop you repeating the specific class of error that all three cases above belong to, which is a large fraction of the errors that actually make it into a board paper or other professional correspondence.

## Why it is always the same direction

Here is the pattern underneath all three, and it is the thing I actually want you to keep close because this is a truism of our modern era and the real implication of the trap.

**The primary source is quieter than the secondary, and quiet reads as absence.**

Aura published one careful statement with no adjectives in it. The aggregators published dozens of confident ones. Microsoft rates an exploit's likelihood in a single flat phrase with no drama attached; a company in the vulnerability business publishes the word "wormable," and that word travels. A regulator publishes a narrow technical instrument; everyone with a product adjacent to it publishes an explainer about what it means for you.

Nobody in that chain is lying. The asymmetry is structural. The parties with the authority to characterize something publish narrowly, late, carefully, and without adjectives -- because being wrong is expensive for them in a way it is not for anybody downstream. The parties with a commercial interest in the adjective publish early, loudly, and often, because being interesting is the entire point.

So the record assembles itself out of the loud half. And the quiet half does not read as caution. It reads as *nothing there.*

Once you have seen it, you cannot unsee it, and it is oddly freeing. It means you do not have to be suspicious of everybody, which is exhausting and makes you worse at your job. You just have to remember that silence from the party who would actually know is information, and usually the most important information available.

## What this is actually good for

I want to be clear about what I am claiming, because it would be easy to read all of this as a complaint about the security press, and it is not. Those three items reached me through a pipeline I built, which read sources I chose, and I am the one who nearly repeated all three. If there is an indictment here it is of a process, and the process is mine.

The reason to develop this particular skill is not accuracy for its own sake. It is that in about eighteen months you are going to be asked, by somebody who does not have time to check, whether a thing you were told is true -- and increasingly the confident version will have arrived through a machine that is very good at sounding like it read the primary. The four questions are not a defense against that. They are just a habit that means you notice the seam.

My partner's credit file did get untangled, eventually. Not because anybody got angry -- there was never anybody to be angry at -- but because one person in the chain finally went back to the primary record instead of inheriting the match. That is the whole skill. That is all four questions are.

So the next time a number lands in front of you and it is a good number, one that would make a slide, spend the four minutes. Most of the time everything will hold up and you will have lost four minutes. Occasionally it will not hold up, and you will be the only person in the room who knows.
