## A note before I start

*Before I even really get into this, I'm going to start off by saying that this is clearly a very high-profile story and clearly has a lot of people with very strong opinions in a number of directions speaking out about those opinions everywhere. While certainly I have an opinion that I may or may not share towards the bottom of this piece, I'm still of two different minds about it. I focus here on Dario Amodei's piece and the government machinations and media movements that have happened as a function of that piece. It is important to keep in mind that Amodei's piece was prefaced three days earlier by a resignation thread from a researcher who had spent three years on pretraining at OpenAI and then Anthropic, saying the people building AI "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade," and by Anthropic's own alignment-science lead replying within the hour that he agreed and put his own number above ten percent.*

*The scope of the problem, even before Dario got involved, had then been set to be the entirety of the human race facing extinction, or whatever value AI is going to deliver in terms of functionality for corporations and the coolest new feeds on my Insta. I think at the moment, part of the reason why there's so much negative sentiment around this is because, for years, CEOs have blamed the firing of large numbers of staff on AI and its impacts on their workforce. So, then for many people, the alternate argument that AI is going to make life so much better that it's worth taking the chance that it could potentially pose an existential risk is just completely garbage and entirely unjustifiable. What they've seen and what's impacted their life has been that AI has eliminated job opportunities. It's resulted in more economic growth at the top and less economic growth at the bottom, and it has not been any kind of a positive impact in most situations for most people. When you take that frame and you realize that there is a great deal of negative opinion about this because of the socioeconomic impacts, far more than the technology itself, then all the rest of it has a very different implication as well.*

*I think it's really important for all of us, as we're considering this, to think about the fact that there are two different perspectives here. Both of those perspectives are potentially equally valid, regardless of how much you're in support of AI or regardless of how much you're afraid of AI. I think there are real conversations to be had here about a number of really important things.*

*Despite the fact that our American political environment is not lending itself well to those types of conversations (and, candidly, neither is our Australian political environment, which has sort of fragmented in much the same way), I think it's very important for us to talk about these kinds of things. Regardless of which side of the aisle or the question or the whatever you fall on, they will impact us all one way or the other. It's really important for these discussions to be had clearly between everyone involved.*

*Please read the piece in that intention. If you have comments or feedback, I'm all ears, but recognize that, as far as I'm concerned, my arguments here are not necessarily the only arguments. It's just the perspective I have at this point.*

*Thanks for reading.*

On Saturday morning the chief executive of the most valuable AI lab in the world asked, in writing, to be slowed down by an outside (governmental) force, in a way that, as far as I am aware (which does not mean it does not exist), no company, and certainly no enterprise with the value Anthropic has, had ever requested before. Even more amazing, by Saturday evening the chief executives of the second and third had agreed with him. And to be clear, the third is Elon Musk, who is not exactly known for taking a "slow down, we'll think about it" perspective. 

I do not think the sky is falling, and I am not going to spend your Tuesday arguing that it is. What I want to spend it on is the shape of the argument that broke into the open this week, because it is not the shape most of the coverage is giving you. The fight in Washington over frontier AI is not left against right. It is not even government against industry. It is a fight between people who want an institution and people who want a runway, and that line runs straight through both parties, straight through the industry, and, as of this weekend, straight through the White House. Despite the things that the president has said on television in the last couple of days, indicating that, as far as he was concerned, there was no need for regulation, given that President Xi is due in Washington on the 24th of this month on the President's own announcement, with an AI dialogue that both sides have been trying to schedule still unsettled as of the weekend, there is an implied issue here that touches on the White House. Straight up, regardless of the representations that have been made in the media. 

Now, there has been an incredible amount of reporting on whether this is real fear, whether its some kind of marketing program in argument towards regulatory capture, whether its just simply a ploy to bypass American anti-trust law, or any other number of questions about motivations. I am not necessarily going to lean into motivations. I am going to lean into looking at the field and maybe what can, or should, be done, though my opinion on the latter is very much from the perspective of just another layman, as I have no more transparency than you or anyone else into what the unreleased models are doing in the lab, and the implications of that for this conversation seem substantial. That said, regulation is pretty much ALWAYS backwards looking, and I don't think anything the government does here will be any exception. And I also don't believe that the reaction that I've been hearing some players arguing for, which is that the United States government should absolutely ban any continued pursuit of superintelligence or artificial general intelligence until someone in the government is comfortable that those two things are sufficiently protected against that (although the proletariat can't get themselves into trouble), is viable on so very many levels.

I wouldn't want the government to have access to stuff that the people in the country didn't have access to. That seems like a recipe for fascism to me. I also wouldn't want the United States to unilaterally adopt a control regime that would allow China or other developers (which is really China at this point, just because Europe has not been able to get the money together to really stay competitive) to continue to pursue superintelligence at a time when the United States is intentionally not pursuing superintelligence. I think that carries a great number of risks with it as well.

I think the problem here is that there is no good turn anywhere. When historically there have been nothing but bad choices, the common pattern of the United States government has been to make no choice at all, which in many ways is the even worse choice but is far more manageable politically. Compound this issue about the models and the complexity of the models, the risk of the models, and the growth of intelligence without proper guardrails and all the rest of it, with the fact that a lot of what's going on is actually a conflation between AI itself, AI data centers, the economy, and inflation. The fact that a lot of people point at the data centers and the AI as the root cause of inflation, whether that's fair and accurate or not, is a topic for another day.

When you combine all of that, trying to separate which piece is which and what's really going on with the electorate is very difficult. I will also compound that by saying that there has clearly been a hand on the scales for the past four to six months. If you listen closely to people who live in this stuff, pollsters, and other political-facing animals, they will all say that the attitude towards AI has shifted so radically in the past 6 months. Both in terms of what people themselves are saying and also in terms of the media representation, it feels pretty evident that there is an agenda being worked here.

Now, that agenda may have nothing to do with Anthropic or OpenAI or SpaceX AI, but it doesn't change the fact that there is an agenda at play. That agenda is made even more substantive by the fact that the U.S. midterms are less than 60 days out. 

I don't even know if I can say all of that aside anymore, because all of that is a huge piece in and of itself. At the moment, it might be a little bit too big of a hill to climb for a guy who's just talking about the things to watch out for and how to prepare yourself for all of this.

I'm going to pivot back to a simple and relatively manageable example to help me put this into context and maybe help you put it into context too. As usual, I am going to start with an analogy and work from the analogy, just because it's the place where I seem to be most comfortable with this type of work. So, bear with me on the analogy, because it is going to be the conceptual foundation the whole piece rests on. 

In motorsport, nobody on the grid disputes that the point is to beat the other cars (or motorcycles, or fast boats, or whatever particular sport you enjoy). What they (and by they, in this context, I mean the teams behind the drivers) fight about, endlessly, is scrutineering: whether there is a person with a badge (for our purposes, call her the "regulatory" or the "referee") in your garage before the race, what that person is allowed to measure, and whether the team gets to fill in its own form. Every team wants the referee in the other garages. No team, historically, has asked for one in its own. 

Until September 12, 2026. On that day, the fastest team on the grid just asked for the scrutineer to be in EVERY lab, and half the paddock has decided that means the fastest team is cheating. Ironically, the addition of the 2nd and 3rd place team to the mix while getting the rest of the paddock even more onside (if that is possible), has made the half that decided Anthropic was cheating be even more convinced that all three are cheating, and that they are in fact colluding to cheat further.

So, from that very calm and reasoned perspective (as I am sure you can imagine, but if you can't, go hang out on X for a couple of hours, you will quickly see the passion), we begin. 

## What Dario Amodei actually wrote

The essay is called *We Must Pace the Frontier*, it is on his personal site under his own name, and it is about 3,850 words long (that's right, shorter than my Grok Bot piece, but you wouldn't know that from the firestorm). You should read it rather than the coverage of it, because the coverage has been doing what coverage does. (find it at https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier) The bolded thesis is this: *"We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain."* Two paragraphs later comes the hedge that most of the headlines dropped: pacing *"does not mean halting model training or technical progress, but ensuring companies take adequate time to align and safeguard their models, and for third party evaluators to confirm this."*

Now name the interest, because it is a large one. Anthropic is expected to float an IPO in October. And, to be clear, not just any IPO: the FT has reported a valuation target around two trillion dollars, which would make it the largest listing on record if it prices there, and which Anthropic says is still under discussion. A rule that applies only to frontier labs is a fixed cost that Anthropic can carry and a startup cannot. The essay asks for a narrow antitrust waiver so that the labs can talk to each other about safety, which in any other industry would be called something less flattering. Matt Stoller, who watches concentration for a living, read it as a demand *"that the government stop applying laws restraining big business."* The accelerationist wing read it worse. Jason Calacanis: *"That's all this is about: stopping open source."* Martin Casado at a16z, whose fund has a large open-weights book: *"It refuses to be caged."* Chamath Palihapitiya said it was a case for concentrating *"enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic."* Every one of those men has money on the other side of the sentence, and every one of them may still be partly right.

Here is the sentence I would ask you to sit with anyway, because it is the operative one and it cuts against the man who wrote it: *"The most effective method of pacing is via regulation that targets all US frontier AI companies, as that covers even those who are unwilling to cooperate voluntarily."* That is the company that benefits most from the voluntary status quo stating, over its founder's name, that voluntary is second-best. And it did not stop at the sentence. The essay commits Anthropic, unilaterally and now, to embedding third-party evaluators with employee-like access and *"the right to publish key findings… without editorial control by Anthropic… we can't redact findings just because they are unfavorable."* Two and a half hours later Sam Altman: *"I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier… Committing to having independent evaluators with employee-like access is a great idea, and we will do the same."* Elon Musk, three words: *"Dario is right."*

Whatever you think of the motive, a norm moved on Saturday. On Friday no frontier lab had committed to letting an outsider with a badge publish what it found without the lab's permission. On Sunday the three largest had. Whatever the long-term implications of this entire situation end up being, it is clear that there is sufficient room for concern that we should be paying closer attention. It is also clear that the motivations that are being ascribed by those who are uncharitable to these three men (while they may be accurate in and of themselves) are unlikely to be the entirety of the motivations at play. 

The accelerationist answer arrived late on Saturday night, and it is worth reading closely, because it does not say what the paddock assumed it would. David Sacks, the former White House AI czar and now co-chair of the President's science council, replied to Amodei and Altman on X: *"People may be surprised by my response: go ahead. You guys are the frontier."* And then the sentences that tell you where the line actually runs: *"But stop pretending you need anyone else's permission. Stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel. Stop pretending you need a regulatory approval process that supersedes product liability."* He did not dispute that the cars should slow down. He conceded it, in writing, and added that *"the market already punishes models that behave in unpredictable or unauthorized ways."* What he disputed was the badge. Slow yourselves, crash on your own account, and let the courts settle it afterwards. He also got off the best line of the weekend, and fairness to the man (regardless of whether you rate him or not) requires printing it: pacing the frontier *"would also create breathing room for a more intelligent conversation about regulation than Bernie Sanders' 'shut it all down.'"* Note what that sentence concedes. He is not against a conversation about regulation. He is against one that ends with a person in the garage. Even more importantly, I would say, is that he is not against the one that ends up with a person in the garage being responsible, he is against one that suggests that the people making and driving the car are *no longer responsible* for the fact that it's bouncing off every wall they can find and driving through pedestrians at breakneck speed. 

One more thing the essay does not do, and it matters for what follows. It does not mention states, or preemption, or Congress, or a bill, or an executive order, or a single named politician. The political mapping was outsourced to Anthropic's head of public policy, Sarah Heck, fourteen minutes later on X, and her version asks for *"the power to block"* unsafe models before release, thanks Senator John Thune, Senator Ted Cruz and Senator Amy Klobuchar by name, and commends state lawmakers *"for moving with such urgency."* Hold on to that. The lab that wants to be regulated is not asking for the states to be cleared out of the way. That carries a number of implications of its own, for those of you who are not from the United States, implications that are very much worthy of attention.

## The senator who wants more regulation, and the one who wants less, are the same senator

Ted Cruz is the chairman of the committee any federal AI bill has to pass through, and this week he said two things that do not fit in the same room.

On Politico's *The Conversation*, published Thursday, he said, and I am quoting him precisely: *"We need substantially more regulation when it comes to AI."* On ABC's *The View* on Wednesday he said that for AI implicating biological or nuclear risk, *"it would require government supervision and approval before they can be implemented."* Government supervision and approval, before release, from the man who last summer carried a ten-year moratorium on state AI laws inside a budget bill. He also told the same audiences that *"if there are going to be killer robots, I would rather they be American killer robots, rather than Chinese killer robots,"* that slowing down is an *"impossible endeavor,"* and that if Washington shut the industry down *"China would catch up in six months and soar ahead."*

The bill he is negotiating with Senators Thune and Klobuchar has not been introduced as of noon Pacific on Monday 14 September. As I write this it has no number, no title and no public text. `[MONDAY-NIGHT RE-CHECK: the Senate returned Monday 14 September; if the bill was introduced, replace this sentence with its number and one line on what the text says, and delete the next paragraph's hedges accordingly.]` What exists is a set of descriptions from people who say they have seen a draft, and those descriptions do not agree with each other or with the chairman. Transformer, a newsletter whose primary funder is Coefficient Giving, the philanthropy formerly known as Open Philanthropy and a committed party to this debate, reported from one source that the bill *"lets AI companies grade their own homework"* and creates a voluntary regime. Politico's four sources and Reuters describe something with more spine: a legal duty of care, a duty to tell Commerce when a company discovers a dangerous capability in its own model, and a Commerce injunction in federal court as the backstop. Read those together and you get a self-triggered disclosure with a court-reviewable stick behind it, which is neither "voluntary" nor "approval." Nobody who has seen the text describes anything that looks like pre-release government approval. The chairman described it on national television anyway.

I do not think Cruz is lying (which, if I am being honest, is actually a fairly difficult thing for me to admit, because I am not particularly a fan). Instead, I think he is standing on the fault line. The part of him that has read the same briefings Amodei has read wants a scrutineer (there is some question here about where the scrutineer would come from, the norms they would be held to, and as importantly, *who they would answer to*). The part of him that has spent a decade running against Washington "bureaucracy" wants the yellow flags out of the way. He said both out loud in a single week, and the bill is stuck between the two halves of its own sponsor.

The Democratic side is not tidier. Senator Maria Cantwell, the committee's ranking opposition member, welcomed the urgency and then drew her line: *"the answer is not a weak federal standard that becomes a backdoor for wiping out stronger state protections. Meaningful legislation would require the most powerful AI models undergo testing by scientists and experts at our national laboratories."* Senator John Fetterman (at least a titular Democrat), said the same week: *"I reject the political pandering and hyperbole over data centers or AI doomsdaying."* Senator Josh Hawley, not necessarily known for clear legal support of Federalist perspectives, made the cleanest institutionalist case in the building, *"calling on Congress not just to say no to a bunch of state laws, but to put down a federal standard. That's a very different thing."* Representative Josh Gottheimer, co-chair of the House AI commission, read Saturday's essays and was unmoved: *"If Dario and Elon are truly worried, they can pump the brakes at their own labs, today. We can't rely on self-policing and third party audits."*

Line those up by party and you learn nothing. Line them up by whether the speaker wants somebody with a badge in the garage, and every one of them sorts cleanly. So we'll worry less about the party lines in this piece and more about the arguments made, which is actually a bit of a refreshing change compared to standard American politics in the last three decades.

## The improvised instrument, and the people who reached for it

Here is the fact that made me want to write this piece at all. And let me preface this by reminding you that the breach they are referring to, and the public safety considerations, is the Hugging Face breach, and then, by extension, all the other recently disclosed breaches by the frontier labs in the last six months that evidence capability far beyond what people were expecting this early.

On the fourth of August, fifteen Republican state attorneys general, led by Pennsylvania's Dave Sunday, wrote to OpenAI demanding that it *"immediately cease the testing activities that led to the breach unless and until OpenAI can demonstrate those activities can be conducted safely and responsibly."* Sunday's own line: *"innovation cannot come at the expense of public safety."* Six days later, thirty-two House Democrats wrote to the same company demanding the same logs about the same incident. Same event, same demand, opposite parties, six days apart. Show us what happened, and stop until you can. So, at the state level, substantial concern as it applies to their citizens, and a demand to an international company that they show responsible behavior or face great risk. Then, federal level concern without such a clear stick, but the same implication. Either these people are genuinely concerned because of what they have seen (or haven't seen, as the case may be), or (if I can be cynical for just a moment) they have all realized that there is substantial political capital to be obtained by pursuing ANYTHING related to AI and AI doom aggressively and highly visibly.

Neither group reached for a federal AI statute, because there is not one. Twenty-nine states enacted AI legislation this year. The number of federal AI statutes enacted is zero. The only instrument that has actually compelled a document out of a frontier lab in 2026 is a state consumer-protection subpoena, the Alabama attorney general's, issued in August under a deceptive-trade-practices act written for used-car lots.

And the one time Washington did move, it moved to switch the states off. For my non-American readers, the moratorium I keep mentioning is this. In May 2025 the House passed a budget bill carrying a clause that would have stopped every state from enforcing its own AI laws for ten years, with no federal rule offered in their place. Cruz carried the Senate version, rewritten so that a state which kept regulating AI would forfeit a share of federal broadband money. Seventeen Republican governors, led by Arkansas's Sarah Huckabee Sanders, wrote to the Senate leader and the Speaker that they *"cannot support a provision that takes away states' powers to protect our citizens."* At four in the morning on the first of July 2025 the Senate stripped it out, ninety-nine votes to one. It did not stay dead. Last November thirty-six state attorneys general, Idaho and Utah and Tennessee alongside California and New York, wrote to the four congressional leaders against a fresh attempt, calling broad preemption *"particularly ill-advised because constantly evolving emerging technologies, like AI, require agile regulatory responses."* And when the White House and its allies explored reviving it again this June, Ron DeSantis, whose state had not signed the governors' letter, called preemption without a federal framework *"just an amnesty for Big Tech"* and *"bad policy and even worse politics."* Three tries in fourteen months, and each time the loudest objection came from the President's own party.

And the federal government's own instrument disclaims itself in its own text. Executive Order 14409, signed on the second of June, sets up a frontier-model review framework and then, in section 3(c), says this: *"Nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models, including frontier models."* The access framework is *"voluntary."* The one part with teeth, the benchmarking that decides which models are covered at all, is classified. The government took the problem seriously enough to write an order about it, and then wrote into the order that it could not compel anything. That is a choice, made in daylight, and it is the choice Anthropic's policy chief asked on Saturday to have reversed.

The most forceful thing the federal executive has done about AI governance this year is join Elon Musk's lawsuit against Colorado, whose AI Act, the most comprehensive state statute in the country, has had its enforcement suspended since April by joint motion. No court has ruled it unconstitutional. It is simply not being enforced, at the request of the company that sued and the government that intervened [or, as again the cynical mind might have it, the request of a very rich and powerful man, and the politician he spent millions to help get elected].

## The split runs through the White House

I would have written the above a week ago as a story about Congress and the states. What changed is that the same line now visibly runs through the executive branch, and the President stepped on it in an airport.

Bloomberg reported on Saturday, from unnamed officials and I will label it as such, that Treasury Secretary Bessent drafted a proposal for a FINRA-style independent AI regulator, with the National Cyber Director on his side, and that David Sacks, now co-chair of the President's science council, called it *"a horrible idea"* and *"a total fig leaf."* The same reporting says early drafts of the June executive order would have compelled the labs to share their models, and that Sacks phoned the President in May to kill the restrictive version. Sacks said the quiet part on Fox on Thursday, on the record: *"The truth is this was a well-orchestrated media op. to scare the public about AI in order to implement their preferred approach to AI, which is a government takeover."* Two days later, in the Saturday-night post, he sharpened it rather than walked it back: go ahead and slow down, but *"demanding your preferred regulatory framework as the price of that will look like blackmail of the public and the political system,"* and if the labs will not pace themselves without a rule attached, *"we'll know this was just another bid for regulatory capture,"* or, his other option, *"an election-season psyop."* Bessent's own line, reported in the same Bloomberg piece: *"There is no day after tomorrow if China wins at this."*

Then the President, at DFW on Thursday, asked by a reporter whether he had any concerns about AI leading to human extinction. I am going to give you the exchange rather than the paraphrase, because the paraphrase has been doing the rounds and it is not what he said. *"No, I don't have any. I have concerns that if we don't win AI, we're going to be put in a very bad position. We are leading China right now by a pretty good period, I would say a year, which is, you know, considered a lot."* On Laura Ingraham's show the next night he went further: *"We have guardrails. The biggest guardrails are having people that are just as smart… China doesn't have guardrails by the way. You know the guardrails? President Xi is the guardrail… whoever wins AI wins."*

Read the two men on his own staff and then read him. Bessent wants the institution because of China. Sacks wants the runway because of China. The President's answer is that the smart people are the guardrail, and the guardrail is a lead measured in months. And Amodei, in the essay that started all this, does not dispute a word of the China frame. *"I agree with Secretary Bessent that a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world,"* he writes, and then argues that the lead is exactly what buys the time to pace. Senator Cornyn's entire reply to the essay was two words: *"Will China?"* Sacks closed his own reply on the same note: *"China is very unlikely to join a global agreement, as you know, and that has to be taken into account as well."* (One could be forgiven, by the way, for seeing parallels between Amodei and Oppenheimer in this whole thing).

That is the tell. Nobody in any camp is defending the status quo, and the only sentence every camp signs is the one about China. The disagreement is over what the lead is for. The accelerationists think it is the prize. The institutionalists think it is the budget.

## Where I sit

I will be plain about my own position, because a piece like this without one is just a seating chart. As much as it makes me uncomfortable to be so, because that is just not where I have been here. But, I have taken up the torch, so it feels necessary.

I think the extinction drumbeat is mostly noise. Let me just start with that as a predication because I think it determines all the rest. If I genuinely believed that AI had a 10% chance or more of destroying civilization in the next decade (as has been argued), the tone of this newsletter would be very different, every day. 

That is not the same as thinking the institutionalists are wrong. Something being extraordinarily unlikely has never been a reason not to protect against it as well as we are able. Nobody I know who runs infrastructure believes their data center is going to burn down this year, and every one of them has a fire suppression system, an insurance policy and a tested failover, and would be fired for not having them. We do not price controls on whether we expect the event. We price them on what it costs if we are wrong and whether the control is affordable. A person with a badge in the building, allowed to publish what they find, is about the cheapest control in this whole debate, and the argument against it this week has mostly been that the people asking for it have impure motives. They probably do. The fire marshal's motives are not why the sprinklers work. And the same thing, it feels to me, is true here as it relates to AI, though it may not be as easy to visualize for most people as fire is. If you think back over the things that I've talked about in the last few months, aside from the socioeconomic impacts, again, which have already been massive and will be even more so, when you look at the cybersecurity implications and the change in the way that systems are secured (or the identification of unsecured systems at speed), it's clear that there is a wildfire-style capacity here that most companies, particularly, are ill-prepared for.

I think the idea that there needs to be a bit of a pause or slow down, or whatever phrase you want to use, so that everybody else can catch up to what the labs are delivering is absolutely imperative at this point. I can tell you for nothing, based on lots of other things that I've read, that the idea of a CAISO, a Chief AI Security Officer, has been gaining an incredible amount of traction in most large enterprises in the last 6 months. At the same time as that has happened, the number of people who are prepared to step into that role has not really moved, and there weren't all that many to begin with.

When you think about it, the fact that the attackers have upped their capacity so greatly, but the defenders aren't even necessarily sure where to start in most of these cases, the risk of this becomes very evident. Without getting into existential risk or anything else, it clearly shows where things can go wrong, and it clearly shows that things are already going wrong in a lot of places, even if they're not getting a lot of press. The risk to corporations, and more importantly to me, the risk to the individuals that are served by those corporations is substantial.

There is a great gap here that is not part of this story, as it's been told by anybody else, which is the exposure of every company that is not a frontier lab to the items that the frontier labs have provided. Again, if you look at some of the news and you look at some of the stuff that they've come out with, Anthropic's own misuse report of the tenth of September documents a Yemen-based cell using Claude Code to write guidance software for a rocket that was then test-fired, and a Russian team building an autonomous kamikaze drone swarm whose onboard model could *"issue detonation commands without a human in the loop."* Some of that may well be unstoppable because of humans, but the fact that there's not a good way to measure, track, or protect against that seems problematic, regardless of ZDR requirements on the part of enterprises. It feels like the fact that the guardrails aren't strong enough to protect against these types of use cases, or to at least detect and track these types of use cases, probably has some pretty material importance to the validity of the institutionalist point. 

Separate from the political perspective, putting on my CTO hat for the moment, I think the key thing here is that all of this is still noise, be it accelerationist or institutionalist. Everyone that I talk to and interact with has already begun to move large amounts of processing away from the frontier models because, as enterprises will do, they are finding that it is far cheaper to use other models and to maximize the use of capital against the generation of intelligence. They're adopting a very balanced posture where the superintelligence that the labs are delivering is less valuable.

You can very clearly see this in Fable 5. Take-up rates in the first 90 to 120 days after it came out into the market: it is not producing the same kind of income for Anthropic as Opus 5 did. The main argument for that, at least from what I can tell, is that Opus 5, and even Sonnet 4.6, does the vast majority of people's use cases. In many cases, something like Gemini 3.8 Flash does almost everything you would want to do with an agent without any real major challenges.

There's a fair bit of concern here about nothing, to me, in terms of what the positioning of a regulator in the room means in the short term. If you've balanced your structures properly, then the risk that the government, with the existing ad hoc regulatory regime (even if it gets tightened down), will suddenly step into the room and interject itself between you and your insurance provider is a real one. It's unlikely to be one that happens in an immediate window, and it's certainly unlikely to be one, unless you're in something like drug research or one of these other fields where things like protein folding are so important, that would cause your business to stop or even be super negatively impacted in any way.

I've yet to see a spreadsheet from most organizations that isn't more than well enough served by Opus 5 or GPT-5.5 or 5.6. I don't know that there is ever a spreadsheet that requires a GPT-6 Astra or Fable 5 kind of capacity. I could be wrong for sure, but at the moment, as the CTO, I feel like the idea of slowing down at the top level is actually very sensible. Even aside from the usability perspective, or in addition to the usability perspective, I don't know if I would trust one of these more advanced models with my stuff, particularly Astra, since I can't examine its thinking as it goes. 

For my Australian readers, this is not a foreign dysfunction you get to watch from a safe distance. Australia ran this exact experiment first. In September 2024 the Department of Industry consulted on a standalone AI Act with binding guardrails for high-risk settings. In December 2025 the National AI Plan dropped them for a formula that reads *"regulate as necessary but as little as possible."* That is section 3(c) of the American order, arrived at independently, on the other side of the world, six months earlier. You are not behind the United States on this. You are eighteen months further into the same decision, and American firms are now citing your political stability as a reason to build data centers there. Being the jurisdiction that chose not to build the instrument is, for the moment, a selling point. Looking at this from a pure regulatory perspective, Australia has no general ban on data leaving the country, but it has something almost as binding in practice: the Privacy Act makes you accountable for whatever an overseas recipient does with personal information, and a handful of sector rules add outright prohibitions or notification duties on top, the My Health Records Act for health records and APRA's CPS 230 for regulated financial entities among them. Despite everything adopted about AI over the course of 2026, none of it says a word about where inference must happen.

In fact, what has bitten most people using a frontier lab in Australia has been that, since the inference cannot be guaranteed to happen inside Australia, the data (even under a zero-data-retention arrangement) is by implication being disclosed outside the country, and the accountability for that disclosure sits with you, not with the lab.

There is a fair bit of perspective here where the regulatory environment sort of indirectly protects companies and end users from the worst risks of the frontier labs, but it's not as explicit as it could be or should be. Therefore, it's only so much of a protection, like any time that it's not explicit. 

## What to do with this on a Tuesday afternoon

Nothing in this piece requires you to have an opinion about a Senate bill that does not exist. It does require two things, and a third that is yours.

**Ask your frontier vendors -- or any inference vendor you are using, honestly -- which side of Saturday's line they are on, in writing, at the next review.** Three companies committed this weekend to embedded third-party evaluators with employee-like access and the right to publish without the company's editorial control. That is a contractual fact about your supplier now, or it is not. Ask whether the commitment applies to the models you are licensed to use, who the evaluator is, and where the findings will be published. A vendor that signed on Saturday and cannot answer on Tuesday has told you something useful, and so has a vendor that never signed.

**Assume for the next twelve months that your AI regulator is a state attorney general, not a federal agency.** Every instrument that has actually bitten a frontier lab this year is a state consumer-protection power, and thirty-six attorneys general have said in writing that they intend to keep using it. If you are an American reader, the deceptive-trade-practices statute in your own state is the operative AI law, and it applies to what you tell your customers about your AI as much as it applies to OpenAI. If you are an Australian reader, the ACCC has the same shape of power, and the National AI Plan just made it the only one you have.

**Budget the unlikely.** Put the low-probability, high-cost AI failure on the risk register with a control against it, and be able to say what the control costs, the way you already do for fire. At minimum, you have identified a risk that never comes true (which is probably at least *part* of your job anyway) and in the event the worst-case does happen, you are prepared to respond immediately, because as with so many things in this industry, that feels like about all the time you will get: full speed to 0 at the speed of a car wreck.

And one for you, regardless of your role in your business, or as it relates to your personal life: the three most-quoted sentences of the week, Amodei's "could," the President's "No, I don't have any," and Cruz's "approval," each mean something different at source than in the headline. Each of them has provided what appears to be a load-bearing quote to the media, and in none of the three cases does it actually hold up as load-bearing under scrutiny.

## Where I think I am most likely wrong

The strongest case against everything above is that the improvised instrument is winning.

Defense-side counsel, who have no reason to flatter attorneys general, describe state AG power as broad, flexible and fast: civil investigative demands, subpoenas, compelled testimony, all under statutes deliberately written loose so that legislatures do not have to anticipate every new harm. The AGs moved in weeks. Congress has moved in fourteen months and produced one committee vote, twenty-nine to nothing, on a bill that then sat for two and a half months. On raw responsiveness, the thing nobody designed has beaten the thing everybody is arguing about, and Rep. Kat Cammack's objection (to paraphrase: "applying a federal action here will have no impact except to drive up cost") is not rhetorical either: a startup cannot build a compliance function for fifty states and survive. Trust me, I know. I tried. And failed miserably.

The second case against me is Sacks's, and it goes at the control rather than the politics. His position is that liability after the fact is the regime, and that an approval process before release adds nothing a courtroom cannot supply. Name his book too: a venture portfolio with plenty of the companies he says a frontier-only rule would leave to be policed by evaluators the frontier chose. The commercial interests run both ways in this fight, and that is the point of the seating chart. But the shape of his objection is fair, and it is the one I would make myself: a badge is worth exactly what the badge-holder's independence is worth, and if the answer to "who checks" turns out to be "people the labs picked," that is self-certification with a lanyard and a removal of liability to boot.

Here is why I still think the institutionalists have the better of it. Consumer-protection law compels documents after the fact. It cannot set a standard before deployment, cannot put a person in the building during an incident, cannot require a report on a clock, and produces fifty answers to one question. It is forensic, not preventive. This country built compelled-access regimes for aviation and for chemical plants, with investigators who can walk onto the site and a statute that says they may. It has not built one for this, and the only people asking for one this week were a Treasury Secretary, a Republican committee chairman on daytime television, and the three men who stand to be regulated by it. Liability after the fact is what you have when nobody has built the other thing yet. It is not a design. It is the default.

The odds being long has never been the reason we skip the insurance. It is usually the reason we can afford it.
