Every AI governance framework in circulation ends the same way. The last box says the board oversees it.

It is the final line of the runbook. Escalate to the board. Every maturity model, every vendor deck, every internal policy I have read this year terminates in that sentence, and it gets treated as the part you do not have to explain, the way a fire evacuation plan does not bother to explain the street outside. The assumption sitting underneath it is that somebody is at the end of that escalation path who can take the call, follow what they are being told, and tell whether the answer they are getting is a good one or just a well-delivered one.

I went looking for that person in the disclosure data, because that is the only place you can look for them without asking anybody how they feel about themselves.

They are not there.

## What the S&P 500 wrote down about itself

The Conference Board (a business research outfit that has been counting things about American companies since before most of those companies existed) went through S&P 500 disclosures as of December 2025. Over a single year, the share of companies naming AI as a material risk went from 12% to 83%. Disclosed director technology expertise went from 20% to 51%. Disclosed cybersecurity expertise went from 15% to 27%.

Disclosed AI expertise went from 1.5% to 2.7%.

Put the first number and the last number next to each other. 83% of the S&P 500 has told its own investors, in a filing, that AI is a material risk to the business. 2.7% of the directors of those companies disclose any AI expertise at all.

The kind of number that is matters, because most of the numbers on this beat are the other kind. This one is disclosure-derived. Nobody surveyed a group of directors and asked how confident they felt. Somebody read what the companies themselves filed, under the rules governing what a company says about its own board, and counted. Companies have every incentive to disclose expertise they actually have, so the 2.7% measures what employers were willing to put in writing about their own directors. Call it a floor rather than a ceiling. The floor is 2.7%.

Heidrick & Struggles, the executive search firm that places a great many of these people, looked at the incoming class rather than the sitting one. Board Monitor US 2026, covering 383 Fortune 500 seats filled during 2025: only 6% of newly appointed directors had previously served as a chief technology officer, chief information officer, chief data officer or chief AI officer. Six percent is the level, and I am going to resist the sentence everybody writes next, because I cannot support it -- I have seen no evidence that number has fallen. It has not needed to fall. It has been low the whole time, while the risk disclosures went up by a factor of seven.

## The part where everybody is sure they are fine

Here is where it gets uncomfortable rather than merely structural.

BCG surveyed 351 chief executives and 274 directors in March of this year. About 60% of those CEOs said their boards are rushing AI transformation. Not pushing, not urging, not demanding more ambition. Rushing, in the sense you would use about somebody taking a corner too fast.

The directors do not agree. Fifty-four percent of them said the organization is moving at the right pace, and 18% said it is moving too slowly and that this concerns them.

Two things about that survey before I build anything on it. The 60% is 60% of the 351 chief executives, not of the 625 people in the study, and those are different populations answering different questions. And the sample is 44% US-based and 76% private companies over $100 million in revenue, with no Australian sub-sample disclosed at all. So this is not a statistic about listed-company boardrooms, and it is certainly not one about ours. It is a real measurement of a real disagreement inside a specific kind of company, and that is all it is.

What makes it worth the space is the line underneath the headline, which BCG published and almost nobody has quoted:

> "frustration with the pace of adoption is highest in those who have a more permissive view on governance."

And next to it:

> "lower levels of self-assessed AI understanding may be fueling board FOMO... Board members with less confidence in their AI knowledge are more likely to believe their organizations are moving too slowly."

Sit with that one. The directors pressing hardest to go faster are, on the same instrument, the ones who report understanding the least about what they are going faster at. I do not want to read that as a character finding, because it is not one. It is the ordinary human response to sitting somewhere you cannot evaluate the thing under discussion: you cannot assess the risk of moving, so you assess the risk of being left behind, which is the only risk on the table you are actually qualified to see.

The same survey found that 75% of board members believe their AI knowledge is on par with or more advanced than their peers'.

Now, a self-assessment and a disclosure count are different instruments measuring different things -- one is what people think of themselves, the other is what their company filed. I am not going to pretend they are the same number seen twice. But the distance between them is the actual finding of this piece. Three-quarters of a group placing itself at or above its own peers is a statement about calibration rather than about AI, and a board with no calibration has no instrument for weighing anything management carries into it. Which is the entire job.

## What I have actually watched happen

I have sat in meetings with my own board, and I have sat in meetings with customer boards, and I can tell you from experience that the most aggressive questions about AI come from the people with little or no understanding of it. I think that aligns with the CEO finding above, the one about boards rushing transformation, though perhaps not in the way many of the people quoting it might have meant.

Every board I have interacted with has intense levels of FOMO about AI, being concerned that our business is going to end up worthless if we do not do enough of it (which is certainly a pattern visible in the public US markets, though it has moderated a bit), and in the case of customer boards, being concerned that they are leaving money on the table or otherwise providing a disservice to their business by not pursuing it more aggressively. And I would agree with the analysis that it is markedly worse with people who are entirely unfamiliar with AI and have not realized the extent to which it does not replace things, it just provides more ability to realize known goals quickly.

I think for many CEOs, that response -- the pushing back of the board to accomplish more faster -- is so out of character for their boards that it seems disproportionately impactful.

## Australia, where the directors are telling you the truth

The Australian data is smaller in reputation and better in construction.

The AICD's Director Sentiment Index for the first half of 2026 ran through Roy Morgan, fielded to 828 company directors between 20 February and 10 March. More than half of them said the pace of change is faster than their organizations can keep up with. Almost two-thirds reported that AI tools have already delivered productivity benefits, so this is not a group of skeptics talking themselves out of the technology. And they put vulnerability to cyber-crime, data security and the potential misuse of AI at the top of the risk list.

American chief executives report being rushed by their boards. Australian directors report being outpaced by the world. Same year, opposite direction, and the Australian sample is the sounder of the two -- 828 directors in one jurisdiction, a named fieldhouse and a stated fieldwork window, against 351 executives spread across an undisclosed mix of markets.

I find the Australian answer more useful, and not only because it is my second audience. It is the answer of people who have correctly identified that they are behind. That is the beginning of governance. The other answer is the beginning of a purchase order.

## Your own signals directorate already wrote the questions

This month the Australian Signals Directorate (the agency that does signals intelligence and national cyber security, so roughly the NSA and CISA in one building) published *Frontier AI cyber threat considerations for boards of directors*, developed with the AICD.

Weeks before this piece. Aimed directly at the people in the room.

The document is careful with its verbs and the care is worth copying. On attack outcomes it hedges: frontier AI **can** make cyber attacks faster, more effective and easier to carry out, and this **may** rapidly invalidate an organization's current risk tolerance. Then, on the underlying change, it stops hedging entirely:

> "Overall, frontier AI models are currently, and will continue to, fundamentally transform both offensive and defensive cyber capabilities."

That is a signals agency writing in the present indicative about transformation and in the conditional about consequences, which is exactly the right way around and almost the reverse of how it reaches most boards.

And then it does the thing nobody else in this conversation has done. It writes out the questions. Sixteen of them, under a heading that says *what threshold questions should boards ask*, and they are not the questions you would expect a government document to produce. A sample, verbatim:

> "What assumptions underpin our current risk assessments, and how might frontier AI threats invalidate those assumptions?"
> "Are there known weaknesses in our systems that could become materially more dangerous if identified and exploited at machine speed?"
> "Are we delaying addressing any weaknesses due to a perceived low exposure or severity?"

That third one is doing more work than it looks like it is doing. A signals directorate is asking boards whether they are deferring on the basis of a severity rating, in a year when the people who used to supply severity ratings have started declining to. I will come back to it on Thursday.

So the gap I have been describing is not a foreign statistic any more. Your own national security agency has written down the questions your board should be able to answer, put them somewhere free and public, and dated them a few weeks before you read this. The gap is now measurable at your own table, in an afternoon, with a document nobody has to buy and nobody has to be talked into.

## What the obligation actually is, and what it is not

The AICD, with the Human Technology Institute at UTS, publishes a separate document -- *A Director's Guide to AI Governance*, second edition, June 2026 -- and its central sentence is the standing obligation:

> "Boards are ultimately accountable for the adoption, implementation and outcomes of AI technologies in their organisations."

That sentence is in the foreword, incidentally, which is written by a minister rather than by the AICD or the university, and I mention it because it is the difference between an industry body's aspiration and a statement of where the government thinks responsibility sits.

The guide itself is realistic about what that requires. Directors "do not need to be AI experts. But they do need a minimum viable understanding of AI to ask the right questions, make informed strategic decisions, and ensure appropriate governance structures and processes are in place." That is the correct bar and it is a low one, which is what makes 2.7% and 6% so awkward. Nobody is asking a board to build the model. They are asking it to notice when the answer it just received was fluent and hollow.

Let me be precise about the legal position, because this beat attracts overreach and I would rather under-claim it. In Europe, NIS2 Article 20(1) is law and it bites: management bodies of essential and important entities must approve the cyber risk-management measures, oversee their implementation, and "can be held liable for infringements." A hard obligation with a hard consequence.

Everything else you have read is doctrine rather than case law. There is no decided AI-Caremark case. Not one. The Delaware oversight authority everybody cites in these pieces predates all of this by years, and the legal commentary is careful with its modals in a way the summaries are not: liability **may** arise, a board **may** find, AI stress-tests the oversight duty rather than transforming it. Every headline telling you directors are now liable for AI failures has taken a *may* and quietly promoted it to a *does*. The doctrine is solid, the exposure is real, and the cases have not happened yet. You can hold all three of those at once.

Australia is closer to a stated position than most places, and I want to hand it over carefully. The same guide reproduces what it presents as ASIC's own words: that directors and officers must discharge their duties with a reasonable degree of care and diligence, that "these duties extend to the adoption, deployment and use of AI," and that they should be aware of AI use inside their companies, of how far they are relying on AI-generated information to do their jobs, and of the reasonably foreseeable risks that come with it.

A regulator describing the duty it already has as already covering this is a far more interesting claim than a new rule would be, and considerably harder to argue with. I went looking for it at ASIC itself and could not find it. Everything I could reach was a law firm quoting the same passage. So it is real, it is quoted at one remove, and until somebody puts the regulator's own page in front of me I will keep saying "as quoted in the guide" rather than "ASIC says."

## What we did last time

We have built a missing oversight layer before, and it is worth remembering how, because it was not by hiring geniuses.

After the accounting failures of the early 2000s, the fix for boards that could not read their own financials was not a requirement that every director become an accountant. It was a disclosure rule. Say whether your audit committee has a financial expert, and if it does not, say why not. The SEC was unusually blunt about the limits of what it was doing, in the adopting release itself: "none of these amendments requires a company to have an audit committee financial expert." It made an absence say its own name out loud and left the filling of it to everybody else.

It also did something in the same rule that is worth stealing outright, because it kills the objection every board raises. Naming somebody as the financial expert "does not impose on such person any duties, obligations or liability that are greater than" what they already carry as a director. Say that part again, because it is the whole reason the mechanism was usable: the rule that made you point at somebody explicitly guaranteed that pointing at them did not make them the defendant. Nobody in the AI governance conversation has proposed anything like it, and it costs nothing.

Then two things happened close together, which is why this story usually gets told wrong. The disclosure rule landed in January 2003. In November, the SEC approved new listing standards from the NYSE and Nasdaq, and those are not comply-or-explain at all: audit committee members have to be financially literate, and at least one has to have real accounting or financial management expertise, or you do not stay listed. Visibility and a floor, in the same year, from two different directions. Anybody who tells you the disclosure rule fixed this on its own is claiming something the timing will not support, and I am not going to claim it either.

Which also answers the sharpest objection to everything above, and I would rather raise it than have it raised at me. You could reasonably say that 2.7% measures the absence of a rule rather than the absence of people - that boards disclose financial expertise because something makes them, and disclose AI expertise at 2.7% because nothing does. That is exactly right, and it is the argument, not a hole in it. Financial expertise became visible because somebody made it visible. Nobody has done that here, so we are all reading a number that only tells us what companies volunteered.

Which is the part that should worry you, because the 2003 response reached for two instruments and the 2026 response has reached for none. AI risk disclosure is now near-universal and it sits in the same documents, filed under the same rules, read by the same investors. The expertise line is a few pages from the risk line. But there is no floor, there is no comply-or-explain, and the absence is only visible to somebody who goes and counts it by hand, which is exactly what the Conference Board had to do to produce the number at the top of this piece.

And I think that the answer to this, as with so many things having to do with AI, is governance, a drum I will keep beating until the head blows out.


## So go and find the end of your own escalation path

**Read your last AI paper to the board again, and mark every sentence a reasonable person could not check.** Not the ones that are wrong. The ones that are unfalsifiable from the chair: the maturity ratings, the "on track" statuses, the risk scores with no arithmetic behind them. That is the material your oversight layer is currently being asked to oversee, and if most of the page is unfalsifiable then the meeting was a briefing rather than a review, whatever the minutes say.

**Then take the ASD document to the next meeting and just work through it out loud.** It is free, it is short, it is written by your own government rather than by anybody selling you anything, and it converts an argument about board composition into an afternoon of specific questions with specific answers. You will find out very quickly whether the gap at your table is knowledge, capacity or attention, and those three have completely different fixes.

**And ask what your own filings say about your board.** Not what your governance framework says. What the disclosure says, in the document your investors actually read. If your company is in the 83% that names AI as a material risk, look at the expertise line a few pages away and decide whether the two pages are describing the same organization.

Nobody decided against building the oversight layer. Every framework in circulation simply treats it as the part that was already there, the way you assume the street outside the fire door exists because the plan has an arrow pointing at it.

So the question is narrower and considerably harder than whether your board has an AI expert, and I would be suspicious of anybody selling you one. Who on your board can tell a good answer from a confident one, and how would you find that out before it is the answer that matters?
