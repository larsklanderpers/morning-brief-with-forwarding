Meta sold seven million pairs of camera glasses last year. The newest ones cost six hundred and forty-nine Australian dollars, answer in Kylie Jenner's voice, and the small light that tells the room it is recording can be switched off.

Nobody ever legislated against notebooks. You can carry one into any meeting in the country, write down every word anybody says, and walk out with it. No notice period, no signage, no consent form. The law is relaxed about this for a reason that has held up for about four hundred years: a notebook stays with the person carrying it. Whatever it captures, it captures for them.

What has arrived is a notebook that publishes. Not eventually, not if the owner decides to, but by construction, because sending what it captures somewhere else is the entire product. And it turns out that the whole architecture of Australian recording law is built on the assumption that never happens.

## The man who asked for the ban, and what he actually asked for

Professor Dali Kaafar is the Executive Director of Macquarie University's Cyber Security Hub. At the end of July he said Meta's new glasses should be banned or restricted, and the line that travelled was this one: *"We're about to find out whether privacy can survive surveillance becoming fashionable."*

I want to be careful with him, because the version of this story that went around is not the version he told. He is an academic, not a government official, and the headlines that promoted him to national cyber chief did him no favors. His ask stops well short of prohibition: a restriction *except in genuine use cases such as disability*, alongside support for a moratorium on sale while the question gets worked out. That is a considerably more careful position than the one being argued about, and I will come back to the carve-out at the end, because it is the most interesting thing in this entire story and almost nobody has picked it up.

The sharpest thing he said is not the fashionable-surveillance line. It is this: *"The issue of consent is not really about the wearer of the glasses. It is about everyone else in the frame."*

Hold onto that, because Australian law does not hold onto it at all.

## The device did not break the rule. It broke the exception.

Here is the part I did not expect when I started reading the statutes.

Australian recording law is usually described as a one-party-consent country, which is wrong, and the wrongness matters. There are three groups. In Victoria, Queensland and the Northern Territory a participant is simply outside the offence. In Western Australia and South Australia participant recording is prohibited with only a narrow lawful-interest exception. And in New South Wales, Tasmania and the ACT it is prohibited with a broad personal-use exception, which is the group worth your attention, because an exception is a condition, and a condition can fail.

The New South Wales condition is the one to read. A participant may record a private conversation where the recording is *"not made for the purpose of communicating or publishing the conversation or a report of it to persons who are not parties."*

Now put Kaafar's own description of the device next to that sentence. A continuous, first-person feed that serves as an input pipeline for AI data collection and model training. That is not a device that might one day communicate the conversation to non-parties. That is a device whose reason for existing is to communicate the conversation to non-parties, and it does so whether or not the person wearing it has thought about it for a single second.

So the exception does not fail because of what the wearer intends. It fails by construction, on the product's own datasheet. The law was not caught out by a technology it failed to imagine. The technology was built through the one door the law deliberately left open, and it walked through that door carrying a purpose the door was never sized for.

And the video side is looser than the audio side, which is the opposite of what most people assume. Queensland, Tasmania and the ACT have no general optical surveillance offence at all -- they regulate listening devices and stop there. In New South Wales the optical offence is gated on trespass: it bites where installing the device involved entry onto premises without consent. A visitor you invited into your building, wearing a camera on their face, has not trespassed onto anything. Silent video capture of a meeting in Brisbane is close to ungoverned by surveillance-devices law, and I do not think that is a drafting failure either. It is a regime written when putting a camera in a room meant putting a camera in a room.

## The workplace statute is pointed the wrong way

The second failure is more embarrassing, because this one we did write recently and we still aimed it backwards.

New South Wales has a Workplace Surveillance Act. It is not weak. An employer must give fourteen days' written notice before surveillance starts, and the notice has to say what kind -- camera, computer, tracking -- when it begins, whether it is continuous or intermittent, ongoing or temporary. Cameras must be visible and signposted. Covert surveillance requires an authority issued by a Magistrate and is otherwise flatly prohibited. There is no surveillance in toilets, change rooms or showers, with penalties attached, and separate penalties for surveilling employees outside work and for misusing the records.

Every single one of those obligations runs in one direction: from the employer, down, to the employee.

None of it reaches a supplier. None of it reaches a customer, a contractor, an auditor, a consultant, a candidate you are interviewing, or the account manager from your platform vendor who has come in for the quarterly review. The entire statutory architecture assumes surveillance flows downward from the organization onto its own people, because for the whole history of workplace surveillance, that is who could afford it.

Ambient wearables make it lateral and inbound. The threat model turned ninety degrees and the statute did not turn with it.

## What it costs to do this, and what it costs to stop it

The whole argument is in the asymmetry, so let me put the two numbers side by side.

Six hundred and forty-nine dollars buys a member of the public a continuous first-person audio and video capture rig with an indicator light that can be turned off. It is sold at retail, to anyone, with a celebrity's voice inside it, and seven million of the previous generation moved in a year. The capability scales by retail distribution, which is to say it scales as fast as people feel like buying it.

Now price the defense. You have a workplace policy you can enforce against your own staff and nobody else. You have a surveillance-devices regime whose optical limb is either trespass-gated or entirely absent depending on which state you are standing in. And you have the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, which has been live since June of last year and is a genuine instrument -- intrusion upon seclusion, misuse of information, non-economic damages capped at a substantial figure -- except that it is a court action an individual has to bring themselves, on their own time and their own money, with the privacy commissioner expressly having no administrative role in it.

So the capability costs six hundred and forty-nine dollars and scales by distribution. The control costs a lawsuit and scales by nothing at all. That gap is not a gap in enforcement. It is a gap in what there is to enforce.

It is worth saying that this is the same shape as the argument I made about sandbox escapes a fortnight ago, and about vendor AI on Wednesday. The thing being deployed moves at the speed of a product release. The thing meant to contain it moves at the speed of a statute, and half the time the containment story turns out to be a story about somebody else's infrastructure anyway.

## Where I would push back on myself

**"People have carried phones for twenty years. This is a moral panic with a new lens."** The strongest objection and it deserves a straight answer. A phone is visible, episodic and socially legible -- you can see someone holding it up, and the room adjusts. What separates them is that the recording runs continuously by default, the signal that it is happening can be disabled, and the destination is a model rather than a camera roll. But notice what my own argument concedes: the law treats the phone and the glasses identically, because the law never governed the device. It governed the purpose. The purpose changed and the text did not.

**"Australian regulators have not said anything, so it cannot be serious."** I went looking and could not find published guidance from any Australian regulator on this device class -- no inquiry, no enforcement, nothing beyond a privacy-office spokesperson making a general point that biometric information attracts higher protection. I want to be honest that this is an absence, and absences are hard to prove and easy to over-read. The innocent explanation is the likely one: this is a consumer product that is weeks old at retail, and regulators move slower than shipping. The live regulatory action on ambient glasses anywhere in the world right now is British, not Australian -- the UK information commissioner has written to Meta after reporting that outsourced reviewers in Nairobi were watching footage that included people in their bathrooms and bedrooms. *"The claims in this article are concerning"* is regulator language for something considerably stronger.

**"There is a real product here for real people."** Yes, and that is exactly Kaafar's carve-out, and it is where this piece has to end up.

## The carve-out is the whole argument

Here is the thing that has been sitting in the middle of this story since the end of July and I have not seen anyone pick it up.

Kaafar did not ask for a ban. He asked for a restriction *except in genuine use cases such as disability*. Which is obviously correct -- a device that describes the world aloud to somebody who cannot see it is not the problem, and a rule that catches it is a bad rule.

But look at what that carve-out requires in order to function. Somebody has to be able to tell the difference, at the moment of use, between a device operating as an accessibility aid and a device operating as an ingestion pipeline. That distinction is invisible from outside the frames and absent from the hardware. It lives in configuration, in purpose, in where the feed goes and who keeps it, and to enforce it you would need exactly the thing this entire week has been about: a governance layer that someone can point at, attached to something specific, that a regulator can inspect.

There is no such layer. And when there is no such layer, the only instrument left on the shelf is prohibition.

We know this because Australia has already done it. The under-16 social media ban commenced on the tenth of December last year, with penalties for non-compliant platforms running to tens of millions, and it was pitched to the world as the first domino. Whatever you think of it -- and reasonable people I respect are on both sides -- it was not chosen because prohibition is elegant. It was chosen because there was nothing more precise available to reach for.

That is what a ban actually is. Not the opposite of governance, and not the strong version of it either. It is the shape governance takes when there is nothing to attach it to. A blunt instrument is what you get in a room with no fine ones, and the more carve-outs a proposed ban needs in order to be fair, the more clearly it is telling you that a ban is the wrong instrument and the right one has not been built.

## The two things I would actually do

**Stop reaching for the workplace statute.** If your first instinct on reading this was that your surveillance policy covers it, go and read the policy. It almost certainly governs what you do to your staff, which is the wrong direction entirely. Knowing that you have no instrument is more useful than believing you have one.

**Then name the rooms.** Not a policy, not a poster, not a sign at reception that a visitor is under no obligation to obey. Just the honest list of the rooms where you would genuinely mind -- where the pricing gets argued, where the incident gets discussed, where somebody's health or their livelihood gets talked about out loud. That list is short in most organizations, and it is the only thing on this page you can produce this week without anyone's permission.

## What the fashion line actually means

Kaafar's most quoted sentence is about surveillance becoming fashionable, and I understand why that is the one that travelled. But I think he was making a narrower and better point than the one it gets used for. Surveillance being sold as a lifestyle rather than as a technology is not a story about vanity. It is a story about what regulation can see. A technology arrives through procurement, or a standards body, or an import control, and there are people whose job it is to meet it there. A lifestyle arrives through a retail shelf and a celebrity's voice, and there is nobody standing at that door at all.

So the question is not whether these should be banned, and it was never going to be. The question is what you would want a regulator to attach a rule to instead, and whether anybody is building that thing. Because if the answer is no, then the ban is coming eventually -- not because it works, but because it is the only instrument anyone has left, and by then the argument about carve-outs will be somebody else's to lose.
