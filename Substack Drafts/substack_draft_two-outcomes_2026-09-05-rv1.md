You drive somewhere new with the directions on. You arrive faster than you would have, with no wrong turns, and you feel fine about it. Then a month later you drive there again with the phone dead, and you realize you have absolutely no idea where you are.

Both of those are true at once. The directions made the first trip better and the second trip impossible. And here is the thing about it that I keep coming back to: the first outcome gets measured every single time, by the app, in minutes saved. The second outcome gets measured never, because nobody ever takes the phone away on purpose to find out.

Last Saturday the skill was reading somebody else's claim -- who published it, what they sell, whether the number matches the noun. This Saturday sits a step above that. It is not about how you read the work. It is about how you design it, and specifically about which of the two numbers you have decided to look at.

## The number everybody measures

The evidence that these tools make people better at the task in front of them is, at this point, not really in dispute, and I want to give it its due before I complicate it.

The cleanest study I found this week is a randomized trial first posted as a preprint in June of last year and since published in *npj Digital Medicine*. Seventy US-licensed physicians -- thirty-nine residents, forty-one attendings, almost all internal medicine -- worked through diagnostic vignettes in one of three configurations. In one, the AI gave its opinion first and the doctor worked from there. In another, the doctor formed a view first and then saw the AI's. In the third, conventional resources only.

The numbers: AI-first, 85% diagnostic accuracy. AI-second, 82%. Conventional resources, 75%.

Ten points. On a task where ten points is a great deal. Both AI configurations beat the control convincingly, and the one where the machine went first did best of all.

I want to be honest about what that did to this piece, because it is the kind of thing that usually gets quietly dropped. The version of this Saturday I had planned was going to argue that the way to use these tools well is to make them ask before they answer -- to form your own view first and let the tool critique it. That is a nice idea and I still like it. But the best trial I can find on the subject points the other way on accuracy: the doctors who got the answer first did slightly better than the doctors who tried first. I am not going to cite a study for something it does not show. So the argument has to be a different argument.

## The number almost nobody measures

Here is the different argument.

That trial measured one thing: how accurate the doctor was *with the tool in the room*. It measured it well. It is a real result. And it is the number every vendor study, every pilot, every productivity claim you will read this year is also measuring, because it is the number that is easy to measure and the number that sells.

There is a second number. It is how good the person is at the task *after* months of using the tool, *when the tool is not there*. The dead phone. And I can find almost nobody measuring it, except one group in Poland who did, and what they found is the single beat of this piece I would ask you to sit with.

Four endoscopy centers, September 2021 to March 2022. One thousand four hundred and forty-three colonoscopies. The centers introduced AI-assisted polyp detection partway through. The researchers did not look at the AI-assisted procedures. They looked at the ordinary ones -- the colonoscopies the same endoscopists performed *without* the AI, before and after they had started using it.

Before exposure to the AI, the adenoma detection rate in unassisted procedures was 28.4%. After, it was 22.4%. Six points, with a confidence interval that does not cross zero. The authors, in *The Lancet Gastroenterology and Hepatology*, put it in the careful language the finding deserves: continuous exposure to AI *"might reduce"* detection in standard colonoscopy, *"suggesting a negative effect on endoscopist behaviour."*

Might. It is one study, retrospective, in one country, over six months, and the commentaries since have pointed at the obvious confounds. I am not going to overstate it, and neither did they. But notice what kind of study it is. It is one of the very few that measured the second number at all -- and it moved, in the direction you would fear, in a population of trained specialists doing the thing they are trained for.

So: with the tool, you are better. Without it, after a while, you may be worse than you were before you had it. Both true. Neither contradicts the other. They are answers to different questions, and one of the questions is almost never asked.

## Why the second number is so easy to lose

There is a study from 2023 that I think explains the mechanism, and it is worth being precise about it because it gets misquoted.

Researchers at the University of Deusto ran a series of experiments in which people did a made-up diagnostic task -- classifying tissue samples in a fictional disease -- with the help of an AI that had a deliberate, systematic error built in. Then the AI went away, and they did the task alone.

In the third experiment, with about two hundred online participants, 80.7% of them noticed the AI was making mistakes. Eight in ten. They saw it. And then, in the researchers' words, *"many of them still relied on the AI recommendations to perform the task,"* and when the AI was gone, they kept reproducing its error on their own.

These were not doctors and it was not a real disease, and I want that on the record, because a lab result with lay participants is exactly the kind of thing that gets dressed up as more than it is. But the shape of the finding is what matters. Noticing that a tool is wrong and being able to override it are two different capacities. The first one is cheap and most people have it. The second one requires you to have an independent view of what right looks like -- and that is precisely the thing the second number measures, and precisely the thing that erodes when the tool does the work.

So the mechanism runs like this. The tool improves your output. Your output is what gets measured. Your independent judgment, which nobody is measuring, gets less practice each time. And by the time the phone dies, you have a long record of good outcomes and no way to produce one.

## The people already defending the second number

I said this was a Saturday and I meant it, so here is the part that goes the other way.

Practitioners are not stupid about this. A Microsoft Research study published last October -- and it is worth knowing it is Microsoft, because Microsoft sells the assistant these people use -- surveyed 860 software developers on where they want AI help and where they do not. The pattern was clear enough that the authors named it: developers want the help on the mechanical work, and draw *"clear limits for identity- and relationship-centric work (e.g., mentoring)."* They will let the tool write the boilerplate. They will not let it be the person who teaches the junior. That is a group of people protecting the second number without being told to, on the work where they have understood it matters.

And there is at least one study pointing at a design that helps rather than hurts, and it is worth the numbers.

A CHI paper from 2023, out of the MIT Media Lab, put 204 people in front of ten socially divisive statements -- violent video games cause aggression, that kind of thing -- and asked whether each one was logically valid. Unassisted, they scored 44%. Worse than a coin flip. Told the answer with a causal explanation from the AI, 57%. Asked a question instead -- *if one person played violent video games and was aggressive, does it follow that everyone who plays them will be?* -- 67%. And the advantage of asking over telling showed up specifically on the flawed statements, which is where discernment is the whole job.

The part I find most useful is a smaller finding underneath that one. When the AI *told* people the answer, they reported feeling they had enough information and were less inclined to look further. When it *asked*, they did not. Being told made them stop looking. That is the Deusto result from the other side: the mechanism by which the second number erodes is not that the tool is wrong, it is that being handed an answer switches off the search.

Two things to hold it lightly with, and the authors say both themselves. The AI in that study was always right, so it says nothing about what happens when the tool is wrong. And it was one sitting; nobody came back a month later to see whether the effect had stayed. One lab study, in the right direction, with a mechanism attached. Not the foundation of anything. A real hint that the design space exists.

## Measure the second number yourself, because nobody else will

Here is the constructive part, and it is genuinely constructive, because unlike almost everything else in this newsletter it is entirely inside your own control and it costs nothing.

**Pick the one or two things you are accountable for and would be embarrassed to be unable to do alone.** Not everything. The reconciliation you sign. The architecture call. The diagnosis, the clause, the estimate. The work where, if the tool were wrong and you could not tell, it would be your name on it.

**Then, on a schedule, do one with the tool switched off.** Once a month. Once a quarter. Whatever you can actually keep to. Not as a purity test -- as a measurement. You are not proving you do not need the tool. You are finding out whether the second number has moved, before the day it matters is the day you find out.

**And when it has moved, treat that as information, not failure.** The Polish endoscopists were not lazy. The doctors in the trial were not careless. The second number moves because the first number is being optimized, and that is the correct thing to optimize most of the time. The point is only to know which of your capacities you have quietly stopped exercising, so that you can choose to keep the ones you cannot afford to lose.

That is the whole design decision. Use the tool. Let it make you better on the day. And keep one honest rep on the work where being unable to do it alone would be the thing that ends badly.

The reason to do this now, rather than later, is not that the tools are dangerous. It is that the second number is cheap to measure and expensive to recover. A morning a quarter keeps it. A year of not looking loses it. And you are, as far as I can tell, the only person in your organization with any reason to check.

That last part is not a throwaway. It is tomorrow.
