Every AI contract you signed this year has a clock in it. The clock starts when somebody breaks in, and there is no clock at all for the day the thing you bought simply gets it wrong.

That is my whole perspective for today. However, since two sentences alone don't really give you the full text, I want to spend the next few thousand words showing you that I am not simply paraphrasing; that is the literal default text, and it is the same text at every vendor you are likely to be paying.

Think about the last time you brought in a temp through an agency. The agency agreement probably had a security section, and a good one. If the temp's building pass was stolen, or somebody used their login to walk off with your customer list, the agency would call you, in writing, within a set number of hours. What the agreement almost certainly did not have was any clause at all about the temp spending three days sending the right invoices to the wrong customers. That was not a security event. Nobody broke in. The temp was exactly who they said they were, sitting at the desk you gave them, using the access you approved, and doing the job badly. You found out when a customer rang. And when you rang the agency, the agreement had a sentence for that too, which was that you were responsible for supervising the work.

Now swap the temp for a model, and the agency for the company that sells it to you, and read on.

Earlier this week I wrote about a severity score nobody assigned, and yesterday about a reporting threshold drafted by the company that had just had the incident. Today is where that stops being an observation and starts costing money, because the label upstream of a contract clause decides whether the clause does anything, and almost nobody negotiated the label. They negotiated the clock.

## The sentence all five vendors borrowed

Read the data processing terms of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and AWS (sorry Meta, I didn't put yours in the frame here, but I suspect it's "much of a muchness") start to finish, looking for the trigger that sets off their obligation to tell you something went wrong, and you find that all five use the same sentence. Not a similar sentence. The same one, lifted almost word for word from the definition of a personal data breach in Article 4(12) of the GDPR.

Anthropic's version is typical: a Security Breach *"means a breach of Anthropic's security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to, Customer Personal Data."* And then the number that most security leaders think they bought: Anthropic *"will notify Customer in writing without undue delay, but in any event within 48 hours."*

Forty-eight hours is a good clock. It is faster than most statutes require. But look at what has to happen for it to start. There has to be a breach of **the vendor's security**, and it has to touch **your personal data**. Both. A model that confidently produces a wrong answer, or an agent that takes an action nobody intended, using permissions it was legitimately given, satisfies neither half. Nobody breached anybody's security. Nothing was accessed that was not supposed to be accessed. The thing just did the wrong thing. (Also note that the shape of this disclosure language is suspiciously similar to OpenAI's "new suggested policy structure" from yesterday.)

OpenAI says *"a breach of security."* Google says *"a breach of Google's security."* AWS says *"a breach of AWS's security."* Microsoft says *"a breach of security."* The word *malfunction* does not appear in any of the five documents. Neither does *misalignment*, *harmful output*, *hallucination*, or *AI Act*. Search the complete documents for each of them and you come back with nothing.

We're in the process of building a pilot where there are two key axes of potential exposure that we have to watch for: material disclosure of customer intellectual property and material disclosure of our customer's customers' PII. We slowed down the pilot from my initial draft because we consistently encountered places where a model whose instruction was "disclose neither of these two things" made its own determinations about what qualified as disclosure AND what qualified as one of those two things. We were lucky enough that we were in early trials when it happened -- at $10,000 per breach (Australian privacy law), it would have gotten really expensive really fast if we hadn't caught it. `[VERIFY -- $10,000 per breach: see header ruling 1]` In no event would the model provider have been responsible despite its model being the one reaching its own conclusions.

## The disclaimer on the facing page

A notification clause that does not reach behavior would be a gap. What turns it into a design is what sits a few pages away in the same paper.

OpenAI's Services Agreement: *"Customer is solely responsible for all use of the Outputs and for evaluating the accuracy and appropriateness of Output for Customer's use case."* Anthropic's commercial terms, in the capital letters lawyers use when they want to be sure you saw it: *"ANTHROPIC DOES NOT WARRANT, AND DISCLAIMS THAT, THE SERVICES OR OUTPUTS ARE ACCURATE, COMPLETE OR ERROR-FREE."* Google's service terms say generative services *"may provide inaccurate or offensive Generated Output, and are not designed for or intended to meet Customer's regulatory, legal, or other obligations."*

So read the two halves together. A problem with how the vendor handles your data is a notifiable event with a clock on it. A problem with what the vendor's model produces is, contractually, not an event at all. It is your supervision problem. That is the temp agency again, sentence for sentence.

The one place the paper does reach something like behavior is worth showing you, because the remedy tells you how the drafters think. OpenAI's agreement says that *"if an OpenAI update materially reduces the Services functionality, OpenAI will notify Customer at the Account email address,"* and you then have five business days to decide whether to terminate on thirty days' notice. Microsoft goes further on Azure, with twelve months' notice before removing *"any material feature or functionality."* Both are real, and both are about the product **losing** a capability. Neither is about a product that keeps every feature it had and starts using them badly. And notice the shape of the remedy even where it exists: you are told, and you may leave. Nobody is obliged to fix it, and nobody is obliged to tell you why it happened.

Then there is the ceiling on all of it. OpenAI's total liability under the agreement is capped at what you paid in the twelve months before the event. Anthropic's is the same shape. A model error that costs you a customer, a filing or a week of rework is measured against that number, if it can be claimed at all, which, per the facing page, it mostly cannot.

## When the law finally reached it

The fair response to all of this is that contracts follow law, and the law is still catching up. That is true. What is striking is what the law did when it did catch up.

California's SB 53 is the first statute in the United States to define a reportable AI incident, and it got as far as the case everyone is actually worried about: a frontier model using *"deceptive techniques against the frontier developer to subvert the controls or monitoring of its frontier developer."* Then it added two conditions. The behavior has to occur *"outside of the context of an evaluation designed to elicit this behavior,"* and it has to demonstrate *"materially increased catastrophic risk."* Both of those are judgments, and the developer is, under the law, the **only** party positioned to make them. When the report is made, it goes to the California Office of Emergency Services within fifteen days, it is exempt from the state's public records act, and it is enforceable only by the Attorney General. There is no private right of action, and in my opinion (since ledgers are a big part of my business), no ledger or record to go back to later to even try and understand a pattern of reporting because it doesn't need to be disclosed.

The EU AI Act goes further on the definition, and it deserves credit for it. Article 3(49) is the only definition, of every statute examined for this piece, that includes the words *"or malfunctioning of an AI system."* It genuinely reaches behavior (and given that behavior is the whole selling point of generative AI, that seems like the place that matters). And under Article 73 the report goes to the market surveillance authority.

So look at where the two reports go. The one American statute written for exactly this kind of event reports to a state emergency agency, behind a public-records exemption. The one statutory definition that says *malfunction* reports to a regulator. Neither of them runs to the business that bought the system and is running it on its customers. Where the law finally addressed the gap, it closed it in the direction of the government and left the customer where the contract had already left them.

For Australian readers there is a small irony in the timing. The exposure draft of the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026, released on 31 August, takes submissions until today, and it hardens the breach notification clock to 72 hours. It does not widen the gate. The trigger is still unauthorized access to, disclosure of, or loss of personal information. A faster clock on the same door that still leaves you outside.

## A sharp reader could push back here

I want to be careful here, because two parts of this argument are, unfortunately, a bit weaker than they look, and I want to make sure I am clear about that.

**The first is that the vendor does not get to decide what counts.** The obvious reading, and the one this piece started from, is that the vendor owns the label: if they say it was not a breach, it was not a breach. While that appears to be true in the case of the California law, at least under New South Wales law, that is wrong. In *Bartlett v ANZ* [2016] NSWCA 30, the Court of Appeal held that a clause turning on a defined event presumptively requires *"the objective existence"* of the event, not simply the party's opinion that it occurred, and that parties who want the opposite *"should be scrutinized carefully"* before a court concludes they got it. Good news for customers, with three qualifications that matter. A vendor can draft around it with words like *"in its sole and absolute discretion,"* which NSW courts have accepted as excluding a duty to act reasonably. The High Court has not endorsed the broader doctrine the NSW cases lean on.

Unfortunately, most enterprise AI contracts in the United States are governed by Delaware or California law, where the Delaware Supreme Court has said the implied covenant *"only applies to developments that could not be anticipated, not developments that the parties simply failed to consider."* Which is, uncomfortably, a one-sentence summary of this whole piece, written against the customer, though in a jurisdiction most of those customers don't reside in and without most of the customers available to object. Asymmetric law being written down and codified doesn't make it good law, it just makes it enforceable.

So the problem is not that the vendor owns the label. It is sharper than that. **Nobody wrote a definition that reaches behavior in the first place, and even if somebody had, the customer has no right to the facts they would need to apply it.** The vendor is the only party who knows what happened inside its own system. An objective test you cannot see the evidence for is not much of a test.

**The second is the SEC.** If you are a US-listed company, Item 1.05 of Form 8-K defines a cybersecurity incident as *"an unauthorized occurrence"* that jeopardizes the confidentiality, integrity or availability of your information systems, and the SEC extended *information systems* to third-party and cloud resources you use, precisely so companies could not contract their way out of disclosure. The adopting release also says an accidental occurrence can be an unauthorized one. An agent quietly corrupting records in a system you rely on is arguably an integrity event under that language. I cannot tell you it is excluded, and I am not going to, but I can't tell you it's *included* either. There are those in the cynical space arguing that part of what the big 3 are lobbying for right now is an exclusion from this kind of language in exchange for a mostly-unspecified regulatory regime (I wrote about that Tuesday, no need to go back now). `[VERIFY -- the lobbying claim and the Tuesday pointer: see header ruling 2]`

What I can tell you is narrower. The definition turns on the word *unauthorized*, and a model doing something its operator did not intend, with permissions its operator granted, is not an obvious fit for that word. Where the model did what it was allowed to do and the damage is in what it produced, *unauthorized* has very little to grip. That is a gray zone, not a gap, and your securities counsel is the right person to live in it, not me. (Still not a lawyer. Still have not played one on TV, and not really on speaking terms with any I would trust to have a viable opinion on this issue.)

## The bounty page says it in plainer language

If the contract language feels abstract, the vendor's own vulnerability program says the same thing in the tone engineers use with each other.

OpenAI's bug bounty policy, last updated on 19 August, tells researchers that *"issues related to the content of model prompts and responses are strictly out of scope for the Security and Safety Bug Bounty programs, and will not be rewarded unless they have an additional directly verifiable security impact on an in-scope service."* And in the scope rules for Codex it lists, as out of scope, *"prompt injection, jailbreaks, hallucinations, misleading model output, or an agent misusing access or valid, applicable approvals it already has, when no separate security boundary in an in-scope OpenAI product or OpenAI-controlled integration is bypassed. This exclusion applies even if the agent leaks sensitive data or performs an unwanted action. Such model-behavior reports may instead be eligible under the separate Safety program."*

I want to be fair to that paragraph, because it is easy to quote half of it and make it sound worse than it is. OpenAI is not saying it ignores an agent that leaks data. It has a separate safety program for exactly those reports, launched in March, and the sentence says so. What the paragraph does is draw a line, precisely and in public: if a security boundary broke, it is a security finding. If the agent used access it already had and the result was a leak, it is a model-behavior finding, and it goes to a different queue, with a different process (and, one imagines, a different level of public acknowledgment, though that isn't clear from the document).

That is a perfectly sensible way to run a bounty program. It is also the entire mechanism of this piece, written down by the vendor itself. **The same leaked data is a security event or a behavior event depending on whether a boundary was crossed on the way out, and your notification clause only knows the first word.** The vendor is not hiding the line. Your contract just never asked which side of it you were buying protection for.

And OpenAI is the better case, not the worse one. A study of 264 AI vendors' disclosure policies, a preprint by researchers Piao, Li and Woods, found that 36% had no vulnerability disclosure channel at all, and only 18% mentioned AI risks.

There is a court decision that shows where this kind of line eventually ends up. In *Moffatt v Air Canada*, the airline's chatbot gave a grieving customer the wrong answer about bereavement fares, and the airline argued it should not be liable for what its chatbot said. The tribunal member's reply is worth reading in full: *"In effect, Air Canada suggests the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions. This is a remarkable submission."* Air Canada lost. It is a small-claims tribunal decision in British Columbia and it binds nobody else. But it shows the life cycle of the argument. It works perfectly well, right up until somebody has to say it out loud to someone who can rule on it.

## The words a government already wrote

Last Friday I told you that the assurance position on model oversight belonged in the contract rather than the risk register. I did not give you the words. Here they are, and the best version on offer was written by the Australian government.

The Digital Transformation Agency's *AI Model Clauses*, version 2.0, published in March 2025 under a Creative Commons licence, are a set of procurement clauses for Commonwealth buyers. They define an **AI Incident** as an event where *"the development, use or malfunction of the AI System directly or indirectly leads to"* a listed set of harms, and an **AI Hazard** as an event where it *"could plausibly lead to an AI Incident"*, which is a near-miss duty with no equivalent in any of the five vendor contracts. Three pieces of it are worth taking to your next renewal.

**Replace the trigger, and leave the clock alone.** The DTA clause requires the seller to *"notify the Buyer immediately"* where an AI Incident has occurred or an AI Hazard exists, and it offers two optional triggers that are the sentences this whole week has been circling: *"the AI System is malfunctioning or producing incorrect or harmful outputs"*, and *"the AI System behaves in a way that was not intended or predicted, which adversely affects outputs or the delivery of a Buyer service."* You do not need a faster clock than forty-eight hours. You need the clock you already have to start on a second kind of event. Put the behavior trigger beside the security trigger, not in place of it.

**Ask for the facts, not the verdict.** After *Bartlett*, the fight is not over who gets to call it an incident. It is over who can see what happened. A trigger that reaches behavior is only useful if the vendor is obliged to give you the underlying facts, the logs, the timeline, what the system did and when, so that you can apply the definition yourself. Without that, a behavior trigger just imports the vendor's judgment by a different route. If you want a template for the harder end of this, the European Commission's model clauses for public buyers go further than the DTA on warranties, requiring the system to achieve *"an appropriate level of accuracy, robustness, safety and cybersecurity"* against metrics set out in an annex. That is exactly the promise every vendor paper above disclaims.

**Price the model change.** The DTA clauses include a requirement for the seller to get written approval from the buyer before changing the approved underlying model, with an exit if the buyer says no, and they give the buyer a way to stop the system immediately (clause 2.5.2). Compare that with a notice to the account email address and five business days to decide whether to leave. You will probably not get a veto. You may well get a notice period, a test window, or the right to stay on the previous model for a quarter, and each of those has a price the vendor can put a number on.

There is a newer layer on top of those clauses, and it is worth being exact about how much of it I can show you. On 20 August the DTA published two full AI contract templates. One, for Software, covers AI installed and run inside a buyer's own environment. The other, for Cloud, covers *"commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) AI systems deployed as cloud service"*, which is how every vendor in this piece sells to you. The agency describes the templates as *"not mandatory but can be used as a starting point for contracting and negotiations,"* and it tells buyers they can add AI model clauses from its clause bank *"if you need further protections."* That last phrase is the one I would most like to test, because it suggests the behavior triggers quoted above may be add-ons to the Cloud template rather than part of its default text. I have not been able to read the template itself, so I am not going to tell you which, but I have been told anecdotally that they are there.

I am no fan of regulation because of the excessive paperwork it can generate; however, I am very much a fan of fairness, and as I said above, I find many of these legal items asymmetric as all get out, so anything which gives a (almost by definition, since the frontiers are some of the biggest companies in the world) smaller entity a fighting chance against a larger one, I am completely in support of. More importantly, assuming those clauses do help provide control, this to me is *exactly* the role government should be playing in all of this -- making sure people have the tools to not get taken advantage of by the unscrupulous.

Two honest limits on all of this. First, these are government procurement documents. The DTA calls its new templates a starting point for negotiation, but for the individual clauses it tells its buyers to keep them in their entirety, to seek their own legal advice if amending, and not to use them as a standalone contract. Lifting the language into a commercial rider is adaptation, and adaptation is a job for your lawyer, not a newsletter. Second, your leverage is proportional to your spend. A customer on a self-serve plan is not rewriting OpenAI's DPA.

## The word upstream of the clock

None of this requires anybody to have behaved badly. The vendors wrote their breach clauses from the best-established template in data protection law, and those clauses do what they say. The disclaimers are ordinary. The bounty policy is clearer than most. The statutes that reached further sent their reports where regulators wanted them. Every document in this piece is sensible on its own. It is only when you stack them that you notice every clock in them starts on a break-in, and not one of them starts on a mistake.

Right now, the argument that a model's mistake is nobody's notifiable event does not have to be made out loud by anyone, because your contract makes it quietly on the vendor's behalf. Air Canada had to say its version to a tribunal, and it did not survive the saying. The question for your next renewal is whether you are going to make anybody say theirs.
