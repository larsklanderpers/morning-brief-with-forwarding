# Signed by You

A performance review is the one document most managers put their name to about another person. A growing share of them did not write the first draft.

An affidavit works on a simple rule. Somebody else can type it, a lawyer can polish it, and the wording can come from a template that has been used a thousand times. None of that matters. What matters is the line at the bottom where you swear that what is above it is true and that you know it to be true. Nobody accepts "my paralegal wrote that part" once the signature is on the page. The signature is not a claim of authorship. It is a claim of knowledge.

A review is not sworn, but the person reading it treats it the same way. They are not asking who typed it. They are asking whether you actually know what you are saying about them.

Last Tuesday was about the people who have not started, and the thirty minutes beside one of them on a task from their own week. This week is about a document a lot of managers have already started using AI on, often without saying so: the review. Same shape as before: what I think is actually going on, what I would do about it, and something you can use today.

## The draft that reads too well

Start with how common this is, because the honest answer is that nobody has measured it very well. A survey Omni Calculator ran in June, 913 working Americans who report to a manager, found 27% saying their manager had used AI to write their performance review or feedback instead of doing it themselves. That is what employees believe, not what managers did. From the other side, a 2025 survey by ZeroBounce (an email company, so this is PR research, and its manager sample was only 250 people) had 41% of managers admitting they had used AI to draft or revise a review. Somewhere between a quarter and two-fifths is a fair reading, and the direction is not in doubt.

And the inputs are not always going where they should. The Predictive Index (which sells manager-development software, so weigh it accordingly) surveyed 399 managers in July, and 44% admitted entering employee names or performance details into public AI tools. That is a privacy problem before it is a quality problem, and I will come back to it.

Now, the quality problem. The risk with an AI-drafted review is not that it reads badly. It is that it reads well. A language model is very good at producing what a review usually sounds like: balanced, specific-sounding, professional, with a strength, a development area and a forward-looking sentence. Give it thin notes and it fills the gaps with things that are plausible for someone in that role. "Consistently goes above and beyond for customers." "Could take more ownership of cross-team dependencies." Each of those is a statement about a real person, and the manager signing it may never have seen either one.

There is no study I could find that measures models inventing specifics in performance reviews. What does exist is close enough to take seriously. A team of computer scientists tested ChatGPT-era models writing reference letters in 2023 and found them describing men as natural leaders and women as well-liked team members, with the bias worse in the parts the model made up. Those are reference letters, and the models are older than what you are using, but the mechanism is the same one: when the model does not know, it reaches for what is typical, and what is typical carries the stereotype with it.

The people on the other end notice more than we would like. In that same Omni survey, 52% said they had received feedback or a decision in the past year that seemed to come from AI, and 68% said AI-written messages from a manager feel less personal. A 2025 study of 1,100 professionals rating a manager's message found that once AI assistance was heavy, readers started to question the sender's sincerity and ability; only 40 to 52% still saw the supervisor as sincere, against 83% when AI use was low. A review is about the worst place to lose that.

So the challenge is not whether to use the tool. It is that the review is the one place where a fluent paragraph you did not check becomes something you said about a person, on the record, with your name under it.

## Where every sentence comes from

Here is the proposal. It does not ask anyone to stop using AI on reviews. It asks the manager to do the one part the tool cannot: know.

**Set the rating before you open the tool.** Decide where the person landed, and why, in a sentence or two of your own. If the draft comes first, the rating tends to follow the draft, and the draft was written by something that has never met them.

**Feed it your notes, not your impressions.** The quality of the review is capped by what you put in. Specific examples with dates beat adjectives every time. And use your company's approved tool, not a public one, with names stripped if you are in any doubt; that 44% figure is a lot of people's performance details sitting somewhere their owners never agreed to.

**Trace every claim, or cut it.** This is the act. Go through the draft sentence by sentence, and for every statement about the person, point to the thing behind it: something you saw, a piece of work, a number, a customer note. If you cannot point to anything, the sentence goes. Not softened. Gone. A review with six true sentences is better than one with twelve plausible ones, and the person reading it can tell the difference faster than you think.

**Ask whether it could be about anyone.** Read each sentence and ask whether it would be equally true of any reasonably competent person in that role. "Is a strong communicator" usually fails. "Rewrote the escalation runbook after the March outage and the next two incidents closed in half the time" does not. The generic sentences are where the model was filling space, and they are also the ones the person will remember as proof you were not paying attention.

**Swap the name and see what moves.** Change the name and pronouns in your notes and generate the draft again. If the strengths drift from "drives results" to "is a pleasure to work with", the tool is bringing something to the review that you did not.

**Read it as them.** Last pass, read it as the person receiving it, on the day they receive it. Anything that would make you wince if they asked "what did you mean by this?" in the meeting needs a better answer or a delete.

On whether to tell people you used it, the evidence cuts both ways. One field study found that AI-generated feedback improved performance, and telling people it came from AI took some of that improvement back. The sincerity research above points the same direction. Against that, people are already guessing, and a manager caught pretending is in a worse spot than one who said "I used a tool to draft this, and I checked every line."

A quick note for readers in Europe or selling into it. The EU AI Act lists AI systems "intended to be used ... to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons" at work as high-risk, and after this summer's amendment those obligations apply from 2 December 2027. A manager pasting notes into a general-purpose assistant is probably not what that line is aimed at; a dedicated tool that scores performance probably is. For my American friends, New York City's AI hiring law covers screening for hiring and promotion, not reviews. In neither case is the law the reason to do this. The person reading the review is.

The economics are simple. A good review takes real time to write, and the tool can take a big chunk of that away. Tracing the claims gives some of it back. That is a very good trade for a document that sits in someone's file for years and shapes their next raise.

## Checks for this afternoon

Two to use today, on the next review you write. The first is a claim ledger. Paste in your draft and your notes (names removed if your tool is not company-approved), and ask:

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

Every NONE is a sentence you either back up from memory with something real or take out. Expect more of them than you would like.

The second catches the filler:

> *"Mark every sentence in this review that would be equally true of any competent person in this role. For each one, tell me what kind of specific example would make it about this person instead. Do not invent the example."*

That last instruction matters. Without it, the tool will happily make up an example to fix a generic sentence, which puts you right back where you started.

There is a fuller set at the end, including one for turning your notes into a first draft without the model filling the gaps.

## The part only you can do

None of this makes a review slower in any way that matters. It moves the time from writing to knowing, which is where it should have been all along. The tool is good at the sentences. It has no idea who the person is.

## Addendum: review toolkit

Copy these, change them to fit your team, and treat each one as a draft. Use your company's approved tool, and strip names if you are not sure.

**1. Notes to first draft, with the gaps left open.**

> *"Here are my notes on [role]'s year, and the rating I have already decided: [rating, and one sentence on why]. Write a first draft of the review using ONLY what is in my notes. Wherever a normal review would say something my notes do not support, write [NEED EXAMPLE] instead of filling it in. Keep it under [length]."*

**2. The claim ledger.**

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

**3. The generic-sentence check.**

> *"Mark every sentence in this review that would be equally true of any competent person in this role. For each one, tell me what kind of specific example would make it about this person instead. Do not invent the example."*

**4. The name swap.** Not a prompt, a practice. Change the name and pronouns in your notes, generate the draft again with the same prompt, and put the two side by side. Look at the verbs in the strengths section. If they change, ask yourself why.

**5. Read it as them.**

> *"Read this review as the person receiving it. List the three sentences most likely to make them ask 'what do you mean by that?' in the meeting, and for each, what they would probably think I meant."*

**6. Notes through the year.** Also a practice. Five minutes on a Friday, a line or two per person: what they did, with a date. A review written from fifty dated lines does not need the model to fill anything in, and the claim ledger comes back almost empty.
