Two documents came out of the same company this year. They do not agree with each other.

The first was a press release in the spring, announcing a restructuring. It used the word "efficiencies," it used the phrase "AI-enabled," and it said a number of roles were being eliminated because the work they did was now being done by software. The second was a job posting in the summer, on the company's own careers page, hiring for that work. Same function. Slightly different title. Salary band a little higher, because they had to go get someone back.

Nobody sent out a release for the second document. There is no press cycle for a walk-back. But of the two, the second one is the honest one, because a press release costs nothing to write and a headcount requisition has to survive a finance review. One of those is talk. The other is money leaving the building.

## The declaration and the container

Here is the picture I keep coming back to, and my apologies to anyone who has ever actually worked a port.

Every shipping container that crosses a border carries a declaration. The declaration says what is inside: what goods, what quantity, what value, whose they are. The whole apparatus of customs, of duty, of AML, of everything my industry spends its compliance budget on, rests on the idea that somebody wrote down what was in the box and signed their name under it. And the reason the system works at all is not that declarations are always true. It is that a declaration is *checkable*. You can open the container. You can compare what you find to what was written. The lie has a shape, and the shape is the gap between the two.

"We're using AI" is a container with no declaration on it.

That is the whole argument of this piece, so I will say it plainly before I spend two thousand words on it. An organization that can tell you which specific tasks moved to an agent, and which specific judgment calls stayed with a person, and who signed for each side of that line, is doing the work. An organization that can only tell you it is "using AI" has not told you anything you can check. And a claim you cannot check is not a claim. It is a costume.

## What the numbers actually say, and what they do not

Start with what got announced, because that part is easy to count and easy to over-read.

Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracks announced job cuts in the US. In May, of 97,006 announced cuts, 38,579 of them -- about forty percent -- were attributed to artificial intelligence. That was the third consecutive month AI led every other stated reason. Year to date it is 87,714 against 54,836 for the whole of last year. Those are real numbers and I am not going to soften them.

But be careful about what they measure, because it matters enormously and almost nobody says it out loud. Challenger counts *stated reasons*. The stated reason comes from the employer, in the announcement, written by the same communications function that wrote the press release. It is a superb measure of what companies are saying. It is not a measure of what happened to the work. The gap between those two things is the entire subject of this piece, and my own notes from July already had this flagged: the AI attribution measures the press release, not the P&L.

So then, what does the inside of the container look like?

The most useful answer I have found comes from Orgvue, who surveyed three hundred US HR managers -- and I will be honest about the size and the timing of that in a minute, because it matters -- about what actually drove their AI deployments. Only twenty-six percent said cost saving was the real rationale. Forty-three percent said economic conditions. Thirty-one percent said restructuring. And sixty-nine percent of HR leaders said AI is being used to justify a *broader range of change initiatives* than the ones it is actually driving.

Sit with that last one, because it is the load-bearing finding and it did not make a single headline I saw. More than two thirds of the people closest to these decisions are watching AI get used as a reason-shaped object. Not as a cause. As a justification, applied afterward, to a decision that had already been made for reasons that are older and duller and much harder to put in a press release. Interest rates. A bad quarter. A restructuring that was coming anyway.

Sam Altman, who has rather a lot of incentive to say the opposite, said it about as directly as it can be said: almost every company that does layoffs is blaming AI, whether or not it really is about AI.

And then there is the one that made me sit up, from the same Orgvue work: twenty-three percent based those layoff decisions on general assumptions about AI rather than any role-specific analysis. Nearly a quarter of them never opened the container at all. They did not go task by task. They did not ask which parts of this job an agent can actually carry and which parts are the judgment we are paying for. They had a number to hit and a story available, and the story fit.

That is the thing I want named, and it needs its own word, so let me borrow the one the community has landed on. It is being called AI washing. Two cautions on the term, because it is doing double duty right now. In securities enforcement, "AI washing" means something specific and legally sharp -- overstating your AI capability to investors, which the SEC has been settling cases over since the Delphia and Global Predictions matters. That is not what I mean here. I mean the workforce version: using AI as the publicly acceptable reason for a cut you were making anyway. Same phrase, different crime, and only one of them currently has a regulator attached.

## Then the receipt arrives

Here is where it stops being a communications problem.

On his daily brief last week, Nathaniel Whittemore made the point that AI layoffs increasingly look like a cash grab wearing an efficiency costume, and he put a number next to it: roughly a third of hiring managers who cut a role for AI have already rehired for the same or a similar one.

A third. Already. Not eventually, not in some slow correction over a decade. These cuts are months old.

Now, I promised I would be honest about my numbers, and this is the place to do it, because I am about to argue that you should distrust unverifiable claims and it would be a fine thing indeed if I passed you one on the way past.

That one-in-three figure traces to Robert Half. Their published wording is that more than three in ten US hiring managers who eliminated positions after implementing AI later had to add those roles, or very similar ones, back. It is first-party research and the phrasing lines up exactly with how it is being quoted. It also has no published sample size, no fielding date, and no methodology statement beyond "conducted by an independent research firm." I went looking. It is not in a press release, it is not on the wire, it is on a marketing page. And Robert Half is a staffing company, which is to say that the business they are in is placing the rehire.

I still think it is probably directionally right, because the separate Orgvue work found thirty-two percent of organizations that made redundancies on the cost-saving promise of AI have had to rehire. But those are two different firms measuring two different things, and the fact that they land on similar figures is a coincidence rather than a corroboration. I have watched at least one outlet stack them in the same paragraph as though they confirmed each other. They do not. If I use both, I have to say that.

So: the rehire number is the hook. It is not the argument. The argument is the twenty-three percent who never did the analysis, and the sixty-nine percent watching the language get borrowed for other purposes. Those I will stand behind.

The sharpest version of the receipt is not a survey anyway. It is Ford. Bloomberg reported, quoting Charles Poon, the company's vice president of vehicle hardware engineering, that Ford has spent three years rehiring veteran engineers -- something on the order of three hundred and fifty of them -- after concluding it had let its most experienced people go before their knowledge could be used to train the AI systems meant to replace them.

Read the shape of that. The cut destroyed the training data for the automation that justified the cut. That is not a story about AI being oversold. That is a story about an organization that could not name what it had, and so could not tell the difference between a headcount line and thirty years of knowing which failures matter. You cannot put that on a spreadsheet, which is precisely why it went out the door first.

And the cost of it is not theoretical. You pay severance. You pay a recruiter. You pay above the old band, because you are hiring back into a market you just told the world you had automated, and everyone in that market read the press release too. You pay for the ramp. Then you pay the opportunity cost of three years, which in this particular window is the most expensive currency there is.

## Read the posting, not the headline

The good news is that this is diagnosable from the outside, and it takes about twenty minutes.

Go look at what a company is actually hiring for. Not what it announced. What it is paying a recruiter to fill this quarter. A job posting is an expensive, slow, internally-negotiated document that has to get past a hiring manager, a finance approval, and a compensation band. It is the closest thing to a declaration a company produces without meaning to.

And the postings are legible now in a way they were not eighteen months ago. There is a whole cohort of roles that exist to hold the line this piece is about. There is a job whose entire function is to sit between what the business wants and what an agent can be trusted to do, and write that down explicitly. There is one whose function is to ask whether a given AI system is making money or just consuming budget, which is a question nobody had to ask in 2024. There is one whose function is to check the machine's reasoning and document how the thing operates, so that a person can be accountable for an output they did not personally produce.

I spent Thursday on what those roles mean for a career, and I am not going to re-run it. Today they are evidence of something else entirely. Every one of those postings is a company writing down where the line is. You do not create a role to audit a decision boundary unless someone has decided where the boundary sits. You do not hire a person to own the economics of an agent unless you have decided the agent has economics worth owning.

So the diagnostic is a comparison, and you can run it on any company you like, including the one that pays you. Put the announcement next to the requisition. Does the org that told the market AI absorbed a function have anyone on staff whose job is to know where that function's edges are? Or did the work simply get quieter, redistributed onto people who now do it in the gaps between their real jobs, with nobody named, until it fails somewhere expensive?

## Where this argument is weak, and I would rather say so

There are three good objections and I want them on the table, because I would rather hand you the holes than have you find them.

**You cannot read a company's insides from its outsides.** This is the real one. A press release is written by communications people who were not in the decision, and plenty of serious organizations say nothing useful in public while doing perfectly rigorous work behind the door. Equally, a company can produce a beautiful task inventory and still get it catastrophically wrong. Ford almost certainly had detailed plans. Naming is necessary. It is nowhere near sufficient.

The answer is that the declaration is not the press release. The declaration is whether anybody inside can produce the list on demand. That test is internal, and it is available to you about your own employer, today, for free. For companies you do not work at, the careers page is a better instrument than the announcement not because it is more honest in intent but because it is more expensive to fake. Talk is cheap. Headcount is a costly signal. That distinction is doing most of the work in this piece and I would rather you hold me to it than take it on faith.

**Rehiring after layoffs is ordinary.** It is. Companies have always cut too deep and hired back; the general boomerang rate has been studied for years and has nothing to do with AI. If you want to accuse me of finding the business cycle and dressing it up as a morality tale, that is a fair swing. My answer is that I am not claiming rehiring is new. I am claiming that *this* stated reason was uniquely falsifiable. "The market softened" cannot be proven wrong next quarter. "An agent can do this job" can, and a third of the people who said it have already found out.

**Maybe nobody can name it in advance.** The strongest objection, honestly, and it comes from Ethan Mollick's point that a job is a bundle of tasks whose seams only become visible in practice. If that is right, then demanding a task-level inventory up front is demanding a precision that does not exist yet.

I think that is true and I think it strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. You cannot know perfectly in advance. That is exactly why naming has to be a practice instead of a document. The organizations doing this well name it provisionally, ship it, find out where they were wrong, and revise. An org that produced the list once in January and has not touched it since is washing on a longer timescale than the one that never wrote it down. The tell is not the existence of the list. It is the date on the last revision.

## The cinematic version and the actual one

There is a version of all this that plays well on a stage. A function got absorbed. The org chart got flatter. The future arrived, and it arrived here first.

The actual version is a document nobody will ever tweet. It is a list of tasks with a line drawn through the middle of it. On one side, the things an agent now carries, named individually, with someone accountable for checking the output. On the other, the judgment calls that stayed, named just as specifically, with a person's name against each one. A date at the top. A date on the last revision, which should be recent. That is not a vision. It is an inventory, and inventories are boring, and the boringness is the point, because it is the only part of this that is checkable.

Every serious control I have ever trusted looked like that. Not a statement of intent. A list somebody signed.

## The question for Tuesday

So here is the thing to do with this, and it costs you one email.

Ask your own organization for the list. Which tasks moved to agents. Which judgment calls stayed with people. Who signed for each. When it was last revised. You are entitled to ask, whatever seat you are in, and the asking is not hostile -- if the work has been done, someone will be pleased you noticed.

If the list comes back inside a week, you work somewhere that is building, and you should take that seriously as a piece of information about your employer that you did not have on Monday. If it does not come back, or if what comes back is a paragraph about the company's commitment to innovation, then you have your answer too. It does not mean AI is doing the work. It means a decision got made, and AI was handed the microphone afterward.

The container is the same either way. The only difference is whether anyone was willing to write down what was inside it.
