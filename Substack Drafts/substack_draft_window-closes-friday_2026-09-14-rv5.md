A word is a word long before it is in the dictionary. The dictionary is still the thing people reach for when they want to settle whether it is one.

That gap is ordinary and mostly harmless. Nobody is hurt by the two or three years a new word spends in common use before an editor puts it in an edition. But there is a second kind of dictionary, one almost nobody outside a statistical agency has ever opened, and the gap in that one is not harmless at all. It is the official list of jobs. Every government keeps one. Yours decides which occupations exist, in the only sense that institutions recognize: which ones get a code, and therefore which ones get a training pathway, a visa category, a salary benchmark, a projection, and a line in somebody's workforce plan.

This week I want to follow one mechanism through five days, because it turns out to be the same mechanism at five different scales. The mechanism is both straightforward and common: **Somebody or something applied a label, the label decided what happened next, and there is usually no name attached to the labelling.**

Tomorrow we're going to diverge briefly into US politics because the labels matter there, too, and with the mid-terms coming up, there is quite the firestorm around tech, data centers, and AI, much of it being elevated into a conversation about existential risk that seems important enough to be worth at least one byline.

Back to things with a more manageable scope for most of us, on Wednesday that is a severity score no human assigned. Thursday brings us an analysis of a vendor choosing which of two filing cabinets an incident goes in, and determining the responses to that incident by nothing more substantial than how it is named and therefore what it triggers. On Friday, we dig even deeper into that idea of the importance of naming a thing as it relates to business continuity and more, by reviewing a contract clause that never fires because the label upstream of it was never defined, and considering at least as much as I can make sense of which that clause implies (reminder: not a lawyer, and didn't even play one on TV).

Today, however, since we're kicking things off, is the one place in the week where the labelling *itself* has a name on it. It has a named committee, a published notice, a docket number, and a deadline. I am starting here on purpose, because you cannot see what is wrong with the rest of the week until you have seen what the accountable version looks like.

And the accountable version has a problem of its own, which is that it runs on a once-a-decade interval and the work it is designed to describe does not.

## Seven hundred and thirty-nine

Let's start with the number that got me into the formulation of this argument, because as they said in the Sound of Music, the beginning is a very fine place to start.

Over the last year a job title has been going around in certain circles, and it is one I have even mentioned myself a couple of times as part of my shape-of-work series: the AI orchestrator, the person who runs a stable of agents rather than doing the task. There is exactly one labor-market figure behind the new job that is ostensibly in very high demand.

**Lightcast counted 739 AI orchestrator postings in February 2026, up from roughly 53 a year earlier.** Lightcast sells labor-market data, which is worth making a point of, because it is their job to highlight things like this, and the figure was provided to a single trade publication rather than published in any report of its own. It is not independently checkable at this point and may not have ever been. The percentage that travels with it, a headline-friendly 1,294 percent, is what any series does when it starts near zero, but sounds a lot more compelling than "53 turned into 739".

So: 739 postings. A real signal in itself, and yet a rounding error in the purposes to which we have seen it be put since then. Both of those things are true at once (as are so many things that intersect with the media these days), but in this particular case, the *second* one is why the story is not the one that has been commonly written, and rewritten, since February.

Why? Because when you go and look for the orchestrator in the official lists, it is not there. Not in the American one. Not in the Australian one. And the interesting question is not whether it should be. **The interesting question is what would have to happen for it to get there, and how long that takes, and whether anybody can still ask.** The interesting question behind that, at least in the US, as you will see, is "will it even matter by the time the opportunity to add it exists again?"

## A large list, but a quiet one

The American list is the Standard Occupational Classification, and it sits underneath more of your working life than you would guess. Federal statistics use it. So do wage surveys, workforce projections, licensing regimes, and a large amount of the private benchmarking that tells you what to pay somebody. If a job is not in it, the job does not stop existing. It just stops being legible to every system that runs on it. And, in some ways, it stops being a load-bearing category for anyone else after the fact.

Here is the part that surprised me. I assumed, before I went and read the thing, that the AI Orchestrator had no code because the number of job positions it potentially represented was too small. Seven hundred and thirty-nine of anything is not much, especially in a job market of about 160 million. That assumption is wrong, and the committee responsible for the maintenance and management of the Classification list says so in writing:

> *"In evaluating collectability and reportability, however, the SOC Policy Committee does not use a specific employment size cut-off."*

Small and concentrated can qualify. Large and scattered can fail. The actual test is the ninth of the classification's published principles (I will spare you the other eight, both as because they are not directly pertinent but also because, unless you are a labor economist, they might not be particularly consumable):

> *"For a detailed occupation to be included in the SOC, either the Bureau of Labor Statistics or the Census Bureau must be able to collect and report data on that occupation."*

Which I think is a completely fair rule and eminent rule, at least on the face of it, and a rule with a name on it, published in a document anybody can download. In other words, **The orchestrator is absent from the list at this time because the work has not settled into something a survey can ask about in a single question, not because 739 is a small number.** If you have ever tried to write the job description, you already know why. Ask five companies what their AI orchestrator does and you get a prompt engineer, a platform engineer, a program manager and two people whose real job is talking their colleagues down.

## The clock, which sadly makes all the reasonableness seem unreasonable

Now the timing, and this is the beat I would carry out of today into the rest of the week if you anticipate sticking with me.

The 2028 revision of the American list opened with a Federal Register notice on **12 June 2024**, docket BLS-2024-0001-0001. It asked the public what should change. **That comment window closed on 12 August 2024.**

Look at those dates against the only number we have. Lightcast's base year is February 2025. The role reached 739 postings in February 2026. **The one public window in which anybody could ask for this job to be named closed roughly six months before the job measurably existed, and eighteen months before anybody was writing about it.**

Nobody shut a door in anybody's face. The door was open, on a published schedule, in the Federal Register, and the thing walked up late. That alone is not cause for angst.

Fortunately or unfortunately, it gets sharper. The committee's own published request for input lists ten categories of information it wanted from the public, and **artificial intelligence does not appear in any of them.** The nearest it comes is asking whether *"the tools and technologies mentioned in existing SOC occupation definitions"* are up to date. In mid-2024, that was a (mostly?) reasonable thing not to ask about. AI had only really been around since the first release of ChatGPT in 2022, and the broad-spectrum "everyone is suddenly paying attention" did not really start to happen until the first "DeepSeek moment" in 2025. Agentic use cases did not start to become achievable until Q4 of 2025. However, the lack of the asking *then* still reads very differently now.

A second notice is coming, carrying the committee's recommendations to the Office of Management and Budget, and it will bring a second public comment window. Its date is listed as to be determined. The final structure is projected for 2027 and implementation for 2028. And the cadence of this thing, 2010 to 2018 to 2028, means that, realistically, unless the Federal Government goes through a retooling at the same level of the rest of us are staring at, a job title which misses 2028 is looking at the late 2030s before it gets into the register.

**A worker choosing what to retrain into, and a hiring manager trying to get a headcount line approved for a role the finance system has no category for, are both waiting on a book whose next edition lands in 2028 and whose one after that lands around 2038.**

The technology underneath them is on an eighteen-month cycle (if one is being GENEROUS). As I have said before, and my colleague often says, "that is not a scandal and nobody involved is behaving badly."

However, what it is, is two clocks, running on completely different time bases, and they are not close to each other or to the work they are governing and interpreting.

I am lucky enough to work in a small enough business that we can sidestep the job descriptions to some extent if we need to; however, in Australia, you are required to match your job to an Australian Taxation Office occupation code every time you file, and we will get to which list those codes are drawn from, and what has just happened to it, in a minute. The lack of an Orchestrator job is, in such a situation, not a minor thing: these are six figure jobs, generally carrying better compensation than a mid-level or even senior developer or level 3 help desk staffer, and if the code doesn't exist, you can easily end up getting paid less than you deserve because the role you have is not the one you disclose.

## Australia already wrote it down

Here is the turn, and it is the reason this piece is worth a few thousand words on a Monday (are you really that concerned about this? Is this just something to get worked up about?).

While the American list waits for its second notice, the Australian one has been rebuilt from the ground up. **On 6 December 2024, the Australian Bureau of Statistics replaced ANZSCO with OSCA, the Occupation Standard Classification for Australia.** If you work in Australia and you have been saying ANZSCO recently (or your software uses the ANZSCO codes), which ours does, the label has been superseded for the better part of two years (and the categories that go along with it).

And OSCA, at less than two years old, is already being updated. The Bureau ran a public round from **11 March to 24 April 2026**, asking which occupations *"have evolved, changed or are not currently separately identified."* It received **265 submissions**, from government, business, skills councils, industry bodies, unions, academics and individuals.

Out of that came a draft carrying **31 new detailed occupations**. One of them is an artificial intelligence engineer. Another is a site reliability engineer, a title that spent roughly fifteen years as an internal Google word before anybody official wrote it down.

**Unfortunately, there is still no orchestration role in the draft.** There is no agent management role and no prompt engineer role (though we have already seen the latter be replaced by a context engineer as the entry to the AI SDLC, for the most part).

However, the draft is open for comment right now. Meaning the Orchestrator can still get in there. The Bureau's language is plain: *"Consultation is now open and will continue through to 18 September 2026."*

That is Friday. Four days after this goes out.

So the same question, put to two governments, comes back with two completely different response shapes, indicative of the structural limitations on this type of ask. **One asked before the job existed and will not ask again this decade. The other asked six months ago, got 265 answers, drafted 31 names, included artificial intelligence engineer, left orchestration out, and is taking arguments about that until Friday afternoon.** I will leave to you to determine which one is more accurate or even "better" if you want to attach that kind of context to it, but suffice to say, the differences are stark.

## The strongest version of the other side

I should put the counterargument up properly, because it is good and I half believe it, and we all know that I love to take both sides of an issue if there is value in doing so.

Maybe the orchestrator should not get a code. Maybe it is a job-ad keyword and not an occupation, a word recruiters reach for because it sounds current, describing work that is really platform engineering, or program management, or the ordinary evolution of a developer's job. In the US, classification principle nine exists precisely to keep that kind of thing out, and a list that admitted every fashionable title would be worse than useless within a decade. The 2010 edition of the American occupational data added 152 new and emerging occupations; not all of those aged well either (you don't really hear about "patient navigators" much, but maybe you do in certain circles; recent changes in the American political environment mean you don't really hear much about "Chief Sustainability Officers" either, that CSO abbreviation seems to be more commonly held for the also-not-yet-fully-listed "Chief Information Security Officer" [also CISO]).

I think that is probably right about the orchestrator specifically and wrong about the general case. **The cybersecurity analyst was a recruiter's word once.** Four cybersecurity occupations went into the 2019 taxonomy, years after the work was real, and the interval between the work existing and the name existing was an interval in which nobody could benchmark a salary, build a degree, or write a workforce plan for any of it. We have all lived through that one. The lesson I take is not that every new title deserves a code. It is that the argument about which ones do is worth having in the room where it is being had, rather than afterward, and the other lesson I take is that the time of new job descriptions in the tech space, in particular, is very short, unlike more traditional job descriptions (a farmer is still a farmer, regardless of the ten year interval).

## What to actually do, and it takes about ten minutes

**If you are in Australia, or you employ anybody there, the Bureau is taking submissions until Friday 18 September.** This is the whole point of the piece, so let me be concrete rather than inspirational. The consultation is on the draft OSCA update and it is run through the Bureau's consultation hub. You do not need standing, an institution or a submission template. The 265 responses to the earlier round included individuals.

**Say what the work is, not what the title is.** The committee decides on work performed. A submission that says "please add AI orchestrator" is the weakest possible version, because it argues about a name. A submission that describes the duties, says how they differ from the nearest existing occupation, names the tools, and says roughly how many people in your organization do it, is arguing on the criteria the classification actually uses. If you think the work is genuinely platform engineering under a new name, **say that instead** and say why. A well-argued no is worth as much to them as a yes, and there are fewer of those. A well-organized commentary addition might look something like this:

> *"We are asking that AI orchestration be separately identified. The work is the design and running of multi-agent workflows: deciding which steps go to an autonomous agent and which stay with a person, sequencing those steps, setting the guardrails for security, compliance and accuracy, monitoring outputs and correcting them when they are wrong, handling escalation back to a human, and keeping a record of what has already been done so that two agents do not do the same work twice. It is not software engineering, because nothing is being built: no model is trained and no application is shipped. It is not ICT project management, because there are no direct reports and no schedule to run; the thing being coordinated is non-human, cannot account for the people working alongside it, and cannot be held responsible for an outcome, which is why accountability stays with the orchestrator. It is not ICT business analysis, because the output is a running system rather than a requirement. The tools are large language models, agent and workflow orchestration platforms, APIs, data pipelines and robotic process automation, with the work sitting across all of them rather than inside any one. In our own organization 4 people do this, out of 12."*

**If you are in the United States, you do not have a window open today,** and as unfortunate as that is, there isn't much to be done about it. The second Federal Register notice has not published and carries no date. What you can do is watch docket BLS-2024-0001-0001 on regulations.gov (hey! you can set a Grok Bot to do that once a week), because when that notice lands it will carry the last public comment window before the 2028 structure is fixed, and on the observed cadence the one after it is a decade out. That is a thinner instruction than I would like. It is also the honest one.

Should you do that? Should you really care? Maybe not. But maybe I should, or maybe anyone who manages tech specialists should, because it does have a lot of meaning. ## Two clocks, redux

The orchestrator may well not deserve a code, and I am not particularly campaigning for it to have one. However, what I cannot get past is that the argument about it is being had almost entirely in venture firm memos, vendor blog posts and conference panels, where it settles nothing and commits nobody, while the actual proceeding that decides the question runs quietly in two government consultations that, at least as far as I can tell, very few people (if any) who are in a position to know the actual ground truth, truly attend. One of those consultations closed before most of us, inside or outside the industry, had heard of the job. The other closes on Friday and has 265 submissions in it, which in a country of that size is a small room, and a small room is the easiest kind to be heard in.

The rest of this week is about labels with nobody's name attached: a severity score that no person assigned, a taxonomy fork chosen in private, a contract trigger that never fires because the word upstream of it was never defined. Today is the opposite case, and it is the one worth holding the others against. **The naming here is done by a committee you can identify, under principles you can read, through a process you can join, on a schedule published years in advance.**

Exactly *everything* that is wrong with it is wrong in the open, which in addition to being virtually unheard of in governments, is itself the only kind of wrong that anybody outside the room can fix. The deadline is Friday, the form is free, and the argument about what your job is called is currently being had by 265 people who are not you.
