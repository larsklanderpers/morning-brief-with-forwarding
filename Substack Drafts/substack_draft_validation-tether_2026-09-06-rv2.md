Six days, one argument. I have been handing it to you in pieces because these things are long enough as it is, trying to get through all of that in one sitting would make it less fun and more challenge. Today, however, as is my wont, I want to take all of that and then say it in one place at one time, in case you didn't feel like assembling it from the parts this week. 

Yesterday ended on a sentence I asked you to hold: nobody is going to write cognition into your job description, because it was implied by your sitting in the chair -- except not any more. Today is why that last clause is true, who worked it out before I did (lots of people!), and what it has to do with a board that cannot read the AI risk it discloses, a control that gets scored but never caught, a recording that teaches everything except the pause, a severity score that stopped arriving in April, and an invoice for a decision nobody wants to own.

Those are not five topics. They are one person, photographed from five sides. Every day this week landed on something only a human being can do, and every day the same human being turned out to be missing, or unpaid for, or quietly being trained out of the building. That was not a coincidence of the week's material. It was the *point* of the week's material.

## The person the week kept finding

Let me name the six, briefly, because the pattern is the point of most of my arguments, but especially this week. Each piece is still available should you want the underlying detail.

Monday: 83% of the S&P 500 discloses AI as a material risk, and 2.7% of their directors disclose any expertise in it. Every governance framework in circulation terminates in that room, and treats the expertise in it as already there.

Tuesday: every delegation framework scores the task, and none of them scores whether you would catch the task coming back wrong. The catch is the part that decides whether the delegation was safe, and it is the part that moves.

Wednesday: a recording of a control function transmits every step of the process and nothing of the pause, the half-second where somebody looks twice at a line that balances. The process gets copied. The judgment stays in one head, and how long to keep paying for that head is a question most organizations have not yet asked out loud.

Thursday: on 15 April, NIST stopped routinely supplying the severity score that every patch policy in the world silently depends on. The word "critical" did not vanish. It moved in-house, to a judgment your organization now has to make, whether or not anyone there was ever trained to make it.

Friday: 26% of the the most commonly-used patching list on earth completed, down from 38%, across thirteen thousand organizations, and the constraint was never money. It was change control, asset ownership, and a decision nobody wanted to be the one to make.

Saturday: with the tool you get better on the day; without it, after months of use, you may be worse than you were when you started, and that second number goes unmeasured because nobody takes the tool away on purpose to find out.

Read those six as a row and the subject repeats. Tell whether it is right. Catch it. Notice the pause. Assign the severity. Own the decision. Keep the capacity. Six verbs, one noun, and the noun is a person with independent judgment in the domain -- which is precisely the thing that none of the frameworks, the vendors, the recordings, or the scoring sheets can supply, and precisely the thing that a great many organizations are, this year, cutting the cost of producing, for reasons that look perfectly sensible from inside each one of them.

## Credit where it is due, and the hedge that comes with it

I did not find the shape of this week on my own. In July, *Human Resource Development Review* -- a peer-reviewed journal in the HR field, not the security field, which is part of why I think it has been missed -- published online a paper by Nolan Lovett called *The Tragedy of the Cognitive Commons: How AI Could Disrupt the Regeneration of Professional Expertise.* Lovett is at NATO Special Operations University in Belgium and is concurrently a doctoral candidate at Old Dominion University, and the paper carries the standard disclaimer that the views are his and not NATO's or the US government's. I am laboring the attribution on purpose. This is one scholar's conceptual paper, peer-reviewed. It is not "NATO research," and it is not a study, because it reports no new data. It takes four literatures that do not usually talk to each other and derives a structure from them. The structure is what I have been leaning on all week, and it deserves his name on it.

Two of his constructs do the work.

The first is what he calls the **Validation Tether**, and his one-sentence version of it is the best -- and simultaneously most concerning -- sentence I read all month: *"effective AI oversight depends on the expertise AI adoption may undermine."* He splits oversight in two. **Surface validation** is checking that an output is coherent, properly formatted, plausible, not obviously broken -- and this, he says, *"can develop through training and experience with AI systems themselves."* You get better at it by using the tool. **Substantive validation** is recognizing that a plausible output is wrong in a way only the domain reveals: the risk assessment that is mathematically correct and built on a flawed assumption, the diagnosis that matches the symptoms and ignores the patient, the generated code that will fall over under a load pattern the model has never seen. That capability, in his words, *"fundamentally depends on the internalized domain knowledge, robust mental models, and tacit understanding"* that you only get from having done the work yourself, for a long time, with your own name on it.

And here is the property that makes it this week's argument rather than a tidy taxonomy. The degradation of substantive validation, he writes, *"is invisible to performance metrics that track output volume rather than output validity."* That is yesterday's second number in a different accent. You can watch surface validation improve every quarter on the dashboards, while the thing the dashboards cannot see gets less practice every quarter, and nothing in the reporting will tell you which of the two you are looking at.

The second construct is the **Cognitive Commons**: *"the collective pool of deep human expertise within a profession."* Not your company's expertise. The profession's. The pool of people who can do the second kind of validation in accounting, in security, in medicine, in software, from which every organization in that field hires when it needs one -- and to which, until about three years ago, every organization in that field also contributed, without meaning to.

That last clause is, really, his whole argument, and its both self-evident once you wrangle it, and subtle at the same time, so let me take it slowly.

## The governance nobody designed

Picture a house being renovated. Somebody takes out a wall on the ground floor because the room is better without it, and the floor above does not fall down. It does not fall that day, or that year. It sags, slowly, by an amount inside the tolerance of anybody's eye, and then one winter a door upstairs stops closing and the argument about whose fault it is has no good answer, because the wall was never on the plans as structural. It was holding the house up by accident. Nobody built it for that. It just happened to be where the load went. We've talked about this before, with more or less this same example, and once again, despite the complete overuse of the phrase "load-bearing" in the AI-assisted writing space, it is absolutely the case here and it absolutely matters to the point, because the reptile brain inside us all struggles with that kind of distance -- a change made today that we don't feel for years -- and when the space is moving as fast as this one is, it becomes even more evident that the pace of change is outstripping the human capacity to keep up.

That is Lovett's sharpest observation, and it is the one I have stolen for myself and would suggest you do as well. The system that has trained every senior professional you have ever hired was never a governance mechanism. It was, in his phrase, *"the product of accidental alignment."* Organizations *"maintained developmental pipelines not because they recognized any stewardship obligation to the profession, but because they needed entry-level workers to perform entry-level work."* And then the line that belongs on a slide at every board meeting where headcount gets discussed this year: *"The operational necessity of junior labor was the hidden governance mechanism."*

Nobody funded the training of the profession. Nobody decided to. The profession got trained because the reconciliation had to be done by somebody, and the somebody was junior, and doing it for four years, badly and then less badly, is how the somebody became senior. The commons regenerated, in his words, *"as a side effect of normal business operations, not as the product of collective will or institutional design."* AI removed the necessity, which was the only reason the wall was there, and the governance went out with the wall, because nobody knew it was governance. *Nobody knew it was governance*, we all just took it for granted as the shape of work. And while that may not be the point of today's piece, I think the last 90 days of writing this newsletter has very clearly established, at least for me, that taking it for granted is the norm and that what AI is really doing for so many things, in some many companies, is highlighting how the shape of work has been so very different than the shared stories we have told ourselves -- and, as with most things, not generally for the better.

Now the arithmetic that turns it from a complaint into a tragedy in the technical sense, the one Garrett Hardin used in 1968 about shared pasture (Lovett is explicit that he is borrowing Hardin's structure and not his fatalism, and he leans on Elinor Ostrom's later work showing that communities do sometimes manage to sustain a commons; hold that thought for the end). Each organization that removes an entry-level role, he writes, *"captures 100% of the efficiency gains"* -- the salary, the training cost, the faster task -- *"while distributing the expertise depletion cost across all organizations that depend on profession-wide expert availability."* You keep the whole saving. Everybody who hires from the same pool, you included, shares the loss, in a slice too thin to see. *"The rational calculation favors appropriation over contribution."* And the organization that does keep its pipeline, that keeps paying juniors to do work the tool does faster, is at a disadvantage against the one down the road that hires trained people from the shrinking pool instead.

I want to be careful about the villain here, because there is not really a clear one, and that makes this different from most of what I write. The CFO who removes the analyst role is not stupid and is not wrong on the numbers she can see. The security lead who stops hiring a junior to triage tickets, because the triage is now a pipeline, is making a defensible call. Lovett has a name for the thing they are collectively failing to buy. He calls it the Human Reserve Paradox: every organization needs expertise held in reserve *"for validation, crisis management, and novel situations that exceed AI capabilities,"* and no organization has *"sufficient economic incentive to maintain that reserve when costs fall on them individually while benefits distribute across the ecosystem."* Like insurance, he says, its worth shows up only when it is needed. Unlike insurance, *"no market mechanism exists to internalize commons maintenance costs across beneficiaries."* There is no premium you can pay. Nobody sells this policy, because nobody can.

I think this issue probably goes even a bit further, and you start to get closer to a villain, at least as it relates to tech and software organizations: for big companies, that provided this incubator methodology, a lot of their bleed over time was to smaller companies hiring away people as they started making that transition between junior and mid-level, and also was to startups, where the really talented could cut their teeth on a large organization's dime and then go off and start something competitive once they had learned more of the ground work. I will leave for someone else to take up the question of corporate vs. individual loyalty, and all the other human factor elements that go here, and just say that, just like the big companies contributed more to the commons [simply because they had more employees and thus more impact], their relentless pursuit of savings and the elimination of the people who formed that commons, has carried a highly disproportionate effect throughout the rest of the job market. There is a reason that the large numbers of tech layoffs (more in the first half of this year than all of last year) get a lot of press, even if they are small numbers compared to other industries, and its not just because people enjoy seeing the high-flyers brought low; its because, for many people, it feels like the canary in the coal mine of where the mistakes are going to come going forward. 

## Why the stock looks fine

The reason nobody will act in time is the same reason the floor upstairs does not fall on the day the wall comes out.

Lovett's arithmetic here is the least dramatic paragraph in the paper and the one I keep going back to: *"If organizations eliminate entry-level positions beginning in 2023, the labor market for experienced workers appears healthy because it reflects developmental investments from 2003-2020. The impact of foregone developmental investments will not manifest in experienced worker availability until 2030-2045, long after the organizational decisions that caused the shortage."* And I will add my own thought here: when it becomes evident, it will be way too late to fix it, because a decade of training that takes time will be missing, and you won't be able to fill the gap in a few months.

The stock of experts looks fine because the stock was built between 2003 and 2020. The flow that built it has been narrowing since 2023, and a narrowed flow does not change the stock for years. Meanwhile, in his words, *"existing experts continue validating AI outputs and managing exceptions competently, creating an impression of stability while the regeneration mechanism experiences disruption."* Which is every number this week, once more. A healthy-looking stock (the board exists, the control passes, the recording runs, the policy says thirty days, the doctor with the tool scores 85%) sitting on top of a flow that has quietly stopped (2.7% of directors, the catch no framework scores, the pause no recording captures, the severity no public body supplies any more, the second number that only gets measured when somebody takes the tool away).

And here is the part that should worry anybody who reads labor statistics for reassurance (is there really anyone who does that? not sure, but it makes for a good intro). Lovett describes two mechanisms. The first is the obvious one: the tool does the entry-level task, and the role goes. That one shows up in employment data, and it is the one every headline about early-career hiring is about. The second is the quiet one. He calls it augmentation without internalization: the role survives, the person gets hired, and the job stops working as training. *"Those who are hired develop fluency in AI orchestration while skipping the cognitive struggle through which Internalized Mastery forms."* His warning about it is exact: *"employment numbers may appear healthy while regeneration quality silently degrades."* You can have a full team of junior analysts and no pipeline, and no statistic anybody publishes will tell you the difference. Similarly, you can even have a full team of junior analysts who all seem fine, until the day comes to explain how they reached the conclusions they did, and the only answer they have is the Little Britain style "computer says no."

Thursday is the cleanest example of the tether pulling tight in real time, so let me put it back on the table for one paragraph. The severity score did not disappear in April. The function moved, from a public body supplying a number to a judgment inside your organization. That is a substantive-validation job by definition: deciding whether a vulnerability is critical *for you* is exactly the wrong-in-a-way-only-the-domain-reveals problem. And it landed on organizations at the precise moment the pathway that produces people able to do it -- the junior who spent three years reading advisories and being wrong about them under supervision -- is the pathway being closed as a cost saving. Friday's 26% is what that looks like once somebody measures it. Not a tooling failure. A judgment-and-ownership failure, in a population where the judgment used to be supplied free and the ownership used to be learned on somebody else's payroll.

## What the paper does not say, and what I will not say either, despite the very strong temptation to do so

I owe you the other half, because the paper is careful and I would be misrepresenting it if I were not.

Lovett's title says *could* disrupt, and he means it. The dissociation between how well you perform with the tool and how well you can perform without it is, he says, *"empirically established"* -- that is Saturday's Polish endoscopists and the rest of yesterday's set. But the profession-level depletion, the actual tragedy of the commons, is *"a structural prediction from that dissociation and commons theory, not an observed outcome,"* offered *"as a falsifiable account of where current incentives lead rather than a claim that the tragedy has already arrived."* In his own assessment, *"the Cognitive Commons remains healthy and functional across most professional domains today."* The data span less than three years of widespread adoption. This is a prediction about where the incentives point, made by somebody who did the work of stating exactly which parts are measured and which are extrapolated. Anybody telling you that AI *is* hollowing out professional expertise, as a settled fact, is telling you more than the best paper on the subject is willing to.

And he prints his own counter-evidence, so I will too. Humlum and Vestergaard found null effects on earnings and recorded hours among workers in Denmark across the first two years of generative AI adoption. Manning and Aguirre document adaptive capacity. Hyman and colleagues find considerable heterogeneity in how retrainable different occupations are. And Everett and colleagues -- the same seventy-physician trial I gave you yesterday, where the doctors handed the answer first did best -- found AI workflows that improved diagnostic accuracy rather than degrading it. Lovett's conclusion from the four: *"the depletion mechanism is not universal."* And his real thesis, which is more useful than the one in his title, is this: *"the question for HRD is not whether AI weakens expertise but under what design conditions it builds or erodes it."* Not to stay I told you so, but this is exactly the argument we have made multiple times in the past: cognition only remains when you protect it, and you should be working to ensure that your interactions with AI, and if you are a manager, the interactions of the people who work with you, and built with intent to protect that cognition because it will only get more valuable, not less, as we go.

That is a question a manager can answer this quarter. It is the reason this week had a Saturday, and it is the Ostrom half of the paper: a commons is not doomed, it is merely unowned, and unowned things get looked after by whoever decides to.

## The org chart and the year

So here is the capstone, said plainly, as I promised at the top.

This week was not about boards, or delegation frameworks, or screen recordings, or CVSS scores, or patch invoices, or colonoscopies. It was about one person: the human being who can look at a plausible output in their own domain and tell that it is wrong. That person is structurally necessary. Every framework this week assumed them, every regulator's control terminates in them, every vendor's productivity number silently relies on them being in the room to catch the miss. And that same person is structurally unfunded, because the mechanism that used to produce them was never a budget line. It was a wall nobody knew was holding the floor up, and the renovation is going well.

I am not going to give you a rule today, because rules are what the last two Sundays gave you, and because the paper's own honesty is that the outcome depends on design conditions that have not yet been tested directly. I am going to give you a walk instead, and it takes about as long as a coffee.

**Go down your own org chart.** Not the whole company. Your function, the one whose outputs carry your name. Find the person who, in five years, will be the one able to look at a machine-generated answer in your domain and say no -- and be right, and be listened to. Put a name on them. If you cannot, that is finding number one, and it is not a small one.

**Then ask what that person is doing this year that will make them able to.** Not what training they are booked on. What work they are doing, with their own hands, under supervision, that they will be wrong about and corrected on, often enough that in five years the being-wrong has turned into the thing that lets them catch it. If the answer is that they are reviewing outputs from a tool that does the work, go back to Wednesday and ask which half of the job that is teaching them.

If the honest answer is "nothing, because we removed the job that used to do it," then you have not found a problem with your people. You have found the wall, and you have found that it is already out. The floor is fine. It will be fine for years, which is the whole problem, because fine is exactly what the stock looks like after the flow has stopped.

So: who in your building will be able to say no in 2031, and what are they doing this year to learn how?
