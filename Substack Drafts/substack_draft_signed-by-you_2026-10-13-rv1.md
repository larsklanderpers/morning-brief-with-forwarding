# Only What You Saw

*AI can help you write a better performance review than you would write alone. It only works if every sentence in it is testimony, not speculation.*

Most managers write performance reviews from memory, in the week before they are due. Memory makes a poor witness.

In a courtroom, a witness is allowed to tell you what they saw and heard themselves. Ask them what a colleague told them, and the lawyer on the other side is on their feet: hearsay. Ask them what the defendant was probably thinking, and the objection is speculation. Neither rule says the witness is lying. They say the witness is not the source, and testimony is only worth what the source is worth.

A review is not testimony, but the person reading it hears it that way. They are not asking who typed it. They are asking whether you actually saw what you are saying about them.

Last Tuesday was about the people who have not started, and the thirty minutes beside one of them on a task from their own week. This week is about a place where a lot of managers have already started, often without saying so, and where the tool can genuinely make them better at the job: the review. Same shape as before: what I think is actually going on, what I would do about it, and something you can use today. And just to be clear about the boundary with a few weeks back, that Tuesday was about reviewing the work people do with AI; this one is about the review you write about the person.

## Better than you would write it alone

Start with the upside, because it is real. Anyone who has written a review from memory the week it was due knows how it tends to go: the last month gets most of the words, the big project from February gets one line, and the sentences that fill the rest could have been written about half the team. A model given a year of real notes does not have that problem. It will use all of them, organize them, and turn them into something clear and specific faster than most of us can stare at a blank page.

There is even some evidence that it lands. One field study found that feedback generated by AI improved employees' performance, and that telling them it came from AI took some of that improvement back. I will come back to the second half of that.

How many managers are already doing this is harder to pin down. A June survey by Omni Calculator of 913 working Americans found 27% saying their manager had used AI to write their review or feedback; a 2025 survey by ZeroBounce (an email company, so this is PR research, and its manager sample was 250 people) had 41% of managers saying they had used AI to draft or revise one. Somewhere between a quarter and two-fifths is a fair reading. And in a July survey of 399 managers by The Predictive Index (which sells manager-development software, so weigh it accordingly), 44% admitted entering employee names or performance details into public AI tools. That last one is a problem before anything else is, and the fix is simple: use your company's approved tool.

Now, the catch, and it is the same one the courtroom guards against. A language model is very good at producing what a review usually sounds like. Give it thin notes and it fills the space with what is plausible for someone in that role: "Consistently goes above and beyond for customers." "Could take more ownership of cross-team dependencies." That is speculation, delivered in a confident voice, about a real person, and the manager sending it may never have seen either one.

There is no study I could find that measures models inventing specifics in performance reviews. The closest thing is a 2023 study of ChatGPT-era models writing reference letters, which found them describing men as natural leaders and women as well-liked team members, with the bias worse in the parts the model made up. Older models, different document, but the mechanism carries over: when the model does not know, it reaches for what is typical, and what is typical brings the stereotype along with it.

So the question is not whether to use the tool on reviews. It is how to make it testify from what you actually know, and strike what it cannot back up.

## From notes to a review you can stand behind

None of this asks anyone to stop using AI. Most of it makes the AI more useful.

**Keep notes through the year.** Five minutes on a Friday, a line or two per person: what they did, with a date. This is the habit that makes everything else work, and honestly it is a good management habit with or without AI. A model working from fifty dated lines does not need to fill anything in, and the review it drafts is about the person instead of about the role.

**Set the rating before you open the tool.** Decide where the person landed, and why, in a sentence or two of your own. If the draft comes first, the rating tends to drift toward the draft, and the draft was written by something that has never met them.

**Make it show you what it does not know.** Tell the model to use only your notes, and to mark every spot where a normal review would say something your notes do not support. Those markers are the most useful part of the draft. Each one is either a memory you can back up with something real, or a sentence that should not exist.

**Trace every claim, or strike it.** This is the act. Go through the draft sentence by sentence, and for every statement about the person, point to the thing behind it: something you saw, a piece of work, a number, a customer note. If you cannot point to anything, the sentence goes. Not softened. Gone. A review with six true sentences is better than one with twelve plausible ones, and the person reading it can tell the difference faster than you think.

**Swap the name and see what moves.** Change the name and pronouns in your notes and generate the draft again. If the strengths drift from "drives results" to "is a pleasure to work with", the tool is bringing something to the review that you did not. This is one place where AI can make a review fairer than one written alone, because you can actually run the test, which is hard to do on your own instincts.

**Read it as them.** Last pass, read it as the person receiving it, on the day they receive it. Anything that would make you wince if they asked "what did you mean by this?" in the meeting needs a better answer or a delete.

On whether to tell people you used it, the evidence cuts both ways. That same field study found disclosure took back some of the improvement. A 2025 study of 1,100 professionals rating a manager's message found that once AI assistance was heavy, only 40 to 52% still saw the supervisor as sincere, against 83% when AI use was low. Against that, people are already guessing: in the Omni survey, 52% said they had received feedback or a decision in the past year that seemed to come from AI. A manager caught pretending is in a worse spot than one who said "I used a tool to draft this, and I checked every line."

A quick note for readers in Europe or selling into it. The EU AI Act lists AI systems "intended to be used ... to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons" at work as high-risk, and after this summer's amendment those obligations apply from 2 December 2027. A manager pasting notes into a general-purpose assistant is probably not what that line is aimed at; a dedicated tool that scores performance probably is. For my American friends, New York City's AI hiring law covers screening for hiring and promotion, not reviews. In neither case is the law the reason to do this. The person reading the review is.

The economics are simple. A good review takes real time, and most of that time goes into turning scattered memory into clear sentences, which is exactly the part the tool is good at. Tracing the claims gives some of that time back. That is a very good trade for a document that sits in someone's file for years and shapes their next raise.

## Prompts for the next review

Two to use today. The first turns your notes into a draft without letting the model fill the holes:

> *"Here are my notes on this person's year, and the rating I have already decided, with one sentence on why. Write a first draft of the review using ONLY what is in my notes. Wherever a normal review would say something my notes do not support, write [NEED EXAMPLE] instead of filling it in."*

The second is the claim table, for any draft, including one you wrote yourself:

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

Every NONE is a sentence you either back up with something real or take out. Expect more of them than you would like, at least the first time. If you kept the Friday notes, expect far fewer.

There is a fuller set at the end, including the name swap and a check for sentences that could describe anyone.

## Could you say where it came from?

Next Tuesday we start a run, function by function: what changes for support, development, installations, finance, sales and customer care, and the conversation a manager has with each of them. This week is smaller than that. One review, built from what you actually noted, with the tool doing the drafting and you doing the knowing.

That split is the whole trick to managing with these tools, and reviews are just where it matters most. The tool can write the sentences. Only you were there.

## Addendum: review toolkit

Copy these, change them to fit your team, and treat each one as a draft. Use your company's approved tool, and strip names if you are not sure.

**1. Friday notes.** A practice, not a prompt. Five minutes a week, a line or two per person: what they did, with a date. Everything below works better with these.

**2. Notes to first draft, with the holes left open.**

> *"Here are my notes on [role]'s year, and the rating I have already decided: [rating, and one sentence on why]. Write a first draft of the review using ONLY what is in my notes. Wherever a normal review would say something my notes do not support, write [NEED EXAMPLE] instead of filling it in. Keep it under [length]."*

**3. The claim table.**

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

**4. The could-be-anyone check.**

> *"Mark every sentence in this review that would be equally true of any competent person in this role. For each one, tell me what kind of specific example would make it about this person instead. Do not invent the example."*

That last instruction matters. Without it, the tool will happily make up an example to fix a generic sentence, which puts you right back where you started.

**5. The name swap.** Change the name and pronouns in your notes, generate the draft again with the same prompt, and put the two side by side. Look at the verbs in the strengths section. If they change, ask yourself why.

**6. Read it as them.**

> *"Read this review as the person receiving it. List the three sentences most likely to make them ask 'what do you mean by that?' in the meeting, and for each, what they would probably think I meant."*
