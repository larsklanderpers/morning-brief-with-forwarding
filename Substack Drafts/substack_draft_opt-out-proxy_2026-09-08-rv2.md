You have an opt-out. Somebody else decides whether it ever gets used.

I want to be clear at the top that this is not a piece about a company behaving badly. The two instruments I am going to walk you through are both documented, both working exactly the way their documentation says they work, and neither one is hiding. That is what makes them worth your time. The control is real. It is written down. It is simply held by somebody other than the person the data is about, and once you have seen that shape you will start finding it in places you had not thought to look.

The sort last Tuesday was into two columns: the things you would notice coming back wrong, and the handful where the only thing standing between wrong and shipped is you reading it and feeling like it is fine. That sort assumed something today's does not. Before you can ask whether you would catch it, you have to know who is permitted to switch the thing off, and for a good deal of what is running against your data this morning the honest answer is a party you have never spoken to. In other words, there is another player in the mix in front of the actual decisions you can make who, depending on the specific action you are doing, either sits between you and what goes out or *sits between what goes out and the people who receive it*, and regardless of which provenance it holds, it still abides by rules you cannot control.

Two of these shipped within days of each other in the middle of August. One is enterprise and squarely my own turf. The other is a video platform. They are the same shape, and the consumer one is easier to see, which is why it comes second in my explanation, because it is easy enough to see that it provides clarity for the first.

## The record you cannot delete

Anthropic's Compliance API returns the transcripts of sessions your people run in Claude Cowork and Claude Code, on their own machines. Since August the coverage has widened. It now also reaches the Claude Science desktop app and the Claude add-ins for Excel, PowerPoint, Word and Outlook (that coverage is still in beta, unlike Cowork and Code), which is to say it reaches the documents your finance team is drafting in the applications they have always drafted them in, provided they have installed the Claude plug-in for Office. Note that is not "installed and approved the specific outcome" -- the installation *is* the approval.

Let's begin with what it is not, because the obvious reading is wrong. Nothing is installed on the laptop. There is no agent, no endpoint tooling, no inspection of the device at all. Not in any of the use cases that I have so far been able to identify. The documentation is direct about this:

> "For local sessions, Anthropic records each conversation server-side as its requests reach the Claude API; nothing is installed on the device, and nothing is collected beyond the requests the client already sends to the Claude API."

The word local names where the session runs. The capture happens somewhere else, on traffic the client was already sending. That is a quieter mechanism than the one most people picture, and if you have been watching your endpoints for an agent you have been watching the wrong place.

What comes back is the conversation. Prompts, responses, tool calls, and the text of tool results, which means the contents of files the assistant read, in text, along with your project instruction files. Thinking blocks are never included and the system prompt is never returned. Images and PDFs come back as a placeholder. And there is one sentence in there that is more use to a security team than most vendor documentation manages in a page: "Nothing masks URLs, credentials, or personal data in that content, so treat transcripts as sensitive."

An honest limit is stated alongside it, and it should travel wherever the fact travels: "Local session transcripts show what Claude was asked to do and what it returned, not what happened on the device." Activity that never reaches the API is not captured. It is a record of a conversation. So to be clear: anything that happens on the machine, unless it relates to calling tools or something else that the AI does, never gets transmitted. It stays on the local. Anything that ever gets transmitted is fair game to be stored on the other side. I have given you a diagram here in the hopes of making that a bit clearer.

And it is important to recognize -- the rule about local vs. cloud transcripts *is only guaranteed to apply when you are using your own harness.* Reading between the lines, both Cowork and Claude Code pass pretty much anything in their conversational flow to the back-end (though they may optimize for token efficiency), so you give up even the *illusion* of control when using their tools to access their inference unless you have forced an intermediate layer.

Back to the point -- retention is six years from capture by default, or your organization's own conversation retention period where a finite one is set (and where you actually have the rights to set it), and where more than one is configured the shortest applies. There is a wrinkle in that worth carrying: shortening the period takes effect immediately for what can be read back, while lengthening it later does not restore anything that has already expired. The setting ratchets one way. 

Then the part that is pretty critical to understand. 

The endpoints are read-only, and sessions cannot be deleted through them. Sitting inside that is an asymmetry I read twice because I did not believe it the first time. A Cowork session that ran in the cloud is retained for six years *unless a user deletes the session sooner*, and once they do it is gone and it is not recoverable through the API. A session that ran on the person's own machine has no equivalent path. For the Office add-ins the documentation says it outright: **deleting the conversation in the add-in happens only on the client, so the session stays listed until retention removes it.**

You can delete the one that ran in a data center. You cannot delete the one that ran on your own laptop.

Last Tuesday's piece happened to carry the exact inverse of that, in OpenAI's Computer History feature, where the memory files sit on your own filesystem until you delete them. Same category of product, a week apart, opposite arrangement, and no way for a buyer to reason from one to the other. That is the part that should bother you more than either arrangement on its own.

## Where the consent question actually sits

Capture is tied to the Compliance API being enabled for the organization, and it applies while people are signed in with their enterprise account. Enablement happens once, by the primary owner, and it cascades from the parent organization down to every linked one.

I went looking for the user's side of that and want to report what I found precisely, because the loose version is a claim I cannot stand up. Across four of Anthropic's documentation pages -- the session endpoints, the FAQ, the API overview and the integration guidance -- there is nothing about notifying, informing or obtaining consent from the people whose sessions are captured. The same pages carry careful and quite specific guidance on SIEM correlation, on legal hold, and on chain of custody for exported records.

That absence is a real observation and it is a limited one. These are administrator-facing documents. Telling your staff sits with you, under whatever law applies where they work, and a vendor's API reference is not where you would look for it. So the sentence I will stand behind is the narrow one: the documentation describes no user notification, consent or opt-out path. Whether one exists in your building is a question about your building, and you already know whether anybody has asked it. Now, for those of my readers in New South Wales -- and it is New South Wales and the ACT, not Australia, because there is no federal act -- the Workplace Surveillance Act 2005 makes this a real problem if you haven't told your staff. Section 12 says computer surveillance of an employee cannot be carried out unless it follows a written policy the employee was told about in advance, and section 10 wants fourteen days of written notice before it starts. Miss those and you have not merely failed a formality: section 3 defines covert surveillance as surveillance that does not comply with Part 2, and section 19 makes covert surveillance without a magistrate's authority an offense. Section 5 puts an employee at work wherever they are performing work for you, so the personal laptop at the kitchen table is inside the Act rather than outside it. No court has applied any of this to a vendor's compliance API, so treat it as exposure rather than settled breach.

Worth naming the economics, because there are none. No new key, no new scope, no new setting and no client update -- the documentation says exactly that. The capability arrives inside a product the organization has already bought, and one person turns it on. Extending a six-year record across every Word document and Outlook draft your people touch with an assistant costs a toggle. And it happens without any confirmation on the part of any individual at any internal layer within the enterprise acknowledging that the situation has changed.

The HR ramifications here are massive, at least in Australia. In the USA, there is no explicit law about shadowing your employees, so you're unlikely to run into legal complications (depending on what state you are in, and definitely consult your lawyer, because I am not one and you should not take my word for it), but it seems to me that there is an inherent *trust* issue here that is real and substantive. We have built audit tracking and logging into our systems for three decades; and when someone is performing an action in the business's interests, it is critical that there be audit logging, and employees understand that and defend that because it protects them as much as it protects the enterprise. However, when someone is working inside a tool (like Excel) and seeing AI assistance (say, for instance, with searching a list, something I still struggle with after all this time), and they ask the AI for help, and the AI records it, is that information available to my manager during quarterly reviews? If I switch out of my corporate worksheet to work on my home budget during my lunch hour, is all of that information about how much my partner spends on streaming subscriptions now company data that can be used for or against me? In New South Wales that is the Act I just walked you through, and the ACT has a stricter one again in the Workplace Privacy Act 2011, which adds a duty to consult. Most other countries do not have a workplace surveillance statute at all. What they have is a transparency duty sitting inside general data protection law, which arrives at much the same place by a different road: the UK regulator tells employers they must make workers aware of what is being collected and that covert monitoring is unlikely to be justifiable, and Ontario obliges any employer with 25 or more staff to publish a written electronic monitoring policy. But in the USA, I think the trust impact almost exceeds the legal concern elsewhere. Especially after April, when Meta told US employees that an internal tool would capture their mouse movements, clicks, keystrokes and some screenshots on designated work applications in order to train computer-use agents, and that staff could help by just doing their daily work. Reuters had the memo on 21 April. That was work machines rather than personal ones, which is a real difference and I am not going to pretend otherwise. And the number underneath all of this is not the one I expected to find. Employer trust is not collapsing: Edelman has "I trust my employer to do what is right" at 78 percent globally, ahead of government, media, business and NGOs, and up two points on the year. Edelman sells employee communications advisory, so read it knowing who benefits from that finding. But take it at close to face value and it says the employer is the last institution most people still trust, which is exactly why spending that balance on a transcript capture nobody was told about is the expensive move rather than the cheap one.

I will say plainly that I use both Code and Cowork heavily in my day to day, and that this whole outcome concerns me greatly. Lucky for you, there is no AI involved in your *reading* this newsletter, so you don't need to let your latent paranoia bubble up just yet -- at least not about that. 

## Chatting on somebody else's stream

Now the consumer version, which is the same structure with the volume turned up.

In the middle of August, Twitch added a setting called Training for Generative AI. Note the verb, because the tense matters and most of the coverage got it wrong, including a summary that came across my own desk: the toggle is new, the permission in the privacy notice is not. What changed is that a control appeared, not that a practice started.

Twitch defines the scope in its own help article: "Your stream and the stream's chat, your VODs, your Clips, Highlights, and any text or images on your Channel may be used." May be used, throughout. Twitch nowhere says content has been used, and that is not pedantry. A permission and a report are different documents.

Then the sentence that made me want to write this:

> "If you chat on someone else's stream, their opt-out preferences govern if that chat can be used for training."

You typed it. It is your sentence, in your words, under your name. Whether it becomes training data is decided by the person whose channel you were standing in, who has never met you and whose incentives, as Twitch itself describes them in the same paragraph, run toward growth and monetization. Your consent is delegated upward, silently, to somebody with a business reason to leave the switch where it is. *And with no direct obligation to tell you what their settings are.*

That means that **opting out is prospective only.** Twitch is clear that content "will not be used in future training," which leaves any model already trained exactly where it is.

And a documentation detail that I keep turning over. The Privacy Choices page is the page Twitch's own privacy notice points you to when you want to know what choices you have. I read it on Friday. It says "Last modified on 05/17/2023," and it does not contain the word generative, or training, or AI. The most consequential new choice on the platform is absent from the canonical document about your choices, nearly a month after the toggle shipped and considerably longer after the permission did. It is a forwarding address to a dead-letter box. That is the second one this week, and the first was a government's.

I looked for a geographic carve-out, because the obvious question for anyone reading this outside the United States is whether the European or Australian default is different. Twitch documents none. The setting text is identical when the help article is served in British English, the privacy notice never names generative AI training at all, and the notice's own jurisdictions section carries only representative designations and a minimum-age provision. What I can tell you is that Twitch documents no geographic exception. What I cannot tell you is what the settings screen looks like on an account in Dublin, and I am not going to pretend those are the same statement. But I am pretty willing to bet that their lack of disclosure in three of their largest markets likely holds elsewhere.

## The improvement is real

I want to give the other side its full weight, because I think it is stronger than a piece like this usually admits.

Last Wednesday, the complaint was an absence. A vendor shipped a recording feature and documented nothing about retention, training use, or an administrator switch. Both of today's instruments are the opposite of that. Anthropic documents the retention period, the exclusions, the truncation limits, the error semantics and the boundary of what the record can and cannot show, and it tells you in writing to treat the transcripts as sensitive. Twitch tells you, in the help article, that a viewer's chat is governed by the channel owner, which is a fact it had every commercial reason to leave unstated.

The Compliance API in particular is the artifact the last three weeks of this newsletter kept arguing for. If your position is that agents need an audit trail before they should be allowed near anything that matters, this is what that looks like when somebody actually builds it: an endpoint, a scope name, a retention period, and a chain-of-custody procedure. You do not get to demand the governance layer and then be surprised when it is a governance layer. You do, however, get to demand the right to control what governance layer you are subject to (especially at the enterprise level), and this is problematic on that front, to say the least.

Twitch is also honest in a place it did not have to be, in an FAQ answer about why you cannot opt out of every use of AI on the platform:

> "Some of it runs the service itself, the same way our servers and our code do. Some of it keeps the service secure. And some of it, like AutoMod, protects the whole community. Turning those off for one person would weaken safety and security for everyone else on the platform, so they are not individual settings."

That is a better argument than most companies bother to make, and it happens to be true. However, it is likely not the whole truth, since Amazon owns Twitch and Amazon is the beneficiary of the training, and Amazon I genuinely believe could find a way to monetize nail clippings. Regardless, it does not make the paragraph above less correct. As a systems architect and cybersecurity guy, I would absolutely believe that all of this stuff is very much essential for safety and security. 

## Look up who holds yours

The skill for you to build today is a look-up, and it takes about twenty minutes, but it does require some care and attention.

Pick the systems that actually carry something of yours or your people's, and for each one answer a single question in writing: who is the party that can exercise the control, and whether anybody has ever asked them to. The second half is the one that will surprise you.

The answers tend to sort into a small number of shapes, and it is worth seeing them next to each other:

- **Your employer's tenant administrator.** The assistant settings, the compliance toggles, the retention period. One person, cascading down.
- **The platform, by default.** Anything where the control exists but its factory position was chosen by the vendor and nobody in your organization has revisited it.
- **A third party you have no relationship with.** The channel owner, the group admin, the meeting host who turned recording on, the customer whose tenant your contractor account lives in.
- **A party who has never been asked.** The control is documented, it works, and the person who holds it has had no reason to think about it and no way to hear from you.
- **You.** Which does happen, and is the case worth confirming rather than assuming.

Then count how many landed in the middle three. In my (albeit limited, since this is a whole new space) experience it is more than people expect (and that's proven out by 30 years of experience with it in the pre-AI space), and the interesting part is not the total. It is that almost every one of them is a control somebody could pull today, at no cost, and has not, because pulling it was never on their list and there is no mechanism by which your preference would reach them. 

That is the operational version of all this. The gap is rarely a missing capability. The gap is that the capability sits with a party who has no channel to hear from you and no reason to act if they did. Control the channel where you can, and therefore control the gap when you can, and then (hopefully) take back as much control of these items as you can.

## The point

The session that ran in the cloud, you can delete. The session that ran on your own laptop, you cannot. It is documented, it is not hidden, and it is the reverse of what almost everyone would guess.

That is the shape to go looking for. Not the control that is missing, which is easy to spot and satisfying to complain about, but the one that is right there in the documentation, working exactly as described, sitting in the hands of somebody who has never had a reason to touch it -- and whose interests will only ever accidentally align with your own.
