A conductor is the only person on the stage who never plays a note. And every one of them, without exception, came up through the orchestra first. In fact, if you had a conductor who *hadn't* come up through the orchestra first, no one would have much confidence in him -- not the winds, nor the horns, nor the percussion, and certainly and most definitely, not the audience.

And yet, somehow, in much of our society, while we expect this from the conductor and a million other types of jobs, we believe that we don't need those people now in most. That AI will fill the role without having sat in the 2nd seat, wanted the first seat, wanted to write music, wanted to lead the ebb and flow of the music, not just be *in* the music but *of* the music, and that that will be OK. That the results that we will get out of that action will be comparable to that of the person who worked forever to get there, because it's easier to believe that everyone is just legos stackable in different orders.

On Tuesday I made the optimistic case, and I meant it. The gap between the people AI is happening *to* and the people making it work is a skills gap, not a talent gap, and it closes fast when a leader tools people up properly. The happy version of where this all lands is that the human job moves up a floor: less doing, more deciding. You stop being the one who types and become the one who judges, edits, steers. Management becomes the superpower. Everybody gets handed a baton. And everyone feels the satisfaction of knowing that they always had the capacity to be the conductor, they just needed the opportunity to get there.

It is a genuinely good story, and for most of the people reading this it is also true. I want to spend today on the part of it that is not, because there is a person the story quietly leaves out, and if we tell it without them we are going to all get to feel very wise and philosopher-kingish and be very wrong and only be looking at the shadows.

## The convergence is real. Say so first.

We have spent the better part of a year building a vocabulary for this. The archetypes: the Grower, who takes a rough capability and cultivates it into something a team can rely on; the Editor, who does not produce the draft but knows, in their bones, when the draft is wrong; the Risk Steward, who owns the question of what happens when it fails. The shape underneath your job title, once the agents take the volume off your desk. Half that map came out of Boris Cherny's breakdown of the roles that actually appear inside a tool like Claude Code; the other half from the people who write about this work for a living. It has held up remarkably well, because it describes something real: as the machine absorbs the doing, the work that is left is the work that faces people and judgment instead of the keyboard. It is, ironically, the human-facing wheat separated from the agent-facing chaff and it's important that we all remember that the wheat is what makes the bread.

And the market is pricing it, not just describing it. PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer, built on roughly a billion job ads across twenty-seven countries, puts the wage premium for AI skills at 62%, up from 57% the year before. Jobs that ask for those skills are growing 69% against 9% for the market as a whole. If you already have judgment -- if you have been in the orchestra long enough to know what the second violins can and cannot do -- this is arguably the best moment of your career. Your taste finally scales. Do not let anyone talk you out of how good that is. And make sure you go all the way to take advantage of it, because inevitably, these moments do not last.

Now the turn.

## Who the story is actually for

Every one of those archetypes is a promotion, and a promotion assumes a climb already made. The Editor knows the draft is wrong because they wrote ten thousand bad ones. The Grower can tell a promising capability from a dead end because they have nursed both. The Risk Steward has actually carried the risk, once, at two in the morning, and remembers it. Orchestrate what, exactly, if you have never been a player? Edit whom, if you have never been the one turning in the draft? Understand the risk, and the pain of decision making, and the pressure of the wrong choice when all eyes are on you when you have never felt the weight of it?

The convergence is upward. That is the good news, and it is also the catch, because it means the reassurance we have all been handing around is addressed to people who are already up. It lands beautifully for the mid-career professional whose ceiling just lifted. It lands on a twenty-four-year-old trying to *get into* the building as precisely nothing, because you cannot skip to the podium. You cannot jump through the second floor window to get the escalator to the third. Despite the possibilities of the hour, as always seems to be the case, those at the entry are being left with the crumbs of them.

You can see the shape of it on a job board. The role everyone points to as the model of the new work -- the Forward-Deployed Engineer, the person who embeds inside a customer and makes the agents actually run -- has seen postings climb more than 700% year over year, and the labs pay somewhere north of two hundred thousand dollars in base to fill it. That is the Orchestrator made flesh, the archetype arriving with a salary band attached. Look closely at who gets hired into it and it is a senior embed, every time. The market's headline answer to "become the Orchestrator" is a job you cannot hold until you have already built things for a decade. The ladder is real. The bottom of it is just further off the ground than it used to be.

## The rung did not vanish. It got seniorised.

Back in July I wrote a piece called "Nobody Starts Senior," and I want to correct it in public, because the honest data has come in and it is more interesting than what I said.

The doom version -- the entry-level job is disappearing -- is not what is happening, and I am not going to pretend it is. The aggregate holds. The Yale Budget Lab, which has been tracking this since ChatGPT shipped, finds no meaningful break in unemployment for the workers most exposed to AI. Ramp went through more than twenty-one thousand firms and found the *heaviest* AI adopters actually grew their entry-level headcount, not shrank it. So no, the bottom rung has not been sawn off. Anyone selling you that is selling you a fright.

What the same data shows is quieter and, if you are twenty-four, worse. PwC's finding is that entry-level roles most exposed to AI are now seven times more likely to demand the traditionally senior skills -- leadership, judgment, the face-to-face stuff -- that used to take years to earn. Those seniorised entry roles grew 35% since 2019. The ordinary ones, the ones that asked for a warm body and taught you the trade, shrank 10%. Their own Pete Brown put it more plainly than I could: AI is removing the routine work that once acted as an apprenticeship, while demanding judgment and adaptability much earlier in a career.

Read that as a door that did not close but moved. It is still there. It is just up a floor now, past the point a beginner can reach, because the tasks that used to be the ladder -- the document review, the first drafts, the grunt research that was quietly teaching you the whole time -- are exactly the tasks the agent now does. We did not delete the entry job. We deleted the part of it that was the training, and kept the part that requires that self-same training, though it is no longer available.

## "Tool people up" is only half the job

This is where Tuesday and Thursday shake hands and disagree.

Tooling people up properly -- Tuesday's whole prescription -- re-skills the people already inside the building. That is right, and it is necessary, and it is not sufficient, because re-skilling is not the same thing as rebuilding. You can put every current employee through the best agent-fluency program on earth and still be quietly starving the pipeline that produced them, because none of that touches the person who was supposed to be hired onto the bottom rung and now cannot be. Upskill the survivors all you like. If the ladder that made them is gone, you have optimized a generation and orphaned the next one.

I wrote a whole piece a couple of weekends ago about the employer's side of this -- that the cheapest head on the org chart is a junior and the most expensive thing you can lose is a manager who came up through that seat, and that gutting the rung is borrowing against your own future. I am not going to re-run it here. I will just say the obligation has not moved an inch. If you employ people, you owe them real rungs, and every serious look at the numbers says you are better off building them than banking the short-term save. That is on us, the people who do the hiring, and no tool arriving changes it.

But there is a second half to this that genuinely is new, and it is the part I actually want to leave you with.

## The part that is actually new

For most of history, if the ladder in front of you was missing its bottom rungs, your options were to wait for someone to build them or to go into a trade where you could be your own boss with your own hands. You could be a plumber for yourself. You could not, in any practical sense, be a knowledge worker for yourself -- that took compute, capital, a firm's worth of scaffolding, a team to sell and market and support whatever you made. The door to building your own thing was mostly painted on.

That is the thing that changed, and it changed recently. The cost of curiosity has fallen to about zero. You can now find out what you can and cannot do -- really do, with real tools, on a real problem -- in an afternoon, for the price of a subscription, without asking anyone's permission or joining anyone's graduate program. Nate Herk put a free six-hour course online teaching people who have never written a line of code how to drive an agentic coding tool, and tens of thousands of them showed up inside a week. Skip past the get-rich-in-thirty-days packaging that clings to this corner of the internet; the durable thing underneath it is that the gap between "AI is happening to me" and "I am making it work" is now closable in weeks by someone with no credential at all.

Be honest about the money, though, because I always try to be. The cost of *curiosity* is near zero. The cost of *tooling* is not. Finding out is free; the thing you build once you know is not, and the people who lose here are the ones who mistake the first for the second and spend accordingly. Climb it the way the whole week has said to climb everything -- cheapest rung first, on purpose, and only pay for the next rung when the last one has earned it. And if you want the single most transferable skill to practice while you are down there, it is not prompt-whispering. It is writing a brief clear enough that an agent executes it without you hovering. Specification. The market is already quietly paying for that, and a twenty-four-year-old can start practicing it tonight.

## One honest thing before you quit your job

I have to guard this hard, because it is the kind of optimism that curdles into a cop-out if you let it.

"Go build your own ladder" is not a thing an employer gets to say to the kid whose rung they just automated. It is not absolution. The moral weight stays exactly where it was. And it is not the go-it-alone romance either, the one where the scrappy solo operator was always going to be freer outside the system. I do not believe that, and the reason I do not is personal.

I have spent 30 years now, going it alone, in the romantic sense; I have owned or been an owner in four separate small businesses and in every single one, I would like to believe that I made things better for most of the people who worked for me. I have been the boss; I have been steps from the boss. I have been the co-boss. I have been, and remain, the guy starting early every morning and finishing late every night. I never took being the boss as a pass for doing the hard work; rather, I generally interpreted it as my responsibility to do the hardest work, because that's just what you do. That has had various levels of success for me, I will admit, but in that time, I have learned a great many things, and I like to think I am a far better person for it. I have made the hard decisions, and I have made the easy decisions, but I have all too often had to step back a little and lead people to the hard decisions so that they *owned* them, rather than simply being *told* them. Doesn't matter if you are 24 or 54, leading and mentoring is not about being in the front, but rather about who you bring along with you and how good it works out for them, and that is a built skill -- both the mentoring *and the receiving of same.*

Everything I know about telling a real problem from a shiny one, I learned by working for other people, on their problems, when I did not get to pick. Most of what I learned about how to grow and pay attention I learned from the people I respect, or from the mouths of my kids who never felt the need to hold back. Learning to distinguish what must be done, what should be done, and what you want to do from each other, is one of the hardest tasks there is, and many, many people never learn it. But even more than that: the discipline of separating what someone is asking for from what it will actually do is not a thing you teach yourself in a room alone with a model that agrees with you. It is a thing the rung teaches, slowly, by making you do work you would not have chosen. By *letting* you do work that is destined to fail. By celebrating you the first time you get it right, and the tenth time you get it right, and then listening carefully to the ways you have to improve it. But that back and forth, that invaluable experience, presumes a work environment that is all too rare. 

So, then, build-your-own ladder is an option so that no one is ever *trapped* -- so that if the guardrails in a place stop serving what you believe in, walking out is genuinely possible now in a way it was not five years ago. It is a floor under your feet, not a first move. The kid who wants to skip straight to running his own thing usually wants it because he has not yet learned how much the climb was giving him; and with all due respect to the wunderkinds amongst us, it is the kid who learns how to climb who reaches the top [for whatever his definition of top might be], not the one who has decided to go build his own ladder in the wilderness so no one can tell him what to do.

## So, Thursday

The convergence is real, and it is kind to most of us, and that is exactly why it is easy to mistake for a plan. It is not a plan. It is a reassurance addressed to the people who already climbed, and a reassurance is a poor thing to hand a generation still standing on the ground looking up at a rung that moved, and is still moving, and that I have no doubt feels more and more distant with each passing day.

So do both of the honest things. Keep the ladder down -- for the people you employ, whose bottom rung is yours to rebuild, and for the person you might yet decide to become. And where there is no rung at all, remember that for the first time the tools to build your own are sitting right there, cheap enough to start this weekend. Which, as it happens, is exactly what I am going to show you how to do on Saturday.
