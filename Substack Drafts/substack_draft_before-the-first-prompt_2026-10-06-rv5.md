# Before the First Prompt

Close to half of American workers never use AI at work. A lot of them have never been told where to start, or whether they are allowed to.

Most kitchens have an appliance like this. Somebody bought a bread maker, or got one as a wedding present, and it has lived in the cupboard above the fridge ever since. Nobody in the house is against fresh bread. Everybody likes the idea. But the manual opens with a recipe for a loaf nobody in the house eats, the first attempt looks like it will make a mess, and there is a vague sense that whoever owns the kitchen might not love flour all over the counter on a Tuesday night. So the machine stays in the cupboard, and the family keeps buying bread. Nobody decided against it. It just never got a first loaf.

Last Tuesday was about the people on your team who started without you, and the one-on-one over a real piece of work that tells you how they are checking what the tool gives them. This week is the other half I promised: the people who have not started at all. Same shape as last week: what I think is actually going on, what I would do about it, and something you can use today.

## The people still waiting

Start with how many there are, because it is more than most leadership teams assume. Gallup's tracking of US employees, as of May, has 48% saying they never use AI at work, and only 15% using it every day. Same survey: 25% say their company has communicated a clear plan for it, and 20% do not know whether their company has adopted AI at all.

One in five people does not know whether the thing is even switched on where they work. You cannot weigh up a tool you were never told about, let alone reject it.

The reasons people give line up with that. When the Pew Research Center (the nonpartisan survey shop, for those of you who do not live in American polling data) asked workers who had never used an AI chatbot for work why not, the top answer, at 36%, was that there was no use for one in their job. Not interested came in at 22%. Not knowing how was 10%, and their employer not allowing it was 9%. That survey is from late 2024, so treat the levels as dated; the ranking is the part worth keeping. And in the same survey, half of workers said their employer neither encourages nor discourages using it.

Put those two findings next to each other. The most common reason is that people cannot see where it fits in their own job, and the most common employer position is silence.

Now, the evidence does not let me oversell this. Gallup ran a separate study in February, 23,717 employed adults, and looked specifically at people who work somewhere that offers AI tools and still do not use them. Among those non-users, 46% said they would rather keep doing their work the way they do it now, 43% worried about data privacy, security or compliance, 39% did not believe AI could help with the work they do, and 43% said they were ethically opposed to using it. That last group is real, and it is not small. Some of your people have thought about this and decided against it, and they are entitled to that.

So the honest picture is three groups, not one. There are people who cannot see a task, people who have never been told it is allowed (or who have been told something vague enough to sound like a no), and people who object. The first two are by far the easiest to move, and the usual adoption program is aimed at neither of them.

## A nudge beats a newsletter

The usual response to low adoption is more information. An all-hands demo, a newsletter with tips, a slide on how many hours it saves. The evidence on that is not kind.

Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard (two economists who surveyed roughly 100,000 workers in Denmark across eleven occupations) ran exactly that experiment: they told a randomly chosen group of workers how much time ChatGPT could save them on their own tasks. It worked, in the sense that people believed it. It barely changed what they did. In the same study, 43% of workers said they needed training to use it, and 35% said their employer actively restricted it. Neither of those gets fixed by a better slide.

What does move people is closer to a nudge from someone they report to. When Microsoft gave its own engineers GitHub Copilot as part of a randomized rollout, 8.5% of them picked it up in the first two weeks. Then came a couple of follow-up emails, and within two weeks of those, adoption was at 42.5%. At Accenture, the group that got training sessions and encouragement from their managers reached 69.4% adoption, against 24.4% in the group that was simply left alone with access. Those are engineers, about as close to a tool-friendly population as you will find, and most of them did not start until somebody pushed. Even with the push, close to a third never did.

Gallup lands in the same place from the other direction. Among people who work somewhere that offers AI, 78% of those who strongly agree that their manager actively supports using it are frequent users. For everyone else it is 44%. Self-reported, and correlation rather than cause, but it is a big gap, and it lines up with the experiments.

And there is one finding I think every manager should hear. In a study of more than 7,000 workers across 66 companies given Microsoft's Copilot, the company someone worked for explained nearly twice as much of how much they used it as anything about how that person had worked before. Put more plainly: the environment you set matters more than the personality you hired. That is the lever, and it is yours.

Generally, in my experience with this, it's the personal value proposition vs. the time investment involved calculation that wins people over. My partner, whom I am lucky enough to have work with me, has been unconvinced of the utility of AI for her job. The best conversion process I was able to offer her was to show her how to manage a complex series of workbook pages in a spreadsheet, something that would have taken her half a day or more, into about 30 minutes. And then showed her how to have it do it for her every week after that so she didn't need to build it again. She reviews it now for completeness, makes sure it represents what she knows the ground truth to be, and then transmits it to the recipients (about half of the company). Four hours back a week -- the time she saved that she can use for other things -- wasn't that impactful in and of itself. What was was that she *dreaded* putting the thing together. On heavy weeks, it could take a day or even a day and a half to do -- and she was unconvinced, even after several years of doing it, that any of the people who were meant to read it actually *read it.* It was, in her opinion, just about the worst part of her job. Now it's something that she spends not more than 30 minutes on, even in the really heavy weeks, and she can focus her energy on stuff that she knows has impact. That is so valuable, it far exceeds the efficiency quotient of it.

## Sit down with one person

So here is the proposal, and it is deliberately small. It is about thirty minutes per person, and it goes after the first two groups directly. It (hopefully) plays to your strengths as a leader, and it doesn't require, as we know, much technical capacity -- just a willingness to grow.

**Pick one person who has not started.** Not the most senior holdout and not the loudest skeptic. Someone who seems curious but stuck. If you do not know who that is, that is useful information too; it probably means nobody has asked. You should do that first, then.

**Find the task in their week, not yours.** This is the part most programs get backward. The demo is always somebody else's task, usually a marketing email or a summary of a document nobody needed summarized. Ask them what ate the most time last week that they did not enjoy: a status report they assemble from four places, a first draft of a policy, a spreadsheet they reconcile every month, the reply to the same customer question they answer ten times. That is the first loaf. It has to be bread they actually eat.

**Do it together, on the tool, for thirty minutes.** You do not drive. They type, and you sit beside them. Let the tool (and the user) get something wrong, and work out together how you would have caught it. That is the habit you will be checking for in the one-on-ones from last week anyway, so it may as well start on day one, with your first convert.

**Put the permission in writing.** One line, from you, in an email they can point to later. Which tool, which task, what not to put into it, and who owns the call if something goes sideways (you). Half of workers say their employer neither encourages nor discourages it; "I assumed it was fine" is a terrible position for an employee to be in, and "my manager said in writing it was fine for this" is a very comfortable one. If your company has a policy, the note points to it. If it does not, the note is a smaller and faster version of one, and it is worth asking yourself why you are the first to write it.

**Leave the objectors alone.** If someone has decided on ethical grounds that they do not want to use it, the thirty minutes is not for them, and pushing will cost you more trust than it buys. Ask what their concern is, take it seriously, and move on. Some of them are going to be right about things the rest of us find out later.

**Next week, pick someone else.** Like last week's one-on-ones, this works one person at a time, and it compounds. The person you started this week is the one who shows the person next to them next month.

The economics are not complicated. If your company is already paying for seats, every person who has never opened the tool is a line item buying nothing, and the per-seat bill arrives whether anybody uses it or not. Against that, half an hour of a manager's time is about the cheapest intervention available, and on the best evidence we have, it is also one of the few that actually works, consistently and repeatably, in my experience.

## The first task and the note

Two things to use today. The first finds the task. Have the person paste in a rough list of what they did last week (their calendar, their to-do list, or just a few lines from memory), stripping out names, customer details and anything secret before they paste, and ask the tool:

> *"Here is roughly what I did at work last week. If anything that looks like PII or a secret slipped through in this list or the attachments, flag it and do not repeat it. Now, pick the one task out of that list where an AI assistant could save me the most time with the least damage if it gets something wrong. Tell me in two sentences why you picked it, then walk me through how I would start it, step by step. Ask me any questions you need answered first."*

Tip: In many cases, what a knowledge worker will have done last week will be a report in a spreadsheet (or a dozen!), or a document, or a slide presentation -- if you can get some attachments, all the better. The more context, the more the tool can help. But the sentence about the PII becomes even more critical in that case, as you work to minimize the information exhaust in the underlying system.

Beyond the attachments and related context, I want to emphasize that the last line matters more than it looks. A lot of first attempts fail on the blank page, and getting the tool to ask the questions turns the blank page into a conversation.

The second is the permission note -- generated by you and under your aegis. Fill in the brackets and send it:

> *For [the task], you are clear to use [the company-standard tool] starting today. Please do not put [customer details / anything marked confidential / whatever your line is] into it. If it gets something wrong, bring it to me. That is my call to own, not yours.*

That last sentence is the one that does the work. It tells them the risk sits with you. And it gives them, effectively, a free pass to learn without concern of criticism or mockery.

There is a fuller set of prompts at the end, including one for working out a first task for each person on your team before you sit down with any of them.

## Let the program wait

The first two groups of people who have not started need surprisingly little from you: a task they recognize and a sentence that says it is okay. Both of those are things a manager can easily hand over in half an hour. The only thing I would suggest you do beyond that is encourage the result, even if the early attempts are a bit off and you can *see* it. Remember you didn't have much experience with this either not so horribly long ago, and work with your team member from that perspective. Make it exciting, and if at all possible, fun, and not so threatening.

The program can come later, if you still need one. This week it is one person, one task from their own week, and one sentence, and a piece of bread of their preferred type, from you. Easy; and worth the effort.

## Addendum: first-task toolkit

Copy these, change them to fit your team, and treat each one as a draft.

**1. Finding the first task (for the person).** Strip out names, customer details and anything secret before pasting.

> *"Here is roughly what I did at work last week. If anything that looks like PII or a secret slipped through in this list or the attachments, flag it and do not repeat it. Now, pick the one task out of that list where an AI assistant could save me the most time with the least damage if it gets something wrong. Tell me in two sentences why you picked it, then walk me through how I would start it, step by step. Ask me any questions you need answered first."*

**2. "I cannot see where it fits" (for the person who said exactly that).**

> *"I work as a [role]. Most of my day is spent on [three or four things]. For each one, tell me honestly whether you could help with it, and if so, how. Then name one thing on that list you should not be used for, and why. Keep it practical."*

**3. The first session, interview first.**

> *"I need to [the task]. Before you write anything, ask me up to five questions about what a good result looks like, who it is for, and what I already have. Then produce a first draft and point out the two places you are least sure about."*

**4. Planning the thirty minutes (for you, the manager).**

> *"Here are the roles on my team and a typical week for each, with names removed. For each role, suggest one low-risk first task where an AI assistant would save real time, explain why it is low-risk, and list one thing that person should not paste into the tool. Flag any role where you think a first task would be a stretch."*

**5. For the privacy-worried.** Not a prompt, a practice. Before the first session, write down two or three examples of the task with the real names, numbers and customer details swapped for made-up ones, and use those. It lets the person learn the tool without the worry, and it teaches the habit of thinking about what goes in before it goes in.

**6. The permission note.**

> *For [the task], you are clear to use [the company-standard tool] starting today. Please do not put [customer details / anything marked confidential / whatever your line is] into it. If it gets something wrong, bring it to me. That is my call to own, not yours.*
