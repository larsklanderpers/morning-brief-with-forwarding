# Paid by the Action

*The AI is getting cheaper to buy. What it does on your behalf is where the money is being made, and the seller is the one doing the counting.*

Robinhood will sell you the AI at the model's list price. It intends to make its money on what the AI does with your account.

That is not a scandal. It is a pricing decision, and it is the clearest example I have seen of something happening across almost every agent product on the market right now.

Think about a taxi. The driver is paid by the mile, so the driver has a perfectly ordinary reason to prefer the long way round, and most of them take the short way anyway because they are decent people and because you can see the meter ticking. The part we rarely think about is the meter itself. In a lot of cities, someone who does not own the cab tests that meter and puts a seal on it, because everybody understood a long time ago that the person paid by the mile should not also be the person who decides what a mile is.

AI agents are being sold by the mile now. The mile is called an action, or an outcome, or a resolution, or a reviewed file, and in almost every case the company selling the agent is also the one that built the meter.

On Wednesday the question was how far an agent can reach. Yesterday it was where the agent lives and who brings it on. Today it is what the agent is paid on, because that is the term of a delegation that quietly decides which way it leans. My argument is that the meter has moved off the model and onto the action, and that whoever counts the action ends up owning the margin.

## The trade, not the token

Start with Robinhood, because it says the quiet part in its own support pages.

At its HOOD Summit at the end of September, Robinhood announced Robinhood Agents, "a fully embedded AI experience that can analyze the market, build strategies, and trade on your behalf around the clock," across equities, options and crypto, with a model you choose "from several leading AI labs including OpenAI." The in-app version is "coming soon to eligible U.S. customers." (The 150,000 accounts in a lot of the coverage belong to the earlier product Robinhood launched in May, which lets outside agents connect to your account; it is not the same thing.)

Here is how you pay for the AI: "Robinhood charges the model's public token cost, rounded to the nearest cent, after your agent responds." You load a prepaid balance from your buying power, and the tokens come out of it at the price the lab publishes. Whatever Robinhood actually pays the labs, it does not say, so I would not go as far as "at cost". But the shape is clear enough. The AI is priced to be unremarkable.

The money is somewhere else. In the second quarter, Robinhood's transaction-based revenue was $776 million out of $1.31 billion total: options $342 million, event contracts $156 million, equities $129 million, crypto $100 million. In the same release, the company noted that "with the launch of Agentic Trading in May, customers are now able to trade equities, options, and crypto through AI-powered agents." At the summit, Vlad Tenev (Robinhood's co-founder and CEO) said "markets don't work without traders," and the company describes its mission as being "the #1 place for active traders."

So picture the incentive, without imputing anything bad to anyone. A delegate that works around the clock, on a platform paid per trade, with launch pricing on perpetual futures at "one basis point per trade" and "4x intraday margin." Approval of each trade is on by default, which is good, and it "is adjustable at any time," which is the part to watch. Robinhood's description of its scheduled strategies is admirably plain: they "may place, modify, or cancel trades in your account automatically... including while you're asleep."

We have been here before with people. A human broker paid per trade has the same pull toward volume, and we wrote a rule for it a long time ago. FINRA's quantitative suitability rule (FINRA is the US industry regulator for brokers) says a series of trades has to be "not excessive and unsuitable for the customer when taken together," "even if suitable when viewed in isolation," and it tells examiners to look at "the turnover rate, the cost-equity ratio, and the use of in-and-out trading." In plain English: every individual trade can look fine, and the account can still be churned. The rule exists because the pay structure was known to pull that way.

Nothing like that exists for an agent trading a retail account. Robinhood's own disclosure says it does "not control, supervise, monitor, recommend, or audit agents." I could not find an SEC or FINRA statement aimed at retail AI trading agents, and the SEC proposal that came closest (the 2023 rule on predictive data analytics in investor interactions) was withdrawn in June 2025.

## The money that never moved

Now flip it around, because the same mechanism cuts the other way when the agent works for the customer.

For my Australian friends: in the US, the national average rate on an interest-bearing checking account is 0.07%, according to the FDIC's own published rates as of late September. Savings averages 0.37%. Meanwhile some online savings accounts are paying above 4%. And the FDIC's quarterly data has about $4 trillion sitting in domestic deposits that pay no interest at all.

Very few people leave money there because they think 0.07% is a good deal. Most leave it there because moving it is a chore, the bank is the one they have always used, and the difference is easy not to think about. That inertia is a revenue line, and the economists have measured it. Itamar Drechsler, Alexi Savov and Philipp Schnabl, in a widely cited 2021 paper, found that "the deposit franchise gives banks substantial market power over deposits, allowing them to pay deposit rates that are low and insensitive to market interest rates." To be fair to the banks, the same paper says keeping that franchise "requires banks to incur large, interest-insensitive operating costs" (branches, staff, the apps), so it is not pure profit. But it is real money, and it was paid for by customers not shopping.

At the end of September, Torsten Slok (chief economist at Apollo, the investment firm) put a name on what happens when someone else does the shopping: "Muse and similar agentic AI assistants could soon sweep household cash automatically into accounts paying 3.3% to 5.0%, instead of the 0.1% national average on checking accounts." He called it an agentic bank run. The bank analysts are not convinced yet; Bank of America's Ebrahim Poonawala says "the disruption thesis remains conceptual" until deposit costs rise faster than rates and competition can explain. I think he is right about the timing, and I have not seen any data showing that money moving yet. "Could soon" is doing a lot of work in Slok's sentence.

But look at where the margin goes if it does move. It does not simply vanish into the customer's pocket. An agent that sweeps your cash has to sweep it *somewhere*, and the account it picks by default is the new shelf space. The company that owns the agent (Meta, in Muse's case) gets to decide, or sell, what the default looks like. The bank lost the inertia. Whoever owns the defaults inherits it.

## The meter belongs to the seller

Which brings me to the agent products most of my readers actually buy, and where the meter is sitting in plain sight.

Salesforce sells Agentforce at "$2 per conversation," or through Flex Credits, where, in its own May 2025 announcement, "each of these actions consumes just 20 Flex Credits ($0.10 per action)." On the current pricing page, actions run from 20 to 120 credits each, so ten cents is the cheapest action, not the going rate. Microsoft's Copilot Studio sells "25,000 Copilot Credits" for $200 a month, and its documentation prices each thing an agent does: a classic answer is 1 credit, a generative answer 2, an "agent action" 5, grounding on your company's data 10.

Then there are the vendors who charge per outcome, which sounds like the honest version, and in some ways it is. Intercom charges "$0.99 per Fin outcome," and to its credit, "you're only charged for one outcome per conversation, even if Fin takes multiple actions." But look at what counts. A resolution is counted when the customer confirms the answer worked, *or* when the customer "exits the conversation without requesting further assistance (assumed resolution)." The unit has also widened: an outcome now includes a handoff to a human that ends a scripted procedure. Zendesk charges $1.50 to $2.00 per automated resolution, and whether a resolution happened is judged by a language model that checks whether the answer was relevant and satisfactory. Sierra (a well-funded agent startup) charges only "when we complete a task for you," and the tasks it lists include saved cancellations and upsells.

None of those definitions is unreasonable on its face. Every one of them was written by the party that gets paid when the number goes up. At least one Intercom customer has said so in public, complaining on Intercom's own forum that Fin "'assumes' it resolved an issue way too often," and the reply was a promise to pass the feedback along.

CodeRabbit is the example I like best, because it shows the meter at the level of individual files. CodeRabbit is an AI code reviewer (it reads every pull request, the bundle of changes a developer submits for review, and comments on it). It sells seats: $24 per developer per month on its Essentials plan billed annually, $30 month to month, up to $72 or $90 on Advanced. A seat is any Git identity that authors a pull request. Each plan comes with a rate limit, a handful of reviews per developer per hour, and CodeRabbit "counts one review event every time it evaluates a pull request." It "reviews pushes, not commits," which means every new push to an open pull request is another review.

Since April, the reviews that go past that limit can keep going on a usage add-on: "Price per credit $1.00 | Files reviewed per credit 4 | Cost per reviewed file $0.25," billed as "pay-as-you-go usage and added to your monthly invoice." It is opt-in. An admin can set it to automatic, on demand or off, set a monthly cap, and every paid review posts a receipt with the number of files reviewed and the amount charged. Honestly, that is a better-instrumented meter than most. But follow the units: a seat per author, a review per push, a quarter per file past the limit, and the vendor decides what a push, a review and a reviewed file are. I could not find CodeRabbit's documentation saying whether a re-review after a push counts every changed file in the pull request again or only the new ones, and on a busy repository that one definition is most of the bill.

For contrast, GitHub moved Copilot to usage-based billing in June. Its code review now consumes AI credits ("1 AI credit = $0.01 USD," charged per token) plus GitHub Actions minutes, and the model doing the review "is selected automatically and isn't disclosed." So GitHub chooses the unit, and also how many of them each review burns.

The model labs are doing a version of the same thing with subscriptions. OpenAI now sells ChatGPT Pro at $100, $200 and $500 a month, with the top tier at "25 times the ChatGPT Plus allowance." New subscribers on the $200 plan get "a lower usage allowance than previously offered" (TNW reports it went from 20 times Plus to 10 times), which OpenAI ties to "our increasingly efficient models." That may well be true; a smarter model can finish the same job in less allowance. But the unit is "times the Plus allowance," OpenAI defines the Plus allowance, and it just changed what the $200 buys. Neha Kabra (a writer on AI economics) asks the right question about it: "when we say AI is getting cheaper, which cost are we measuring, and what have we assumed stays unchanged?"

To be clear about the other side of the ledger, the cost per task really is falling in a lot of places. Anthropic says its newest mid-tier model "costs up to 30% less per task than its predecessor" at the same token price (an independent benchmark tracker has measured the opposite on its own task mix, which tells you how much the definition of "task" matters). GPT-6.1 Sol costs a fifth of what OpenAI's flagship Astra does. Cheaper is real. It is just not the same thing as cheaper *for you*, and the difference lives in whoever defined the unit.

## Read the meter before the price

None of this means per-action pricing is bad. Paying for work done is often fairer than paying for seats nobody uses, and a vendor that only gets paid when something happens has every reason to make something happen. The issue is the delegation term underneath it. If you hand an agent authority and the seller counts the work, you need your own view of the count.

**Read the unit definition before you read the price.** Every one of these products publishes what counts: a resolution, an outcome, an action, a reviewed file. Put that definition in front of the person signing the contract, and ask what happens to the count in your busiest week, not your average one. An "assumed resolution" in a support queue where customers give up rather than complain is a very different number from a confirmed one.

**Ask who decides it happened, and get the receipt.** If the vendor's own model judges whether the outcome occurred, you want a sample of those judgments every month, and the right to dispute them. CodeRabbit's per-review receipt is the right idea. Most vendors do not offer one unless you ask.

**Set the cap before the first run, not after the first invoice.** Spending caps, approve-on-demand modes and limits on re-reviews exist in most of these products, and they are usually off or unlimited by default. Turn them on while you learn what normal looks like.

**Keep a human approval on anything that moves money.** Robinhood ships per-trade approval switched on. Leave it on. If an agent is going to sweep cash, know which account it sweeps to by default and who chose that account.

**If you sell agents, price on something your customer would recognize.** A resolved ticket the customer confirmed, a defect that did not reach production, a trade the customer approved. The closer your unit sits to the customer's own idea of value, the less your meter needs a seal.

## Whoever writes the definition

The price of the intelligence is falling, and that is good news. The price of the action is the new number, and for most agent products right now, the company paid for the action also writes the definition of one.

We worked this out a long time ago for brokers, for cabs and for anyone else paid by the unit: the count has to be visible to the person paying, and someone other than the seller has to be able to check it. Agents are being handed authority faster than that has been written down, and the cheapest place to write it is in your own contract, before the first action is counted.
