On 19 October, a service outage resulting from a cyberattack stops being the airline's fault. It will not happen in a court or in a headline; it happens in the one place that decides whether you get a hotel room when your flight does not leave.

Anybody who flies more than a couple of times a year knows the moment. The departures board flips to DELAYED, the gate agent picks up the microphone, and the whole gate listens for one word. If the word is *weather*, people sigh, get out their phones and start booking their own rooms. If the word is *crew*, or *maintenance*, or anything that sounds like the airline's own doing, a line forms at the desk (or all the platinum members get on-hold with the desk right away), because everybody has learned (some of us the hard way, at midnight, in an airport with one open restaurant) that the cause of the delay decides who pays for the night.

This has happened to me many times. I think the most memorable was a night spent sleeping on the floor in the Miami airport because they didn't cancel my flight until well after they had canceled about two dozen others (as a function of a rolling industrial action), and so all the rooms within a ten-mile radius were already booked and the expectation was that the flight was going to leave around 5:30 am when it was already nearly midnight. I am not a small man; sleeping on the floor of an airport did not give me pleasant memories of long bus rides with sports teams when I was a teenager; rather, it gave me a panic attack that I wouldn't fall asleep until right before we were meant to leave and then I would fall asleep so hard I would miss the announcements. I don't recommend it.

The cause is a *label*. In the US, it's a carry-over label from deregulation. Regardless of the source, the airline writes the label, not you, and not an independent third party. That label has lots of impact, but now, in addition to all the historic elements, a rule the Department of Transportation published on 3 September adds *cybersecurity attacks* to the list of labels that send the bill to you instead of to the airline.

Monday was about the organization that deploys a model and ends up holding the risk for it. This is the mirror image, and it comes out of my own field: an operator whose systems fail, and a label that moves the cost of that failure off the operator and onto the customer standing at the gate.

## The new column

The rule is short, and most of what it does is bookkeeping. Airlines report to DOT why each flight was late or canceled, and those causes are published, which is how the "on-time" league tables you see in the news get made. Until now there was one category for things the airline controls. From 19 October there is a new one, named for the section of the 2024 FAA reauthorization law that required it, and ten kinds of event move into it:

- cleaning the aircraft after a passenger's death
- aircraft damage from extreme weather, debris or sabotage
- an outage of a baggage system not controlled by the carrier or its contractor
- *"cybersecurity attacks (provided that the air carrier is in compliance with applicable cybersecurity regulations)"*
- an unexpected failure of government systems
- overheated brakes after an emergency
- certain unscheduled maintenance
- a medical emergency that was not the carrier's fault
- removing an unruly passenger
- an airport closed by volcanic ash, wind or wind shear

Most of that list is fair on its face. Nobody thinks an airline should buy you dinner because a volcano erupted (or at least, nobody reasonable).

Now look at what that reporting category is attached to. Every one of the nine large US airlines on DOT's own customer service dashboard commits to a meal voucher when a *controllable* cancellation leaves you waiting three hours or more, and eight of the nine commit to a hotel room and transport to it for an overnight one. Those commitments hang on the word controllable. Move a cause out of the controllable column and the commitment does not apply to it.

DOT does not dress this up. In the rule's own words, the number of delays and cancellations *"for which air carriers provide amenities and compensation to consumers is expected to be reduced."* It went out without a public comment period, which DOT called *"unnecessary"* because Congress wrote the list and the department had no discretion over it. Airlines for America, the industry's trade group, had petitioned DOT to move faster and to go further than the law required. And in the cost-benefit section, the benefit the department names for carriers is fewer delays being blamed on them in public, which it calls *"negative reputational impacts."*

So there are two things riding on the same label. One is your hotel room. The other is the airline's place in the on-time rankings. Both of them now move in the airline's favor whenever the cause is a cyberattack.

## A condition nobody can check

Here is the part I cannot let go of, and it is the reason this is a cyber piece and not a travel piece (which I don't really write, but could be interesting).

The cybersecurity exemption is conditional. The attack only moves out of the airline's column *"provided that the air carrier is in compliance with applicable cybersecurity regulations."* That proviso is doing all the work, and DOT's rule adopts the statute's words *"without further interpretation."* It does not say which regulations apply, or who decides whether the airline was in compliance on the day.

The obvious candidates are TSA's. In March 2023, TSA issued an emergency amendment that wrote cybersecurity into the security programs of the largest US airlines and airports: network segmentation so the operational systems keep running if the IT side is compromised, access controls, continuous monitoring, risk-based patching, and a plan for all of it that TSA approves and can inspect. Those are reasonable requirements, and I have spent a good part of my career trying to get organizations to meet requirements a lot like them.

But look at where they live. TSA's own testimony to Congress describes these as amendments to the carriers' security programs. And under 49 CFR 1520.5, an aircraft operator's security program, *"including any comments, instructions, or implementing guidance,"* is Sensitive Security Information. You are not allowed to read it. Neither is your travel manager, and neither is any journalist.

Put those pieces together. Whether you get a hotel room after a cyberattack depends on whether the airline complied with a security program, and that security program is a document you are legally barred from seeing. The airline assigns the cause. The standard it has to meet to assign that cause is confidential. And the compliance record sits with the airline and with TSA, neither of which has a reason to publish the answer to your question at 11pm at the gate.

I want to be fair about why that document is confidential. There are good reasons not to publish an airline's network segmentation plan, and I would not want it published. My objection is narrower. Somebody chose to hang a consumer entitlement on a condition that the consumer can never test.

## Whose systems count

There is a second gap, and it is in the drafting.

Go back to the baggage line in that list. A baggage system outage only moves out of the airline's column if the system is *"not controlled by a carrier or its contractor."* Somebody thought about vendors there. If your bag belt is run by a contractor and it breaks, the airline still owns it.

The cybersecurity line has no such language. It says *"cybersecurity attacks"*, and it does not say whose.

That is not a hypothetical distinction, and the case that proves it had its first anniversary last weekend. On the night of 19 September 2025, a ransomware attack on Collins Aerospace, the RTX subsidiary whose software runs airline check-in and bag drop at airports in a lot of countries, pushed Heathrow, Brussels and Berlin back to manual check-in. The EU's cybersecurity agency confirmed it was ransomware, and by the Monday Brussels was still canceling flights. That happened in Europe, where a different set of passenger rules applies, and I am using it only as an illustration. The airline was not the one attacked. Its supplier was.

Anyone who has run a third-party risk program will recognize that shape. So the question for 19 October is a simple one that the text does not answer. When the airline's check-in vendor gets hit, is that a cybersecurity attack on the airline, or is it the kind of contractor failure Congress was careful to keep in the airline's column for bags? Whoever answers that question first gets to write the precedent, and the airline is the one reporting the cause.

## Before you repeat any of this

The honest version of this piece has limits, and you should know them before you repeat any of it.

**Refunds are untouched.** DOT's automatic refund rule still applies. If your flight is canceled and you do not take the rebooking or a voucher, you are owed your money back *"regardless of the reason."* What changes is the hotel, the meals and the ride to the hotel, not the ticket.

**DOT did not invent this.** The list is Congress's, from 2024, and the department is right that it had little room to change it. The rule is effectively DOT doing what a two-year-old statute told it to do.

**There is a fair case on the airline's side.** An airline hit by a state-sponsored crew that got past controls it really did have in place is not obviously more at fault than an airline grounded by a volcano. The proviso exists precisely to separate the airline that did the work from the one that did not. I agree with the intent. My objection is that nobody outside the airline and TSA can tell the two apart.

**Nobody knows how often this happens.** As CyberScoop put it, *"there's no formal accounting"* of how often cyberattacks have caused delays or cancellations. That is part of why the new reporting category matters. From October there will finally be a count. It will be a count of what the airlines report.

## Your side of the gate

**Save the plan while the old wording still stands.** DOT's customer service dashboard lists what each large airline commits to for controllable delays and cancellations, and each airline's own plan spells it out. Save a copy of your usual carrier's plan now. If the language changes after 19 October, you will want to know what it said before.

**Budget the night, not the flight.** If you run a corporate travel program, the hotel an airline used to cover is now a line your duty-of-care budget may have to absorb, and a cyber event does not strand one traveler at a time. It strands every one of your people routed through the same hub on the same evening. Check whether your travel insurance or your corporate card's trip-delay cover still pays when the stated cause is a cyberattack, because from October the airline may not.

**Make compliance provable on the worst day.** This one is for my side of the house. The airlines have just been handed a model that more of us are going to see: a cost that moves off your books if, and only if, you were compliant at the moment you were hit. That turns compliance evidence from an audit artifact into a financial instrument. If your organization cannot produce, in the first forty-eight hours of an incident, proof that the relevant controls were in place and working that morning, then a clause like this one will not help you, however good your controls were. Build the evidence trail before you need it.

And without knowing the specifics of how to prove it, what you really need is a live audit running constantly, visible in segmentation by the regulator in the event of a breach. If you roll back, though, we talked last week about how these things become binding, and we have talked numerous times over the last few months about how much what is happening in cybersecurity right now is about compression, and about how all these timelines have moved more and more tightly to the exploit determination itself. So, imagine yourself in, say, United Airlines' shoes, where they have integrated a number of other airlines over the years, all with their own computer systems, and they have the union systems, and the scheduling systems, and any other huge number of support systems necessary when you employ over 110,000 people, and imagine that patch clock shifting to three days, and suddenly that criteria about being compliant has a lot more bite than it initially seemed to.

## The word at the gate

But if you are the one at the gate, your concern is unlikely to be about United's cyber policies and the strength of its IT organization. And from 19 October, a cyberattack on an airline that was *compliant at the time of attack* becomes something the airline does not control. The airline reports the cause, and the standard it has to meet is one the passenger is not allowed to read.

So when the board flips to *delayed* and the agent says the word, it is worth asking the one question the rule leaves open. Compliant with what, exactly?
