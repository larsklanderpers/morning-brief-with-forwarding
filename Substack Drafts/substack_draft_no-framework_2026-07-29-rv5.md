Australia is the heaviest per-capita user of AI on earth. It also does not have an AI law.

Hold those two facts next to each other, because the gap between them is the whole story. Anthropic's own usage index puts Australia first out of 121 countries, running at 6.4 times the rate its population would predict -- up from 4.1x just two months earlier. Not plateauing. Accelerating. And the country doing all that adopting will not have a single operative national framework governing any of it until 2027 at the earliest.

That gap is the reason for this piece -- the Wednesday of a week I'm spending on who controls what. Two weeks ago the Prime Minister stood up in Sydney and announced a fix -- a new "Office of AI," pitched as a "single national framework" and, in his words, a "world first." It made headlines. It made the tech sector nod. And if you read it the way it was written to be read, you would think the vacuum just got filled.

It didn't. What got announced was a front door. A very nice front door, with a brass plate and a ribbon-cutting. Behind it, for now, is a building that hasn't been constructed.

## What was announced, and what actually exists

Let me be precise, because precision is the entire point when a government is selling you a tense.

What exists, as of the speech: an Office of AI, sitting inside the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet, whose job is to coordinate the design of Australian AI standards. It has no statutory powers. It has no announced budget. It has no announced staffing. The Greens' David Shoebridge called it "a single door in his office," and unkind as that is, I have not found a factual error in it.

What does not exist: the framework. The Prime Minister's actual words were that Australia "will be the first country in the world to bring these issues into a single, national framework." Read that again. Watch the verb. Will be. National Cabinet is scheduled to take up data-centre rules next month; legislation is promised for "early next year." Until that legislation passes, nothing new binds anyone. What was delivered is a governance scaffold and a press release. What was promised is a framework, dated 2027.

I want to be fair: a scaffold is not nothing, and coordinating a genuinely fragmented mess of sector rules is real work. But "world first" is doing an enormous amount of lifting for a thing that does not yet operate. The EU has an actual AI Act on the books, with binding obligations already phasing in. Australia's is an intention with a ribbon on it.

## The regime that reversed itself

Here is the part that earns this piece its thread. This is the Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime I have been tracking all month -- the one that fired independent regulators an ocean away in "The Regulators Just Got Fired" three weeks back, and here at home just did the most ad hoc thing I have seen it do yet: it reversed its own reversal.

Rewind to December 2025. The same government looked at the question of a dedicated AI Act and decided against it -- existing frameworks were enough, it said, no need for a standalone law. Seven months later, in July 2026, the same government announces a "world-first single national framework." In eighteen months it has reversed itself on AI so many times you could not draw a straight line through its position with a ruler. This is a regime *so ad hoc it cannot hold a position on whether to be ad hoc*.

That matters to us -- to anyone running something regulated in this country -- because it tells you what kind of ground we're building on. You are not waiting for a stable framework to arrive and then complying with it. You are trying to build a compliance posture on top of a policy that changes direction faster than most procurement cycles complete.

## Electrons before people

Now look at what the new framework reaches for first, because the sequence tells you who it is really for.

The concrete, dated obligations in the announcement are all about data centres. Large AI data centres will have to be net energy generators, underwrite their own new power supply, pay full grid-connection costs, and minimise water use. Those are real requirements with real teeth, and National Cabinet takes them up next month.

Now count the obligations aimed at protecting a person from an automated decision. There is effectively one on the calendar, and it is not new to this announcement: the OAIC's automated-decision-making transparency rule, which lands on 10 December 2026 -- the same clock I started counting in "Your Algorithm Makes Decisions" back in June -- and requires you to disclose in your privacy policy which substantially automated decisions your systems make about people. The right to actually contest one of those decisions -- to stand on the other side of the form, as I put it on 6 July -- is deferred, with no date attached. So you are left explaining yourself to a ghost regulator -- one that does not yet exist, operating inside an ad hoc regulatory regime that is documented nowhere yet -- and justifying your methodology to it in advance. Then, when it disagrees, you have no basis on which to respond, because there is no framework yet in which to litigate or defend anything.

Sit with the ordering. The framework regulates the water and the electrons before it regulates a single decision made about a human being. The heaviest-adopting population on earth gets rules for the buildings the models live in before it gets rules for what the models are allowed to decide about them. And it gets rules to protect the individual that are constructed with so little transparency that even the rule makers can't explain how what is OK and what isn't will be decided.

Here is the line worth drawing before we go any further, because it is the one the ribbon is built to blur. The Framework -- and from here I will give it the capital letters it keeps awarding itself -- was never going to regulate your usage. It is meant to regulate your deployment, and those are not the same thing. The 6.4x is usage: Australians typing into a box, and there is nothing there to govern. What needs governing is what a business does with what comes back -- the inference under the covers, the decision made about a customer who never sees it, the exhaust that quietly tilts toward the house. That is not usage. That is the thing The Framework keeps promising to reach, and keeps dating to 2027.

And the rest of the regime is exactly the piecemeal patchwork the "single framework" language is meant to make you forget. APRA's CPS 230 is in force, and it is worth being precise about it, because it is easy to overclaim. CPS 230 is an operational-resilience standard that names no AI at all. It does not classify your AI vendor as anything. It does not classify ANY AI vendor as anything. What it *does* do is define a material offshoring arrangement by where the service is actually performed, which can reach offshore model inference where the arrangement is material -- but that is a determination you make and defend, not a label the regulator hands you. The OAIC rule lands in December. The ACCC's digital-competition regime is still pre-legislative, "expected" rather than committed. Three regulators, three timelines, no coordination between them -- and not even a consistent view of where the critical protections are supposed to live once The Framework exists to align them -- while adoption compounds at venture speed, making any regime harder to impose against the flow of consumer take-up.

## Who cut the ribbon

One more detail, because it is the tell. Within hours of the Prime Minister's speech, Microsoft published an endorsement of the framework and confirmed it had signed a memorandum committing to the government's data-centre expectations. Anthropic, which signed its own Australian memorandum back in March, is reportedly weighing a very large local data-centre investment. When the two companies whose products are the thing being regulated applaud your regulatory framework within the same news cycle, it is worth asking who the framework is built to serve. One local commentator called the emerging shape a "sophisticated facilities manager" -- a country that hosts the infrastructure and calls the hosting sovereignty. I think that is close to right, and I will come back to it tomorrow, because model sovereignty is Thursday's fight.

## The fair objection

I owe you the strongest counter-argument, because it is a good one and it comes from people who know this landscape better than most. The sharpest version I read -- from Marek Kowalkiewicz, quoting the broadcaster Ellen Fanning -- paired the very same 6.4x number with the announcement and drew the opposite lesson: the usage is the regulation working. Australians have adopted the tools, the market has spoken, so where the government should be pressing is on incentive and capability, not on more governance. "That's the regulation. Where's the incentive?"

It is a fair challenge, and I do not want to wave it away. My answer is the 6.4x number itself. When adoption is running at six-and-a-half times the predicted rate and still climbing, the incentive is plainly not the problem -- the incentive is taking care of itself. What has not kept pace is any operative answer to the question a regulated business actually has to answer: when your AI makes a decision that significantly affects a customer, a member, a patient, who is accountable, and how do you prove it. Adoption at venture speed does not build that answer. It just raises the stakes of not having it.

Come back to that line between usage and deployment, because it is the whole case. The regulation is not aimed at what you do when you type a question into an AI. It is aimed at what your business does with the answer -- and with the exhaust that comes off it. The inference under the covers, the one that tends to weigh toward the operating company, is the thing that has to be justified. Not the customer's use of the tool; the company's use of what the tool infers. It is the same argument I made yesterday: the exhaust is the value, and the exhaust is the second charge -- you pay once to use the tool, and the business collects again on what the tool infers about you along the way.

## So, Wednesday

If you run a club, a fund, a hospital, a lender -- anything in this country where a regulator can already reach you -- here is the thing the big, shiny ribbon-cutting is designed to make you forget: your obligations are not waiting for 2027, regardless of where The Framework is in its long gestation.

**The OAIC clock ticks 10 December, regardless of what the Office of AI eventually becomes.** Privacy-policy disclosure of your automated decisions, on a hard date, whatever The Framework does or doesn't do by then. And not some, not a few, but every piece of inference that affects a business decision about any individual person potentially needs to be justified and detailed in barely four months.

**CPS 230 is live now.** The offshoring question underneath it -- where your inference actually happens, and whether that arrangement is material -- is a determination you make and document this year, not when the framework finally arrives.

**A front door is not a building.** A national framework announced in the future tense does not pause the obligations that already exist in the present one. A door standing on its own is a striking image and nothing more. It is the structure behind it -- the walls, the rooms, the load-bearing beams -- that tells you how inference actually sits inside your company, your decisions, and your day-to-day. The Framework, for now, is the door.

So the calculus for you is not "what will The Framework require." It is "what does the law already require, today, that I have been letting a promised framework distract me from." If you run a club, you already know the shape of this fear: the AUSTRAC five-year audit that lands while your own policy is only two years old -- a lookback that reaches further back than the rules were ever clearly in force. Every regulated business has its own version of that dread, whatever the one regulator is that can already reach you. Here is the part worth sitting with. AUSTRAC points that lookback at a single lane -- money laundering, the cash desk. The Framework, when it finally arrives, points the same retroactive machinery at every lane. Not just your AML. Every marketing decision, every discount and loyalty program, every automated call on who gets which offer -- anywhere a machine helped decide something about a member becomes something you may one day have to justify against rules that were not in force when you made the call. The country's usage is a world-beating number; its governance is a 2027 IOU. And given 2027 is barely four months from being next year, it is entirely possible that IOU is really a 2028 or 2029 bill -- retroactively applied to what you are doing right now.

You get to decide which one your compliance calendar is actually run against -- knowing that every month spent waiting is a month the retroactive clock keeps running. Does the gap between what The Framework promises and what the law already demands change how you spend the four months between now and December -- or is it just one more announcement to file away, believing it will change long before the bill comes due?
