# The World's Biggest AI User Has No Framework

Australia is the heaviest per-capita user of AI on earth. It also does not have an AI law.

Hold those two facts next to each other, because the gap between them is the whole story. Anthropic's own usage index puts Australia first out of 121 countries, running at **6.4 times** the rate its population would predict -- up from 4.1x just two months earlier. Not plateauing. Accelerating. And the country doing all that adopting will not have a single operative national framework governing any of it until 2027 at the earliest.

Here is the thing about that gap, and why I want to spend a Wednesday on it. Two weeks ago the Prime Minister stood up in Sydney and announced a fix -- a new "Office of AI," pitched as a "single national framework" and, in his words, a "world first." It made headlines. It made the tech sector nod. And if you read it the way it was written to be read, you would think the vacuum just got filled.

It didn't. What got announced was a front door. A very nice front door, with a brass plate and a ribbon-cutting. Behind it, for now, is a building that hasn't been constructed.

## What was announced, and what actually exists

Let me be precise, because precision is the entire point when a government is selling you a tense.

What exists, as of the speech: an Office of AI, sitting inside the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet, whose job is to *coordinate* the design of Australian AI standards. It has no statutory powers. It has no announced budget. It has no announced staffing. The Greens' David Shoebridge called it "a single door in his office," and unkind as that is, I have not found a factual error in it.

What does not exist: the framework. The Prime Minister's actual words were that Australia "will be the first country in the world to bring these issues into a single, national framework." Read that sentence again and watch the verb. *Will be.* National Cabinet is scheduled to take up data-centre rules next month; legislation is promised for "early next year." Until that legislation passes, nothing new binds anyone. What was delivered is a governance scaffold and a press release. What was promised is a framework, dated 2027.

I want to be fair: a scaffold is not nothing, and coordinating a genuinely fragmented mess of sector rules is real work. But "world first" is doing an enormous amount of lifting for a thing that does not yet operate. The EU AI Act is a real, operative, horizontal law that people are complying with right now. Australia's is an intention with a ribbon on it.

## The regime that reversed itself

Here is the part that earns this piece its thread. This is the Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime I have been tracking all month, and it just did the most ad hoc thing I have seen it do yet: it reversed its own reversal.

Rewind to December 2025. The same government looked at the question of a dedicated AI Act and decided against it -- existing frameworks were enough, it said, no need for a standalone law. Seven months later, in July 2026, the same government announces a "world-first single national framework." One consultant I read counted five separate policy reversals on AI in eighteen months. This is a regime so ad hoc it cannot hold a position on whether to be ad hoc.

That matters to you, if you run anything regulated in this country, because it tells you what kind of ground you are building on. You are not waiting for a stable framework to arrive and then complying with it. You are trying to build a compliance posture on top of a policy that changes direction faster than most procurement cycles complete.

## Electrons before people

Now look at what the new framework actually reaches for first, because the ordering tells you who it is really for.

The concrete, dated obligations in the announcement are all about data centres. Large AI data centres will have to be net energy generators, underwrite their own new power supply, pay full grid-connection costs, and minimise water use. Those are real requirements with real teeth, and National Cabinet takes them up next month.

Now count the obligations aimed at protecting a *person* from an automated decision. There is essentially one on the calendar, and it is not new to this announcement: the OAIC's automated-decision-making transparency rule, which lands on **10 December 2026** and requires you to disclose in your privacy policy which substantially automated decisions your systems make about people. The right to actually *contest* one of those decisions? Deferred, with no date attached.

Sit with the ordering. The framework regulates the water and the electrons before it regulates a single decision made about a human being. The heaviest-adopting population on earth gets rules for the buildings the models live in before it gets rules for what the models are allowed to decide about them.

And the rest of the regime is exactly the piecemeal patchwork the "single framework" language is meant to make you forget. APRA's CPS 230 is in force -- and here I have to correct something, because it matters: CPS 230 is an operational-resilience standard that names no AI at all. It does not classify your AI vendor as anything. What it does is define a *material offshoring arrangement* by where the service is actually performed, which can reach offshore model inference where the arrangement is material -- but that is a determination you make and defend, not a label the regulator hands you. The OAIC rule lands in December. The ACCC's digital-competition regime is still pre-legislative, "expected" rather than committed. Three regulators, three timelines, no coordination between them -- while usage compounds at venture speed.

## Who cut the ribbon

One more detail, because it is the tell. Within hours of the Prime Minister's speech, Microsoft published an endorsement of the framework and confirmed it had signed a memorandum committing to the government's data-centre expectations. Anthropic, which signed its own Australian memorandum back in March, is reportedly weighing a very large local data-centre investment. When the two companies whose products *are* the thing being regulated applaud your regulatory framework within the same news cycle, it is worth asking who the framework is built to serve. One local commentator called the emerging shape a "sophisticated facilities manager" -- a country that hosts the infrastructure and calls the hosting sovereignty. I think that is close to right, and I will come back to it tomorrow, because model sovereignty is Thursday's fight.

## The fair objection

I owe you the strongest counter-argument, because it is a good one and it comes from people who know this landscape better than most. The sharpest version I read paired the very same 6.4x number with the announcement and drew the opposite lesson: the usage *is* the regulation working -- Australians have adopted the tools, the market has spoken, so where the government should be pressing is on *incentive* and capability, not on more governance. "That's the regulation. Where's the incentive?"

It is a fair challenge, and I do not want to wave it away. My answer is the 6.4x number itself. When adoption is running at six-and-a-half times the predicted rate and still climbing, the incentive is plainly not the problem -- the incentive is taking care of itself. What has not kept pace is any operative answer to the question a regulated business actually has to answer: when your AI makes a decision that significantly affects a customer, a member, a patient, who is accountable, and how do you prove it. Adoption at venture speed does not build that answer. It just raises the stakes of not having it.

## So, Wednesday

If you run a club, a fund, a hospital, a lender -- anything in this country where a regulator can already reach you -- here is the thing the ribbon-cutting is designed to make you forget: your obligations are not waiting for 2027.

The OAIC clock ticks on 10 December regardless of what the Office of AI eventually becomes. CPS 230 is live now. The offshoring question about where your inference actually happens is a decision you have to make and document this year, not when the "framework" finally arrives. A front door is not a building, and a national framework announced in the future tense does not pause the obligations that already exist in the present one.

So the calculus for you is not "what will the framework require." It is "what does the law already require, today, that I have been letting a promised framework distract me from." The country's usage is a world-beating number. Its governance is a 2027 IOU. You get to decide which one your compliance calendar is actually run against -- or whether it just scrolls past, one more announcement in a news cycle that never even seems to pause, let alone stop.
