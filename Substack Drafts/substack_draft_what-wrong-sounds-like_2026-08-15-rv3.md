It has been a heavy week on this page. Five days of instruments that did not do what the people relying on them assumed.

So here is the part that is actually good news, and it has been sitting underneath all five days without me saying it out loud.

Anybody who has worked in one place long enough knows which noise is normal. The compressor that always groans on startup and means nothing. The report that runs eleven minutes instead of four, which is fine on a Monday and is not fine on a Thursday. The invoice that is a little too round. Nobody taught you this and it is not written anywhere. You accumulated it, and you probably could not produce a list of it if I asked, but you would notice in a second if it were off.

That is the scarcest thing in the room right now, and this week is five consecutive proofs of it.

## What actually went wrong on Monday through Friday

Go back through it, because the pattern only shows up laid side by side.

The control that checked identity was designed by somebody who did not know that an agent reads text an attacker wrote. The organization that could not name which tasks moved had never sat with the tasks. The inventory scoped to what you operate was drafted by somebody who had not noticed that suppliers now run models inside services. The workplace surveillance statute pointed employer-to-employee because nobody drafting it imagined the surveillance walking in as a guest. And the permission prompt was parsing strings because it was built by people solving a string problem rather than a judgment problem.

Every one of those was a competent person building a sensible thing. Not one of them failed for lack of technical skill. They failed because whoever scoped the control had never stood in the room it was supposed to protect, and so they aimed it at the thing that was easy to describe instead of the thing that actually goes wrong.

Which is a problem, and it is also a job description.

## One signpost, because I used a word yesterday in a different sense

Yesterday's piece was about capability -- the model's. A classifier that catches more than a tired human does.

Today's is about capability too, and it is entirely yours, and the two are not in competition. The machine got better at doing. It did not get better at knowing which of your Thursdays is the wrong kind of Thursday. That gap is not closing on any timeline I can see, and it is the only part of this whole story where the scarcity runs in your favor.

I am also not the only one who sees it. The people furthest out in front of this have started calling it taste. And as in so much else in life, taste turns out to be in short supply.

## The question changed while everybody was arguing

Here is the shift worth noticing, because it happened quietly and it is one of many reasons the displacement argument has gone a bit flat.

For about two years the question was *will this happen to me*, and what will I do when it does. For the people making the investment now, it has changed into *how fast can I shape this in my function.* And the reason the doom conversation is losing oxygen is not that the concern was wrong. It is that the people who absorbed it fastest stopped arguing and started building, and they are no longer in the comments.

The research points the same way, and I can be less hedged about this than I expected to be.

In June, Anthropic published a study of about four hundred thousand Claude Code sessions from roughly two hundred and thirty-five thousand users, run between October and April. It is the same company I was writing about yesterday, which I will simply note rather than make anything of. The finding that matters here is that professional background stopped predicting outcomes. In their words, in sessions that produced code, *"every one of the ten largest occupations in our dataset lands within seven points of software engineers"* on success. Not the coders and everyone else. Ten occupations, near enough level.

What did predict success was expertise -- and specifically expertise in the task at hand rather than on a résumé. Their own framing is that a senior engineer working in a language they do not know is a beginner for that task. Sessions where the person looked like a novice verified success fifteen percent of the time; intermediate or expert, twenty-eight to thirty-three percent.

Now the honest part, because it is more interesting than the finding. Nobody asked those users what they knew. Expertise was inferred from the transcript, from three signals: *"how precisely the user frames their directions, what they ask Claude to verify, and whether the user tends to correct Claude or Claude tends to correct the user."*

Read that twice. There is real circularity in it -- specifying precisely and correcting the machine are behaviors that *cause* good outcomes, so a measure built from them will predict outcomes partly by construction. Which means the study does not quite establish "people who know things do better." It establishes something narrower and, for our purposes, better: **the observable behavior that predicts success is knowing enough to say exactly what you want and to correct the answer rather than be corrected by it.** That is not a credential. That is a disposition, and it is the entire subject of this piece.

One more number from the same study, because it deserves more attention than it has had. Across everything, verified success ran somewhere around twenty-six to thirty percent. Even the expert sessions topped out around a third. Two thirds to three quarters of the time, nobody could confirm the thing worked. That is the vendor's own measurement of its own product, and it is the honest floor under every "the agents do the work now" conversation you will have this year.

Anecdotally, and for whatever it is worth against four hundred thousand sessions: AI is like almost anything else. The more you invest in making it work for you, the more you get back out.

## The thing companies are not telling you

Now the number that made me want to write this as the Saturday piece rather than a Wednesday one.

BCG surveyed nearly twelve thousand workers across fourteen markets this year. Among regular AI users in frontline roles -- a subgroup of a subgroup, and I will come back to that -- **forty-two percent say they save at least a full working day every week**. And **sixty-six percent report getting limited guidance or none at all** about what to do with the time. BCG went one step further than I would have dared infer: more than half do not redirect the time into anything strategic at all.

Sit with the shape of that. The capability arrived. The hours are real, at least as reported. And two thirds of the people holding those hours were handed them with no instruction, so the time quietly flows back into more of the same work, and nobody decides anything.

Two honest caveats, because this is a survey and I have spent a week complaining about numbers people did not read carefully. Those figures describe regular users in frontline roles, not the whole workforce -- a different survey of five thousand knowledge workers, fielded late last year, found that sixty-eight percent of AI users save four hours a week or less. Both can be true; they are different people measured at different times. And BCG's better-known finding from the same study -- that forty-seven percent say they now spend more time directing these tools than doing the work themselves -- is self-reported perception rather than measurement. *Say* is doing real work in that sentence.

The underlying story survives the decimal places anyway, and it is not the one we were promised. Despite all the warnings of a job-pocalypse, what has actually shown up across most working groups is job *transformation*. The hiring data now says so out loud. A study published in June matched corporate card spending against workforce records for more than twenty-one thousand US companies, and found that firms in the top tier of AI spending grew employment by around ten percent after adopting, while light adopters showed no significant change at all. The authors are careful, and so am I: heavy AI adopters were already bigger, faster-growing and better capitalized before they bought anything, so this is correlation wearing good clothes. But it is the opposite direction from the story we were sold, and it is now measured rather than argued. My own read on the difference is executive: the companies treating AI as an efficiency tool cut, and the companies treating it as a capability tool build.

But the gap is the real story. Nobody is going to tell you what to do with the day. That is not a conspiracy, it is that your employer has not worked it out either. Which means, if you want it, you have an unusual opportunity to steer the transition somewhere that suits you.

## The seat that is actually forming

Two things surfaced this week that point at the same shape from different directions, and I want to be careful about both, because one of them is softer than it looked.

The first is what Nathaniel Whittemore has been calling the handoff problem: deciding what to delegate, and verifying what came back. Not operating the agent. The judgment on either side of it. That is the Editor and the Risk Steward from the archetype work, restated as a *skill* rather than a title, which is a considerably more useful framing because you can practice a skill on Monday without asking anyone's permission.

The second arrived as a job title, and the job title turns out not to exist. I went looking for the Cost-per-Accepted-Task Analyst -- somebody measuring the business value of what agents produce rather than the tokens they burn -- and found no postings. Not a thin market. None, anywhere, on any board, ever. It is a useful description of a function, coined on a podcast a fortnight ago, and it traveled fast because it names something real. I am printing that rather than hedging it, because a fortnight from now somebody will quote the title back at you as evidence of a hiring wave.

What *is* real is the measurement underneath it, and the measurement is the interesting part. The industry spent two years counting tokens. Counting tokens made sense when the work was one question and one answer. It stopped making sense when the work became an agent going away for twenty minutes -- because on the best numbers available, around sixty percent of what an agentic task consumes goes not on producing the first answer but on checking, repairing and reverifying it afterwards. McKinsey has been circulating that figure; it originates in an academic paper measuring software engineering workflows, and I would not stretch it past that. But the direction is unmistakable. Most of the money is spent on the part where somebody decides whether the answer was any good.

Which is why the sensible unit is no longer what was produced but what was *accepted* -- and somebody has to decide what accepted means. That is not a finance decision. It is a domain judgment with a price attached to it.

And that seat, unlike the title, is genuinely being filled. AI FinOps became a formal category of the FinOps Framework in March, with certified training behind it, and there are real roles at real companies -- Qualcomm is advertising a lead position between a hundred and fifty-five and two hundred and thirty-three thousand dollars, with Singtel and Autodesk running their own versions. Then the honest deflation, which the governing body supplies itself: this is dozens of postings, not hundreds, and the Foundation's own guidance calls the work *"adapting classic FinOps practices"* rather than a new profession. The billing standard the whole discipline runs on still cannot represent a token properly. A field whose measurement standard cannot yet describe its own basic unit is early, whatever the job boards say.

So: one title that does not exist, one specialty that does and is smaller than the noise around it. What makes both worth your attention is not the volume. It is that the shape being described in both cases is *domain judgment applied to machine output*, which is the thing this whole piece is about.

And it is not abstract to me. We are fighting this exact battle right now.

We are searching for QA testers, and what we need is not somebody who can run the battery of checks in the CI/CD pipeline. That part is the easy part, and increasingly it is the part that runs itself. What we need is somebody who can also tell us about the customer experience -- the actual *quality* of the output, the soft measurements that keep turning out to decide whether a product succeeds. One thing has become clear along the way. When creating the product gets cheaper, the differentiator moves to how the product makes you feel: the smoothness of the delivery, whether it fits your picture of your business and how you work, and how you *want* to work. Testing for that is markedly harder than testing against a checklist. And wherever the AI ends up sitting in the mix -- which will be a forever-growing animal -- the critical skill turns out to be the soft one, even in engineering. The people who can decide what is *best*, and meet somebody else's vision in that decision, are worth a great deal right now. Developing that judgment at scale, about your own domain and about everything your domain touches, is one of the better things you can do for the next decade of your working life.

## The part I am not going to pretend about

Last Thursday I made this argument's honest caveat, and I am not going to re-run it, but I am also not going to quietly drop it now that it is inconvenient.

Everything above is a reassurance to people who already have somewhere to stand. If you have fifteen years in a domain, the message is genuinely good: you are most of the way there and the missing piece is a disposition rather than a degree. If you are twenty-four and trying to accumulate the knowledge in the first place, "your domain expertise is the scarce asset" is not advice, it is a description of something you do not have yet.

I have one real piece of good news for that person and I am going to be careful with it, because it is the kind of number that gets over-read. In the same twenty-one-thousand-company study, the heaviest AI adopters did not just grow headcount -- they grew *entry-level* headcount, by around twelve percent, and entry-level workers ended up a larger share of their workforce than at comparable firms that had not adopted. The lighter adopters went the other way and trimmed their junior ranks. So the picture is not "the entry-level job is disappearing." It is closer to "the entry-level job is disappearing at the companies doing this half-heartedly, and growing at the companies doing it seriously." That is genuinely useful if you are twenty-four, because it tells you where to apply. Same caveat as before: those companies were already growing.

Beyond that I do not have a clean answer, and I distrust anybody who says they do. What I would say is that the accumulation got faster, not slower -- you can now stand next to a system that will explain any part of a domain to you at three in the morning, which is not nothing, and it is a great deal more than I had. And the other half of it needs nothing but regular, deliberate effort. Domain knowledge is the shortcut. But developing the judgment, and then being able to show it -- in the interview, on day one, on day ninety as the domain knowledge starts to knit together -- that is what will make the difference for you, and to any employer worth working for.

## What I would actually do with a Saturday

**Write down three things you would catch that a new hire would not.** Not a process document. Three specific, unglamorous things -- the number that is wrong when it is too round, the sequence that means somebody skipped a step, the customer whose ordinary request is never ordinary. This takes fifteen minutes and almost nobody has ever done it.

**Then hand one of them to an agent and watch it fail.** Give it the task, do not give it the tell, and see what it produces. It will be confident and it will be wrong in the exact place your fifteen years live. That is the most instructive half hour available to you this weekend, and it costs nothing.

**Then write the tell down and give it that too.** What you have just done is the entire job. Not prompting, and not orchestration. You located a piece of judgment that existed only in your head, made it explicit, and installed it. That is the skill the whole market is fumbling towards a title for, and you can practice it on a Saturday in your own function without a budget, a vendor or anybody's approval.

**And if you do not have fifteen years to draw on, take a different tack.** I heard this suggested on a podcast this week and it stuck with me. Set a timer on your phone to go off every hour or two. When it goes, screenshot whatever you are doing, or photograph what is on your screen, or take a short video if the task is complicated. Feed it to Claude or Codex or whatever you use, and ask how you could do that better. Then try the suggestions. They will not all work -- guaranteed -- but if you stay open to them, some will take you somewhere worth going. And either way you are practicing the thing this whole piece is about, which is separating the good suggestions from the bad ones. Nobody learns that without making some poor calls first. So do not sweat it. The effort produces the result. I promise.

## What this week was actually about

Whatever you end up writing down this morning will feel too small to matter. The number that is wrong when it is too round. The sequence that means somebody skipped a step.

It is exactly what was missing all week.

Every instrument that failed between Monday and Friday failed for want of one of those. Not a budget, not a better technology, not a smarter person drafting it. A small piece of knowledge about how the thing actually goes wrong, sitting unwritten in somebody's head, in a building, where nobody thought to ask for it.

I said earlier that the people out in front of this have started calling it taste, and that taste is in short supply. Five days of evidence say the shortage is real. The part worth sitting with on a Saturday is *who* is short of it -- because on this week's evidence it is not you.

None of what starts on Monday begins with a tool, a platform or a course. It begins with somebody deciding that what they already know is worth writing down, and then doing something about it. Which is the one part of this entire enterprise that has never needed anybody's permission.
