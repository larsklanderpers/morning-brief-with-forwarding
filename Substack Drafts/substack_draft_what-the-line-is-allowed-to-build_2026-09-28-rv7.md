# What the Line Is Allowed to Build

A small team can now produce more work in a month than it can decide about. That is the change, and most of what gets written about AI coding is still describing a faster keyboard.

Anyone who has ever sent a job to a commercial printer knows where the real work in that building sits, and it is not at the press. The press runs itself once it is running. The work is the hour before: the proof on the light table, the call back to the customer, somebody with a loupe checking that the phone number in the ad is right, because the press does not care whether the phone number is right. It will produce fifty thousand of whatever you give it, beautifully, at speed, and hand you the pallets. The skill in that building was never the making. It was deciding what the thing was before the machine made fifty thousand of it.

Software just got a press. I can tell you from a first-person seat that it is very much a roller-coaster ride right now. Guys who used to ship 4 PRs in a day and count that as a solid day's work they were really proud of now ship a hundred a week. I myself shipped 140 this past week and I was busy with a bunch of non-code related items. The capacity for shipping has completely changed. The proof that what is shipping is quality -- that has also really changed. Determining what's quality and what's not has completely defined several major roles and actions in software development in the last year, and proving code quality is arguably one of the most critical.

Who signs and stands behind the proof is still an open question.

## They are not describing a tool, they are describing a job change

Three teams have now published, in their own words, what their engineering work looks like at volume. It is worth reading what they actually said, because the summaries have been flattening it into a productivity story, and it is not, in my opinion, a productivity story.

Zach Lloyd, the CEO of Warp, published a post in June titled "We are now factory engineers, not product engineers." The sentence that matters is this one: "The goal of this factory is to ship an incredible product, but the day-to-day job of the engineer is not to build that product directly -- it's to build the thing that builds that product." He goes further in the same post: "The job of every engineer is to improve the efficiency of their team's product factory." And he sets the measure: "It's measured by the percentage of all changes that are shipped automatically."

Read that as a job description rather than as a claim about tooling and it is a different document. The engineer is not being handed a faster way to write the function. The engineer is being moved off writing the function entirely, and onto the question of what the line should be set up to make.

And Warp is not the high-water mark. Lauren Tan, who posts on X as @poteto (and whose Dr. Eggbot I installed back on 12 September), works on Grok Bot at SpaceXAI (before that she was on the React compiler team, and at Cursor and Meta). At the end of August she wrote that her personal set of agent skills had "allowed me to ship 2,000 PRs a month to production with high confidence." Cursor's own conference schedule billed her London talk as "I Shipped 2,000 PRs Last Month." Five days later, on the same day the Warp episode went out, she put the talk up on X herself under the line "here's how i shipped 2,500 PRs last month to production." One person, by her own count, into production, and a number that moved by a quarter in five days, which tells you something about how much weight any single volume figure can carry.

What makes her worth quoting is not the number. It is what she says she now spends her time on. She calls herself the codebase's "gardener and maintainer," watching a team that is "now landing hundreds of PRs a day" and adding lints and checks to keep the whole thing clean. And she is candid that she used to treat PR counts as "simply a vanity metric," until she found that volume does matter, "especially when you are able to maintain or even increase the level of quality of the product with agents." That is the job change, in one person.

The AWS Kiro team published the same shape in September, from three full-time engineers who merged a thousand pull requests in seven days with something like five hundred community contributors. Their line is the honest one: "Every session still needs someone to define its goal, start it, watch for problems, judge its result, and decide what happens next. Doing those five tasks across 50 sessions becomes a full-time job."

That is not a complaint about AI. It is a description of a job that did not exist two years ago and now takes a person all day.

And Stripe, which matters most here for a reason I will get to, described its internal agents in February: "more than a thousand pull requests merged each week," and "Though humans review the code, minions write it from start to finish."

Now, the reason Stripe matters most. Warp sells software factories. Kiro is an AWS product and the post is about Kiro Crew, which you can buy. Lauren works on Grok Bot at SpaceXAI, so her talk is, in part, a showcase for it, and for Cursor, whose conference it was written for and whose marketplace carries her pstack. For all three of them, their own throughput numbers are also their product demo, and you should read them the way you read any vendor reporting its own results, which is to say with interest and with your hand on your wallet. Stripe does not sell you an agent. Stripe is a payments company describing its own engineering, with nothing to sell you at the end of the post. When the company with nothing to sell reports the same shape as the three with something to sell, the shape is probably real even if the numbers are not comparable.

## Said on air, counted in print

The number that got me looking at this in the first place is two thousand. There is a podcast episode from 21 September, on Claire Vo's "How I AI" (a show about how people actually build with these tools), titled "How Warp ships 2,000 PRs a month with AI factories," and Lloyd says the figure on air.

What the spoken number does not tell you is what it counts: opened or merged, human or agent, all repositories or one. Warp's own published numbers are smaller and more careful. In August Lloyd wrote that "At Warp we are currently automating about 30% of our tasks through our factories and expect this number to rise quickly in the next few months." A Warp engineer wrote a week later about "Warp's internal software factory, which has helped us ship over 300 PRs in the past week." Three hundred a week is roughly thirteen hundred a month, so the two figures are either counting different things or measuring different months. Both can be true. Neither tells you the share an agent wrote.

When you watch the entire video, she shares the stack, shares the intent, and shares the accomplishment; but the bigger point she makes is not really about her own PR count. She describes her verification skill as "more like critical infrastructure rather than 'just' a skill," and says that "a good one will amplify the output of your whole team, including non-engineers." In other words, the tooling is good enough that the whole team runs on it, not just her. It's hard to tell the actual, valuable volume, and so it's hard to say just how impactful it is. But for sure, as Lauren says, what was always a vanity metric in the past is no longer; and if the merged and shipped fractions of those metrics are hovering even as low as 40% (which from my own experience is probably not right, our lived action here is closer to 75% of all and climbing), then you still have small teams shipping incredible amounts of improvements.

The argument does not actually depend on either of the two thousand(s), which is just as well, because a single spoken number is the easiest thing in a story like this to misquote. It doesn't even depend on Stripe's thousand a week. Rather, the argument needs one sentence to survive: a named team is shipping at a volume that changes what the job is, and says so in its own words. Lloyd's June post says it without a number attached. If you see the two thousand repeated this week, it is worth asking which of those counts the person repeating it thinks they are quoting. Regardless, this kind of sea change doesn't come about just by doing the same thing faster: it's only accomplishable with role change(s).

## What a unit of work costs now, which is the part the CFO will find first

Here is where it stops being an engineering conversation.

Warp published a benchmark case study at the start of September: "Using Benchmarks, we reduced Warp's internal factory cost per PR from $80 to $30." The detail underneath is that they swapped the implementation model, measured compliance on a thirty-task set drawn from their own codebase, and reported it going from 69% to 87% while the cost came down. The benchmark run itself cost $2,130.57 and took under four hours. Vendor numbers, on a vendor's own benchmark, about a product the vendor sells. Take them as a direction rather than as a figure. Nevertheless, a very important direction, regardless of the specifics of the velocity, and for anyone in the finance side of the interpretation, the kind of thing that makes it easy to say "software engineering is dead" or "the cost per PR has gone down so radically who needs all these developers?"

The direction is what matters, and Lloyd named the consequence himself in June: "It's going to show up as COGS on the P&L."

Sit with that for a second, because it is a bigger sentence than it looks. Engineering has spent forty years as a headcount line. Salaries, benefits, recruiting, the whole apparatus of R&D. You budget it annually, you defend it annually, and it is famously hard to attribute to any particular thing you shipped. Moving a meaningful share of it onto cost of goods sold turns it into something else: a variable, per-unit cost that finance already knows how to manage, because it looks exactly like cloud spend. And finance manages cloud spend by asking, every quarter, whether you need that much of it.

Whoever owns your AI budget is about to get a spreadsheet with a cost-per-change column in it. Anybody who has watched a FinOps team arrive at a cloud bill knows how the next conversation goes. It does not go "let's build more." It goes "why is this one $80 and since we get more than 2.5 times as much for the same cost now, where is the savings that correlates."

That is a good conversation to have, mostly. It is also the conversation where somebody who does not build software gets to set the throughput of the line, and I do not think most engineering leaders have noticed that the lever is being handed over. I do believe that those who have -- like me -- are fighting tooth and nail to maintain control over it. Not because they are protecting their domain; rather, because they understand that the COGS metric, if it becomes the dominant one in hiring and firing decisions, results in a situation where the company is potentially eating its seed corn: optimize on cost per task alone and you cut the people who can tell good output from bad, and with them the people who could have taught anyone else to, so quality drops no matter what you do next.

## Watch which metric they chose

The detail I keep coming back to is not the volume. It is the measure.

Warp tracks something it calls human interactions per pull request. Claire Vo's own recap page describes it as counting the interventions: re-prompting in Slack, commenting on a ticket, correcting code in a review. And the stated goal is to drive that number down over time.

As a productivity metric that is completely reasonable. Every intervention is friction, every intervention is a person stopping what they were doing, and a line that needs constant hand-holding is not a line, it is a very expensive pair of hands.

As a governance metric it is the same number pointing the other way. Every intervention you remove is also a place where a human being was looking at the work and is now not. The metric does not distinguish between an intervention that was waste and an intervention that was the only thing standing between a bad change and production. Driven far enough, a falling interactions-per-change number describes a line with no one watching it, and it will look like an improvement on the dashboard the entire way down.

I am not saying Warp is doing that. They clearly think hard about it, their own posts list human review of specs and of verification output as steps in the workflow, and they publish an open dashboard, which is more transparency than most. I am saying the metric has two readings, and only one of them ever gets put on a slide.

## What happens to the rest of the system

The counter-evidence to all of this is worth taking seriously, with its limits stated.

Faros AI published a report in April built on two years of telemetry from around twenty-two thousand developers across four thousand teams. At high AI adoption they found pull requests merging 16.2% faster and deployments per week down 11.7%. Median time in review up 441.5%. Incidents per pull request up 242.7%. And this line, which I have seen quoted wrong repeatedly: "Pull requests merged without any review, human or agentic, are up 31.3%." Secondary coverage keeps dropping the "or agentic," which turns a claim about unreviewed changes into a claim about human review specifically, and those are not the same claim.

The caveats are real. Faros sells engineering analytics, so it has a commercial interest in showing you a problem its dashboard can see. The data are correlational across adoption bands rather than controlled, so "teams that adopted AI hardest also had more incidents" is not the same as "AI caused the incidents." There are plausible readings where the causation runs the other way, or where both are caused by a team under pressure.

But the direction is consistent with what the volume companies themselves describe. Warp's answer to more changes is more automated verification. Kiro's answer is a person doing five judgment tasks across fifty sessions all day. None of the three teams is claiming that the output arrives ready. They are all, in their own way, describing the same thing: the making got cheap, and something after the making did not.

## How many people are actually working like this

Not many, if you read the survey properly, and it is worth reading properly, because that number is easy to misquote, though I will say there have been a number of viral posts on X in recent weeks suggesting that this outcome might be more common than I originally anticipated or than the numbers support.

Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index, published in May, names a group it calls Frontier Professionals: people who "use agents for multi-step workflows and building multi-agent systems" and who "routinely rethink workflows and identify where agents can augment or automate." The figure attached is 16%.

Sixteen percent of what, though. The survey went to twenty thousand full-time knowledge workers who already use AI at work, across ten markets, run by an outside research firm over about nine weeks. So it is 3,233 people out of twenty thousand AI users, not 16% of workers. And they qualified by self-reporting a combination of three behaviors, which means the category is built out of what people say about themselves, by a company that sells the agents in question.

Cite it for the direction and not the decimal: a real but small minority of people who already use AI at work say they have restructured how they work around it. That is roughly what you would expect of something that is two years old and requires you to rebuild your own job to get the benefit.

Which is the actual point. Three named teams and a slice of a survey is not an industry. Ethan Mollick (a Wharton professor who writes one of the more useful newsletters on how people actually use this stuff and whom you have probably seen me reference in previous pieces) published a piece on 18 September called "The Overhang," and his definition fits this gap exactly: "the gap between what these models can do and what almost anyone is doing with them." Warp, Kiro and Stripe are what the far side of that gap looks like, published by the people furthest along, and there is a window here before the practice hardens into something everyone copies without asking why.

## How this looks from the inside, when you have ink-stained fingers

From sitting in both the developer chair (the principal engineer chair, if you want to be picky about it) and the CTO chair, I can say there's no question that the generation of content has skyrocketed. There's also no question that for the first 6 to 8 weeks, I think, like most developers, I found that sensation of being able to ship at such volume (even though the volume was tied to very small, simple, solvable projects that were easily evaluated and checked) intoxicating. It was awesome to think that we would be able to do that that way forever, but it only took the first couple of times of finding problems to realize that that was not the norm. In fact, it wasn't even an accurate representation of what we were seeing right then and there. 

When you really dig into the theory behind this stuff, there are a lot of arguments about things like loop engineering and graph engineering, and precursors to that that came out over the months prior to where we are now. Ultimately, what all those things come down to is some way of defining how you make sure that what you're doing has quality.

We covered this back in August, when I argued for writing the brief and then writing the check before you look, but it is worth repeating here. The definition of a loop in this context, for pretty much everyone, is a process where you tell the agent or agent fleet, or whatever it is, what to do, and then you tell the agent fleet what success looks like. You run an evaluation of some type that details exactly what they're trying to accomplish. The agent or fleet of agents then goes off and works until it reaches that evaluation point.

This is part of why we were able to have such quick and substantial success when we started doing this in our development cycle: we were able to write simple evaluations for relatively simple problems and prove the whole thing out. When you take a complex piece of software (one of ours has over 480 interaction surfaces), writing an evaluation becomes very difficult, or impossible, realistically.

There are also entirely new disciplines that are springing up around how you take principles of design that have been around for 50 years in some cases and turn them into something that works in this new agentic model. I think that the takeaway from all of this is probably just that there's a lot of conversation right now about how taste and judgment and all these things are really what make the difference. We have not talked much in these pages about how you build taste and judgment, but we have certainly discussed the absolute necessity that you have to do so. One of the best ways to figure out whether what you're doing is actually doing what you think it's doing is to begin the process of running the evaluations and defining the loops and saying, "This is what success looks like."

Being able to do that for complex systems is part of why somebody is a principal engineer or a chief architect, or whatever phrase you want to give them. It takes learning. It takes a long time, for the most part, unless you have a lot of experience. I think what's most relevant to this piece is that it requires you to change your way of thinking about how software gets delivered. I think that's really going to be true, and is already true, but is going to be particularly true going forward for many, many knowledge worker use cases. The evaluation of what's still meaningful, what's still a task I should be doing, and what's something that is just busy work or what's just carryover from before (however you want to think about it) will be a huge part of the challenge of people's day-to-day work. 

## A few things worth considering from this that go beyond "I wish I understood that volume"

**Know who is allowed to authorize a run.** Not who reviews the output -- who decides that the line should produce this thing at all. In most organizations that decision is currently made by whoever happened to open the session, because until recently it rarely needed naming. It was not worth naming when a change cost a day of somebody's time. It is worth naming when it costs thirty dollars and four minutes.

**Find out what your cost per change is, before finance does.** You may not like the number, and the number may be perfectly fine, but the worst version of this conversation is the one where the first person to calculate it is the person looking for savings. If a meaningful share of your engineering spend is about to move from a headcount line to a per-unit line, the team that owns it should be the team that can explain it.

**Ask what your interventions-per-change number is doing, and whether anybody set a floor.** Falling is usually good. Falling without anyone having decided how far it should fall is a different thing, and it does not show up as a problem until it shows up as an incident.

Notice what all three of these statements don't do, which is to specify that you should see how hard and fast you can run and compare that to how good what you turn out looks. Every one of these is about making considered moves and understanding the implications of those moves. As with so many transformative technologies throughout history, the people who do this better than the others are the people who will actually be able to make it a consistent and continuous part of the way that they work. 

I'll give you another brief example from X about what I'm talking about here. One of the many, many voices on X has talked about how he's built a series of agents that basically scrape X overnight looking for new things:
- new demos that people have built
- new concepts
- new applications of a new model
- new applications of crossover between models
- new applications of an agent stack, whatever it might be

He has a series of bots that do nothing but scrape those things and come up with at least 10 every night. He then has the bots turn those 10 into demonstrations that are more meaningful for him based on the specifics of his area of work.

He's not just taking other people's things and turning them into more of the same things. He's taking a starting point, which is "I've seen this can be done." He's taking an evaluation point, which is "Okay, make it do that, except make it what I want it to be." He's evaluating the loop and how it goes, what he gets out of it, and whether that particular item or concept or whatever has relevance to his work every day. That particular loop can work all night, can work all weekend, can work for a week without you being in the middle of it. 

If it's constructed properly, all you focus on is the beginning and the end. You have to know what you want. At least at some level, you have to be able to specify what qualifies as being acceptable, and then you have to check the outcome and make sure you got what you expected. Fine-tune, rinse and repeat.

Lloyd is right that the job changed, and I think he is more right than the people arguing with him. Mollick puts the same turn in two sentences: "Now making is fast and cheap. The scarce resource is your ability to select among stuff using your own taste." The part I would add is that the new job is not smaller than the old one. Defining what gets built, setting the boundaries, and deciding what the line is allowed to produce was always the hardest part of engineering. It was just previously rationed by how slowly we could type.

The press does not care what is on the plate. It never did. The difference is that we used to have a week to look at the proof, and now, by the show's count, we have about thirty-five minutes, and the pallets are already stacking up at the end of the line.
