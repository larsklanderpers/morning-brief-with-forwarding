In Augusta Township, Michigan, voters threw out their own board's unanimous rezoning for a billion-dollar data center on 4 August, 2,314 to 269. Residents are still trying to recall all seven members of that board. As far as I can find, it is the first time American voters have used a referendum to overturn a specific data center approval.

That is what AI and data center politics looks like in 2026 in the places where people actually get to vote on it. It does not look very much like the argument on television, or the one in the Twitter-verse (the X-verse, I suppose).

Last Tuesday, in the middle of a piece about Washington, I wrote that the question of whether data centers and AI are the root cause of inflation, "whether that is fair and accurate or not, is a topic for another day." This is the other day. And I am going to take it a bit wider than inflation, because once you start pulling on the data center thread, it turns out to be attached to the midterms, to the power bill, to the county zoning board, and, somewhat unexpectedly, to a camera on a pole at the end of your street.

This is a Tuesday piece, so it is heavier on opinion than most of the weekday ones, if not a great deal lighter on numbers. The short version is this: AI is argued globally and voted on locally, and those are now two completely different arguments with two completely different scoreboards. Most of the coverage is watching the wrong one.

## The tower behind the school

In the late nineties, the fights were over cell towers.

The national conversation at the time was about the wireless revolution: spectrum auctions, the death of the landline, the phone in every pocket. Spectrum auctions did not fill many public meetings. What people turned up for was the tower, the specific one, going in behind the elementary school, or on top of the church steeple, or on the ridge you could see from your back deck. The global machine was argued in Washington and in boardrooms. The piece of it that needed a hundred feet of land and a concrete pad was argued in a county building, by people who lived within sight of it.

For towers, Congress actually wrote that split down. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 says that nothing in the law "shall limit or affect the authority of a State or local government" over the placement and construction of wireless facilities, which left the tower decision with the town, inside a few federal rules about deadlines, paperwork and not blocking service outright. And then, in the same paragraph, it took one argument off the table entirely: no state or local government may regulate a tower "on the basis of the environmental effects of radio frequency emissions" so long as it meets the federal rules. The town decides where. Washington decided what you are allowed to argue about when the town decides where.

That split matters here, because nothing in the ratepayer bill that just stalled in the Senate draws a line like that for data centers; it tells state utility commissions what to consider, and says nothing to a county about zoning. So every argument is on the table at the county meeting: the power bill, the water, the noise, the tax abatement, the farmland, the promises about jobs. And the county meeting is where the decisions are actually landing.

## The House vote and the Senate floor

Washington did try (which, honestly, is something of a pleasant change, even if it ultimately ended up as more of the same).

Last Wednesday the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3. That is not a typo; in the current Congress, 417 people agreeing on anything is roughly as rare as a quiet week in AI. The bill applies to loads above 100 megawatts and requires state utility commissions to *consider* standards that would make those large loads pay their full incremental cost to the grid. Consider, not adopt. Part of the reason it passed so easily, I suspect, is how little it binds.

On Thursday, Senator Jon Husted of Ohio asked the Senate to pass it by unanimous consent, which is the fast lane: if nobody objects, it goes. Senator Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, the top Democrat on the Energy Committee, objected, saying the bill "falls short of what we need to do to respond," and that instead of "voluntary pledges or suggestions to states" (his characterization of the House bill, to be clear, not mine), "Congress needs to pass real legislation with real teeth." He then asked for unanimous consent on his own bill, the GRID Savings Act, which would require large loads to pay for their grid connections outright. Senator Bernie Moreno of Ohio objected to that one. And then Moreno, with what I can only assume was a straight face, summed it up: "The term do-nothing Congress exists for a reason. We absolutely accomplished that right now."

The bill is not dead. It can still move through the Senate the slow way, and senators can ask for unanimous consent again. But on the day, the two parties each blocked the other's version in the same exchange, and nothing passed.

One more fact, and I am going to set it beside the others and leave it there. In August, Axios reported an internal memo from the Senate Republicans' own campaign committee about Ohio. It said: "More than any other thing in this race, data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck." It also said, "This has become a sleeper issue for the entire election cycle." I will leave the sequence there.

## The weeknight meetings

Now go down a level, to the bodies that meet on a weeknight, publish an agenda, and vote by name.

In Montgomery County, Maryland, the council voted 11 to 0 in July for an 18-month moratorium on data center development. In Prince William County, Virginia, in the middle of northern Virginia's data center belt, the board voted 8 to 0 in July to deny a project on roughly 2,000 acres. In Monterey Park, California, voters banned data centers outright in June with over 86 percent of the vote, and the mayor was admirably blunt about why they went to the ballot rather than leaving it to the council: "The next council could have come back and reversed it. So this ballot measure is more permanent."

And people are losing their seats over it, in both parties. In Box Elder County, Utah, two Republican commissioners lost their primaries in June over a data center campus, and one of them, Lee Perry, did not dress it up: "Do I think that the data center vote cost me the election? Yes I do." In Calvert County, Maryland, three Republican incumbents lost to challengers who backed a pause. Also in Maryland, in Frederick County, it was the Democratic council president who lost. In Festus, Missouri, four incumbents went out, and here is the detail I find most interesting: they included both the members who voted for the project *and* one who voted against it. That looks like anger about how the question got asked, and who got told when, more than anger about the answer. Data Center Knowledge counts eighteen incumbents turned out over data center decisions between August 2025 and August 2026, with its own honest caveat that the evidence is stronger in some cases than others.

The polling moved with it, and it moved fast. A Heatmap Pro survey in August 2025 had registered voters split almost evenly on a data center near their home, 43 in support and 42 opposed. The same question this August came back 75 percent opposed, with strong support down from 13 percent to 4. Gallup, in March, had 71 percent opposing one locally, including 63 percent of Republicans. This is not a blue issue or a red issue. It is a my-county issue.

Even the governors have noticed. Greg Abbott in Texas wants data centers to "bring their own power, reuse their own water and do it in a way that reduces the cost." Tina Kotek in Oregon says her state "needs to stop being a cheap date." Kelly Ayotte in New Hampshire: "It does not make sense at all to site a data center in New Hampshire." A Republican, a Democrat and a Republican, in three different registers, landing in roughly the same place.

I think, to me, the most interesting take on this argument is from talking to different people I know. You may not be aware, but Las Vegas has been home to one of the biggest data center operators in the world, Switch, since 2000, and its main campus sits right inside the valley. The best thing it did for its own good was to go renewable: since January 2016 the company says every one of its data centers has run on 100 percent renewable energy, largely from its own solar farms out near Apex. And so a lot of the objections that people might normally make to it do not really apply -- because it has spent a decade proving itself a good citizen.

Separate from the sound bites, everyone I have talked to about this around the country has a bit of a different take on it, but the most common theme to me has been less about data centers being bad and more about data centers being a bad *deal*. When you look at places where the deal was put together well, the counties and other governmental entities have done well and more importantly the residents have done well (property tax reductions, school improvements, infrastructure improvements, and more). And where it was not, the perception is that the data center is a drain and that it is causing local inflation to worsen by not paying its share. Ultimately, though, I think in so many of these places, the issue is that most of the data center deals have been made behind closed doors, with confidentiality agreements in place, and the impact of the deals gets shared with (or in some views, inflicted on) everyone in the vicinity, but in many cases those people have no idea what the terms of that item are and therefore they have no reason to appreciate it, only to despise it.

## How much of your power bill is theirs

I have to slow down a little here, because the local revolt is running on a claim that is real but only partly true, and a piece that just cheers the revolt on would be doing you a disservice (though honestly it would be a bit enjoyable to run).

The claim is that data centers are raising your electricity bill. The strongest public version of it has already been fact-checked down: in June, PolitiFact rated Senator Elizabeth Warren's statement that bills near large data centers had risen "as much as 267%" Mostly False, because the figure described wholesale prices at specific grid nodes, not residential bills, and wholesale is only somewhere between 30 and 50 percent of what you actually pay.

The more careful numbers are still not nothing. In PJM, the grid operator that covers much of the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, the independent market monitor puts data centers at about 9 percent of total wholesale costs for the first seven months of this year, around $5.1 billion of $56.7 billion, and credits data center load with $29.4 billion in capacity revenue increases across four auctions. And then, just to keep everyone honest, PJM's most recent auction, in July, cleared 2.5 percent *lower* than the year before. Some utilities are filing testimony that big new loads will lower residential bills, spreading fixed costs across more demand. Those are projections, not promises, and you should read them with the same skepticism you would read the scary numbers -- or the same skepticism you might apply to a more established argument, such as "trickle-down economics".

Virginia gives you both halves in a single report. Its legislative watchdog found data centers supply 31 percent of Loudoun County's local tax revenue, about $733 million, and support around 74,000 jobs statewide, and it also projected that unconstrained growth adds about $23 a month to a typical residential bill by 2030. Both of those are true at the same time. On the other side of the ledger, the tax breaks are large too: Good Jobs First puts Georgia's forgone revenue at $2.5 billion in fiscal 2026 alone, and fourteen states publish no annual figure for what their data center breaks cost at all. Water, for what it is worth, is the weakest strand of the case on Virginia's statewide numbers, even though it is one of the loudest at the microphone.

So the fair version is something like this: data centers are one real driver of rising costs among several, the tax base they bring is real where it has been measured, the tax breaks they get are real and often unmeasured, and the person at the county meeting is being asked to weigh all of that with none of it on a single page. That is exactly why process anger, the Festus kind, keeps showing up. People do not trust a deal they were not shown and, as importantly, one they are stuck with for decades or longer.

## The candidates and the money

The midterms are 42 days out, and AI, as such, is not on the ballot. Pew asked voters in July what they most want congressional candidates to talk about, and the economy topped the list at 29 percent; AI did not show up as a volunteered top issue at all. The same survey found 51 percent of Americans agree with *neither* party on AI policy, which is either a sign of a vacuum or a sign of an opportunity, depending on which campaign you work for.

What is on the ballot is the physical footprint of AI, and the money has noticed. AdImpact, via NPR, counts more than $45 million in ads touching data centers, split roughly evenly between the parties, with $12.8 million in the Georgia governor's race alone. In Georgia, two seats on the Public Service Commission, the body that actually sets utility rates, are up on 3 November with the majority at stake, and candidates of both parties are running on making data centers pay.

The two big AI-money networks, Leading the Future on the pro-industry side and Public First Action on the safety side, both spent heavily in the same New York Democratic primary in June, one against Alex Bores and one for him. Micah Lasher beat Bores, and his victory line was aimed at both: "I won't be taking my cues from either of you when it comes to protecting our kids, our jobs, our environment." In Michigan's Senate race, the Republican, Mike Rogers, says "I believe we must slow down development of AI," and the Democrat, Abdul El-Sayed, says "We need to all hit pause." (Rogers came out for a statewide data center moratorium in August, days after disclosures showed between $1.7 million and $2.6 million in AI-exposed holdings, per the Michigan Advance.)

And one more shift worth knowing about: the partisan valence of AI worry has flipped in three years. In 2023, 59 percent of Republicans told Pew they were more concerned than excited about AI, against 46 percent of Democrats. This year it is the other way around: Democrats 56, Republicans 49.

## The camera on the pole

This is the strand I did not expect to be writing about, and it is the one I personally find most interesting because it is one of the best examples I have seen yet of a seemingly innocuous technology that actually strikes at the underlying fabric of what many of us believe it actually *is* to be an American, and the reactions to it from that perspective cross red and blue to an extent that we seem to only be finding with technology issues these days.

Flock Safety sells automated license plate readers, the cameras on poles at intersections and neighborhood entrances that log every car that passes. By its own count it serves more than 5,000 agencies, and it raised $500 million at a valuation of roughly $8.3 billion at the end of August. In most towns, those cameras go up the same way a data center does: a city council or a county board votes on a contract, often on a consent agenda, usually without much of an audience.

The audiences have arrived. In Pflugerville, Texas, a public records request showed that 459 outside agencies had run about 1.6 million searches against the city's 28 cameras over six months, against 5,078 searches by the city's own police. The council ended the contract unanimously in August. The city was paying about $87,500 a year so that other people's police departments could search a record of every car that passed its cameras. In Knox County, Tennessee, under a Republican mayor, commissioners passed a unanimous emergency ordinance in August, and the trigger was procedure rather than privacy: a $2.6 million maintenance ask that had never come before them. In Texas the whole state network is funded by a one-dollar levy on every auto insurance policy, which means no city council or county board ever voted on them, but every driver with a policy is paying for them.

And look at who is doing the objecting. Greg Abbott: "Flock cameras are being reduced dramatically. They're going to be required to be used responsibly if they're used at all." Ron DeSantis: "I'm all about having law enforcement have tools. But I don't want to have this become a surveillance state." Senator Josh Hawley opened a Judiciary subcommittee probe in August: "Americans do not surrender their privacy rights when they drive to work, drop their kids off at school, or go to church." The House bill to cut off federal grants to cities running the cameras, the Flock-Off Act, was introduced by Thomas Massie with Lauren Boebert, Paul Gosar, Chip Roy, Victoria Spartz and, on the other side of the aisle, Ro Khanna. The Fourth Amendment lawsuits are being run by the Institute for Justice and the Wisconsin Institute for Law & Liberty, a libertarian shop and a conservative one, while the ACLU does the records work. The sharpest slogan in the whole fight belongs to a gun-rights group, the National Association for Gun Rights: "No warrant. No suspicion. No vote."

That last line is the Flock fight in six words, really: the objection is as much about the vote that never happened as about the camera.

The company has moved, too. At the end of July, Flock told a reporter its default retention was 30 days. By mid-August its own trust page said seven, "unless local law says otherwise," and it added tools letting a community block searches for specified purposes, including immigration enforcement. That is what a vendor does when the county meetings start going badly.

## The cities that kept their cameras

I would love to tell you that this is a tidy left-right alliance sweeping the country. It is not, and the piece is more honest with the exceptions in.

Cleveland, under a Democratic mayor, voted 9 to 6 in July to renew its cameras, despite records showing around 2,000 immigration-related searches in 2025. Oklahoma City renewed 5 to 3 in August with nobody from the public speaking in favor. Carrollton, Texas, expanded its network this month, and its police chief called it "the best tool we have to reduce crime." (Whether it actually reduces crime is a live fight between two interested institutions, with studies on both sides, and I am not going to pretend it is settled in either direction.) Denver canceled a ten-year, $22 million Flock contract in February and replaced it with a system from Axon, which the ACLU also opposed, so ending a Flock contract is not the same thing as ending plate reading. And the President likes the cameras: "I sort of like them because of that, because of law enforcement."

Most telling of all, in the Reuters/Ipsos survey from August, Republican voters were the group most in favor of the cameras, while Democrats and independents opposed them. Nationally, support has dropped to 38 percent in two separate polls. So the Republican governors and the senator leading this fight are running ahead of their own voters, not behind them. That is a more interesting sentence than "strange bedfellows," and it is the same pattern as the data centers: the argument at the top of the ticket and the argument at the county building have come apart and are moving away from each other at velocity.

## The deal in front of us

I will warn you up front that I tend to skew liberal. I will also say that this is an argument with a great deal of nuance in it, no matter where you come down on it out of the box. I do not believe there is a black-and-white answer anywhere in it, and, as I have said before (probably on these pages), anybody who tries to convince you otherwise is pushing an angle. Some of it has a lot to do with AI. Some of it has nothing to do with AI and everything to do with economics. And some of it is about privacy.

The privacy piece lands differently depending on which side of the Pacific you read it from. In New South Wales, the state government rolled out the world's first mobile phone detection cameras in 2019, then pulled the warning signs off its mobile speed cameras and watched the fines climb, until the backlash put the signs back in 2023. For Aussies, a camera by the road is a familiar argument, and the Flock fight may or may not resonate. For Americans it is a different thing. This is a country where Missouri's Supreme Court struck down St. Louis's red-light camera ordinance as unconstitutional in 2015, and where a handful of states ban speed and red-light cameras outright. The idea of a camera sitting out there around the clock, logging ordinary people minding their own business, is disturbing whether you are left or right. It does not matter whether you are soft or hard on crime, or soft or hard on immigration. Everybody has a concern.

The Wall Street Journal put the split neatly last week: in Oconee County, South Carolina, an all-Republican council's chair says "Government has got to be restrained," and in Asheville, progressives worry about the federal government using the same network against dissidents, transgender residents and immigrants. Same cameras, opposite fears, same conclusion. And the case that made a lot of people pay attention was a small one. In Brown County, Wisconsin, in April 2025, sheriff's deputies used Flock hits to establish that a man drove to Michigan, where marijuana is legal, on a regular basis, then set up a stop on I-41 for his trip home and searched his car. He was convicted of possession. That is the whole thing, and it was enough.

I think the data centers carry the same concept as the cameras. Something large, built by somebody else, arrives in your county on terms you never saw, and you live with it for decades. Opposition to local data centers went from an even split to 75 percent in about a year, and one poll this summer found support dropping by about eleven points just by describing the data center as training AI rather than running banking and streaming. The shift has been fast enough and sharp enough that I do wonder whether something beyond ordinary opinion is working on it. And the label is doing a lot of the work. Per megawatt, an AI data center is not all that different from any other (electricity in, heat out); it is the scale that is different, with AI racks drawing roughly ten times the power, and the International Energy Agency putting a typical AI-focused data center at the electricity use of 100,000 households, with the largest now under construction at twenty times that. But the reaction to the word "AI" goes far beyond what the engineering difference would justify, and that tells you most of what you need to know.

So what is the best thing to do? These facilities make real money, they spend a lot during construction, and done right they provide good jobs, union and otherwise, while they are being built. I think it comes down to being a good corporate citizen and keeping the social contract with the place you land in. Hire local trades to build it, rather than flying in outside crews and keeping the community out of that revenue. Put part of the enterprise toward the schools. Pay for your own power and water, or bring your own generation, solar or nuclear, so that you are not drawing down a grid that, embarrassingly, is old and has not been well maintained. A data center that runs on its own power is attractive to anybody who understands the grid: it protects the neighbors from price spikes and takes load off infrastructure that needs every break it can get.

The data centers that have not been transparent have fallen into exactly the same trap as the frontier labs. "AI is going to take your job" meets "we are building a huge building that will chew up the land, look terrible, push up your power bill and maybe touch the water, and the benefit goes to somebody else, somewhere else." There are constant promises of abundance, and for too many people right now the reply is, "Great, where's my abundance?" They have not seen any of it. What they have seen is money moving to the top.

Americans get fired up, but we also tend to be pragmatic, especially about the bottom line. If data centers write deals that give back to the county they sit in, lower property taxes, keep the infrastructure maintained, supply their own power where they can, and are careful with water (none of which is a no-brainer, because every one of them cuts into margin), I think local opinion turns around quickly. Loudoun County in Virginia is the proof of concept: an industry-funded study estimates that without its data centers the county would need a property tax rate nearly twice the one it charges, which on an $800,000 home is the difference between roughly $6,400 a year and $12,300. That is a benefit nobody misses.

Which brings me back to where I started. What this asks of each of us is to look at the specifics of the deal in front of us and ask whether it makes sense for where we live, for our kids, and for everybody else it touches who probably did not get a seat at the table when it was decided. That is the basis for how you respond. Not "I am completely opposed," and not "I am all in, always." Attention, judgment and principle. Apply those at the local level, at least, and you have a real chance of making things line up better than they would have without you.

## Your own ballot

The one thing I would do differently after reading all of this: **before 3 November, find out who on your own ballot actually votes on the substation, the rezoning and the camera contract.** It is not the name at the top.

**Look up the down-ballot seats first.** County commission, board of supervisors, city council, township board, and in some states the utility commission itself. Those are the people who decided Montgomery County's moratorium, Prince William County's denial and Knox County's cameras. Find out which of those seats are up this year, who is running for them, and what each candidate has said about the specific project nearest you. If they have said nothing, that is also an answer, and you are allowed to ask them to say where they stand before the vote rather than after it.

**Read the next zoning agenda.** The frontier fight will be on the news every night between now and November, and it will not change a thing about your street. The planning commission agenda will. Look for a rezoning of a few hundred acres, a large-load interconnection, a tax abatement, or a data center under whatever name the applicant chose for it. The agenda is posted ahead of the meeting, it is public, and one informed person at the microphone is harder to ignore than you might think.

**File the camera question with your city clerk, in writing.** Does your town have a license plate reader contract, with whom, when does it renew, what is the retention period, and how many searches did outside agencies run against it last year? Pflugerville found its answer through a records request. And while you are at it, ask who *else* runs cameras inside city limits, because when Asheville terminated its contract it covered 11 city cameras, and 86 more, run by other entities, kept running.

---

It is a pretty safe bet that Washington will still be arguing about the frontier in November, and it will very likely still be arguing about it the November after that. The substation, the rezoning and the camera contract will all have been decided long before that argument finishes, and the impact on where you live will start being felt in the interim.

The only open question is whether you turned up for the decision and made sure that impact was as positive on the surrounding environment as you could possibly make it.
