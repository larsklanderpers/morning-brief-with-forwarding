# They Started Without You

Your people did not wait for the program. Most of them have been using AI at work for a year or more, a lot of them on their own accounts, and many of them have never mentioned it to you.

Think about the kid who taught himself guitar off YouTube. He has been playing for two years, in his bedroom, every night. He can get through forty songs. Some of what he does is genuinely good and some of it is a habit that is going to wreck his wrist by the time he is thirty, and he cannot tell which is which, because he has never had anyone sit in the room and listen to him play. Now imagine his parents sign him up for a six-week seminar on why music is important and how exciting it is going to be when he finally picks up an instrument.

That is roughly where most AI change programs are aimed right now. The deck has a burning platform on slide four (the change-management term for the crisis you manufacture so people feel they have to move), a communications plan on slide seven, and a kickoff date on slide nine. It is a competent deck, usually built by competent people. It is also aimed at a problem most organizations no longer have.

If you want a sense of how far ahead of the program people already are, look at what happened last week to a group of contractors working for Mercor, one of the firms OpenAI uses to have humans review and grade ChatGPT's answers. 404 Media reported on 22 September that reviewers were being removed for using AI to do the review work, under rules that said, in so many words, "Do not use AI detection tools, or AI yourself." Mercor's own statement: "When we confirm an expert has used AI to complete a task, we immediately remove them." One worker described it as "pretty much the one thing that will get you kicked off ASAP." Another, who was let go, said simply, "I just needed a little boost and turned to AI to help me."

To be fair to Mercor, they have a real reason. If the people grading a model's output are quietly using a model to do the grading, the training data stops being human judgment, and that is the one thing they are being paid to supply. But sit with the human part for a second. These were people who knew the rule, knew it was enforced, and knew the penalty was the job. They used it anyway.

That is not a workforce that needs to be sold on the idea.

This is the first of a run of Tuesdays on managing with AI, and they will follow the same shape: what I think is actually hard about leading people through this, what I would do differently, and something you can use this afternoon. Today's is about the program you are probably about to run, and the hour you could spend instead.

## A playbook written for people who did not want to move

The two frameworks that most change programs are built on are Kotter's eight steps and Prosci's ADKAR, and both were designed for a very particular kind of change.

John Kotter (a Harvard Business School professor, and more or less the founding father of modern change management) laid out his eight steps in a 1995 Harvard Business Review article and the 1996 book *Leading Change*. Step one is establishing a sense of urgency. Step two is forming a guiding coalition. The ordering is the theory: the change starts at the top, the organization does not want it, and leadership's first job is to make the present uncomfortable enough that people will move. In that 1995 article, Kotter wrote that "Well over 50% of the companies I have watched fail in this first phase." The first phase, again, is creating urgency.

Hold onto that, because urgency is the one thing AI adoption has in surplus.

ADKAR is Jeff Hiatt's model, and Prosci's product: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement, each one needed before the next. It has one genuinely excellent idea in it, which I will come to. Its evidence base, though, is mostly the vendor's own benchmarking, drawn from a self-selected sample out of Prosci's own learning community, with the same person rating both the change management and whether the project worked. It has not been refuted; it has barely been tested. The only independent, quantitative test I have been able to find that checked ADKAR scores against an outcome somebody actually observed is a 2021 study of thirty broiler farms in Belgium and the Netherlands, and it found "no significant association" between the two. Thirty farms proves nothing either way. It is just the only data point on the table.

And then there is the part that should make every change consultant a little uncomfortable. Kotter replaced his own model in 2014. His firm's version now says to "Run the steps concurrently and continuously," and to drive change not with "a small, powerful core group" but with "a large volunteer army from up, down, and across the organization." The eight-step decks in most conference rooms are still teaching the 1996 sequence.

The 2014 version was built for a change that does not finish. That happens to be the one you have.

## Desire was never the barrier

ADKAR's best idea is what Hiatt calls the barrier point: find the first element that is weak, fix that one, and do not spend a dollar on anything after it until you have. If people do not want the change, training them is money down a hole. That is a sharp, practical idea, and it survives most of what can be said against the model.

It is also rarely applied honestly. Ask change practitioners which element usually breaks and the most common answer is desire. The data behind that belief is largely a poll of practitioners about which element they personally find hardest to deliver, which is a statement about the practitioners, not about the changes. It is also, on Prosci's own logic, the one element a practitioner cannot be blamed for.

Now hold that up against what your own people are doing. In 2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn surveyed thirty-one thousand knowledge workers across thirty-one countries for their Work Trend Index. Seventy-five percent said they were using AI at work, 78% of those users were bringing their own tools, and 60% of leaders said their company's leadership lacked a plan and vision for it. That is research paid for by a company that sells AI, so take it for direction rather than precision. The direction is hard to miss, though: the workforce got there first, often paid for it themselves, and did not file a ticket.

A person who puts a tool on their personal card and uses it on company work, quietly, sometimes against policy, does not have an awareness problem or a desire problem. Those were solved before your program existed. So the kickoff, the comms plan and the urgency slide are all pointed at the two elements that were never the barrier.

There is a worse version of this, too. Build a burning platform for people who are already running, and you are not motivating them; you are telling experienced professionals that the way they already work is dangerous. A 2022 paper by McLaren, van der Hoorn and Fein describes that kind of manufactured urgency as "combative (and futile)," producing anxiety and stress rather than movement.

So apply the model's own logic. If awareness and desire are done, the barrier is somewhere further down the list.

## Knowing how is free; knowing when it is wrong is not

ADKAR splits Knowledge (knowing how) from Ability (being able to actually do it). In most changes that split feels academic. In this one it is the entire game.

Knowledge is close to free. The interface is a text box, and the best evidence that your people can learn it in an afternoon is that they already did, on their own. Ability, in Hiatt's definition, is "demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels." And the required performance level here is not operating the tool. It is knowing when the output is wrong.

That turns out to be much harder than anyone budgets for, and, worse, it is invisible to the person who lacks it.

METR (a nonprofit that evaluates AI systems) ran a randomized controlled trial in 2025 with sixteen experienced open-source developers working on 246 real tasks in code they knew well. Sixteen is a small number, and I think anybody quoting this study without saying so is overselling it. With AI, the developers took 19% *longer*. Afterward, having actually been slowed down, they estimated AI had made them about 20% faster. The slowdown is interesting. The fact that they could not feel it is the part a manager should lose sleep over.

The larger studies point the same way. In a preregistered experiment with 758 BCG consultants, AI produced big gains on tasks inside its capability range, and on a task that sat just outside it, the consultants using AI were nineteen percentage points less likely to get the right answer than the ones without it. And a study of 5,172 customer-support agents, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* and measured on the firm's own records rather than self-report, found about 15% more issues resolved per hour on average, around 36% for the least experienced agents, and no significant gain, plus a small dip in quality, for the most skilled.

Put those together and a picture falls out that every manager should recognize. The tool lifts your junior people a lot. It does very little for your best people and may make some of their work slightly worse. And people in both groups have a hard time telling you which of those is happening to them.

That wrecks the standard diagnostic. An ADKAR assessment asks people to rate their own ability on a one-to-five scale, and the METR result says that self-rating is not just noisy, it is flattering, and most flattering exactly where the change is going wrong. No e-learning module fixes that, and no completion certificate measures it.

One more thing, which I have not seen a framework say out loud. The people with the most status to lose are the people the evidence says gain the least. Your most experienced person, the one you may have quietly filed under "not on board," is watching a tool help the newest hire more than it helps them, and drawing a fairly accurate conclusion about where that leaves them. That is not a desire problem. That is a correct read of the situation, and treating it as an attitude problem is a good way to lose them.

## Counting logins

Reinforcement is the last element, and the textbook advice is to anchor the change in the culture. Anchor it to what, exactly? The right practice at twelve months is often wrong at eighteen. The prompting discipline that worked on one model is wasted effort on the next, a review step that was essential becomes theater, and the task you were right to keep for yourself becomes the one you should hand off. Reinforcing last quarter's habits is not sustaining the change; it is setting it in amber. (Which, for what it is worth, is the best argument for teaching Kotter's 2014 model instead of his 1996 one.)

The more immediate problem is what gets counted. Most organizations measure AI adoption in seats and prompts, because those are the numbers the platform hands them. Both go up. Neither says anything about whether the work got better.

The number that does comes from BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab, published in *Harvard Business Review* in September 2025 from a survey of 1,004 full-time US desk workers. They gave the problem a name: workslop, AI-generated work that looks finished and is not, handed to a colleague who then has to do the actual thinking. Forty percent said they had received some in the previous month. They estimated each instance cost them an hour and fifty-one minutes to deal with, and the researchers put the cost at about $186 per employee per month.

That is adoption without reinforcement, seen from the inside. The volume goes up, the checking quietly moves to whoever receives the work, and the dashboard stays green the whole time because the dashboard counts logins. And in that same survey, managers reported receiving workslop more often than individual contributors did. The cost of everyone's unchecked output lands on the person with the least time to check it. That is you.

## What I would do instead

The proposal is not a program. It is an hour a week, and it goes after Ability, which is where the barrier actually sits.

**Cancel the next adoption push.** Whatever is queued (the awareness campaign, the lunch-and-learn, the champions network), it is aimed at awareness and desire, and you already have both. Take that hour back.

**Book one one-to-one over one real piece of work.** Not a demo and not a sandbox exercise. Something that already shipped: a customer email, a board paper, a piece of code, an analysis somebody acted on. Something whose consequences have already happened.

**Ask how they knew it was right.** Do not ask whether they used AI. That question teaches people to hide it, which is precisely the Mercor lesson, and it tells you nothing about the quality of the work. Ask what they checked, what they did not check, and how they decided which was which. If the answer is "it looked right," you have found your barrier point, on a real piece of work rather than on a self-assessment form, and you have found it without anyone getting in trouble.

**Then do it again next week with someone else.** Ten of those conversations will tell you more about where your team really stands than any survey instrument, because you are looking at the output rather than at people's confidence in it.

**Change one number on the dashboard.** Retire one usage metric and put up something about quality: rework, deliverables sent back, how often a piece of work comes back for a second pass. You will not pick the right one first time. The wrong quality metric still tells you more than a perfect count of logins.

Measured against the $186 a month in cleanup the workslop researchers estimated, an hour of your week is cheap. It does come with one catch. You cannot ask someone how they checked their AI-assisted work if you have never had to answer that question about your own.

## Start with your own meeting notes

So before the first one-to-one, practice on yourself, and the easiest place to do it is the meeting recap.

If your organization runs Teams, Zoom or Google Meet with an AI assistant switched on, you are already producing AI output every day, and most of it goes out unread. The recap lands in the channel, everyone assumes someone else checked it, and a week later two people are working to two different versions of what was agreed. It is the most common piece of workslop in most companies, and it is the one with a manager's name on it.

The fix is not to turn it off. It is to make the recap show its work, so that checking it takes five minutes instead of a re-watch. Ask for the decisions, and for each one, the line from the transcript that supports it. Something like this:

> *"From this meeting transcript, list every decision that was actually agreed, with the owner and the date if one was given. For each decision, quote the exact line from the transcript that supports it and who said it. Then, separately, list anything that sounded like a decision but was not clearly agreed, and every number or date that was mentioned, with who said it. Do not summarize the discussion."*

Then check the quotes, not the summary. If a decision has no supporting line, it is not a decision yet; it is the model's guess, and it goes back to the room as a question instead of going out as a fact. The second list (the things that sounded agreed but were not) tends to be where the real value is, because that is where the next argument was going to come from.

Do that for two weeks and a couple of things happen. You get better recaps, which is nice. More usefully, you start to develop your own feel for where these tools go wrong, which is exactly the judgment you are about to ask your team to show you. And you get a small, honest story to open the one-to-one with ("here is what I caught in my own notes last week"), which does more to make the conversation safe than any policy memo about acceptable use.

The same move then carries straight into the one-to-one itself. Before you sit down, give the piece of work to the model and ask it to help you prepare, not to judge:

> *"Here is a piece of work a member of my team produced with AI help. Do not grade it. Give me five questions I could ask the author about how they checked it, from easiest to hardest, and point out the two or three specific claims in it that I should verify myself before we talk."*

It will not always pick the right claims. Checking whether it did is, again, the point.

There are more of these at the end, including a one-line handoff note your team can put at the bottom of anything they send you.

## The part that looks like bad news

If your change program is aimed at the wrong element, that sounds like bad news. I think it is mostly good news.

The expensive part of organizational change has always been getting people to want it. It is the part that eats eighteen months and a good deal of political capital, and it is where Kotter watched more than half of his companies stall. You got that part for free. Your people did the hardest step of the model on their own time, often with their own money, sometimes at real risk to themselves, before anyone asked them to. That is not a governance failure to be cleaned up. That is a team that went out and found the thing.

What is left is the tractable part: helping capable adults work out when to trust the output, and building the habit of checking. That is a coaching problem, and coaching is what managers are for. They are not waiting for a kickoff. They are waiting for someone to sit in the room and listen to them play.

## Addendum: the Tuesday toolkit

Copy these, change them to fit how your team talks, and treat every one of them as a draft.

**1. The meeting recap that shows its work.**
> *"From this meeting transcript, list every decision that was actually agreed, with the owner and the date if one was given. For each decision, quote the exact line from the transcript that supports it and who said it. Then, separately, list anything that sounded like a decision but was not clearly agreed, and every number or date that was mentioned, with who said it. Do not summarize the discussion."*

**2. The action-item check before the recap goes out.**
> *"Here is my draft meeting recap and the transcript. Compare them. List anything in my recap that the transcript does not support, anything important in the transcript that my recap leaves out, and any action item that has no owner or no date."*

**3. Preparing the one-to-one.**
> *"Here is a piece of work a member of my team produced with AI help. Do not grade it. Give me five questions I could ask the author about how they checked it, from easiest to hardest, and point out the two or three specific claims in it that I should verify myself before we talk."*

**4. Finding what to check in your own work.**
> *"Here is something I am about to send. Identify the three statements in it most likely to be wrong or overstated, and tell me why for each. Do not rewrite it. I will check them myself."*

**5. The handoff note.** Not a prompt, a habit. Ask your team to put three short lines at the bottom of anything AI-assisted they send you, and do it yourself first:
> *Drafted with: [tool, or "no AI"]*
> *Checked: [what you actually verified, and against what]*
> *Not checked: [what you are trusting]*

The third line is the one that matters. It is also the one people will find hardest to write honestly for the first few weeks, which is exactly why it is worth asking for.
