A truck scale is a superb instrument. It is accurate to within about forty pounds, which is exactly what you want under a loaded semi weighing more than twenty tons and completely useless under an envelope weighing a fraction of an ounce.

Nobody settles an argument about an envelope by putting it on a truck scale. Yet that is, almost precisely, the shape of the argument that has run all year about artificial intelligence and employment, in the earnings calls, the conference panels and the think-tank trackers. It is also why six days of this week keep arriving at the same uncomfortable place. Monday was a regulator's control and what it actually costs. Tuesday was the consent you thought you held and the company that *actually* holds it. Wednesday was a unit price that fell while the bill went up. Thursday was a gate somebody else built on the front of the thing you depend on. Friday was a window a laboratory promised you could see through, and now says in writing is clouding over. Saturday was a thing you can buy for twenty dollars a month and what comes attached to it, and particularly, the disproportionate impact that $20 can have on your quality of life (out of all of them, I would say Saturday was fun! and was your payoff for sticking it out this week).

Six controls, and in every case the answer to *who is holding this* turned out to be somebody other than you, on terms written somewhere you were not. Today is the last one, and it is the one underneath all six. The instruments being quoted in this argument are also held by somebody else, built for a different question, and they cannot resolve the thing being argued about. Not because anyone is hiding anything. Because of how the instruments themselves were designed, forty and sixty and eighty years ago, for questions nobody was asking about language models (because, if it's not intuitively obvious, *language models didn't exist*, so the models were built around the shapes of things that *did*).

## Four instruments, four questions, and no denominator between them

Start with the reason the four numbers cannot be set against each other at all.

Four sources get stacked into a single paragraph every time this subject comes up, mine included. The Budget Lab at Yale. Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine." Indeed's Hiring Lab. ADP Research. They are quoted at each other as four readings of one quantity. Nothing in them shares a denominator.

They are not four readings of one quantity. They are four different quantities.

The Budget Lab measures the **rate of change in the occupational composition of the entire US labor force**, expressed as a dissimilarity index in percentage points, out of CPS microdata (the Current Population Survey, the monthly household survey the unemployment rate itself comes from). Stanford measures the **relative employment of twenty-two to twenty-five year olds** in high-exposure occupations against the same cohort in low-exposure occupations, inside one balanced panel of ADP payroll clients. Indeed measures the **flow of new job postings on one job board**, by occupation and advertised seniority. ADP measures a **monthly separation flow rate**, quits plus layoffs over lagged employment.

Composition share. Cohort headcount. Vacancy count. Separation rate. **No two of them share a denominator.** They cannot corroborate each other, and they cannot contradict each other. Staging them as rival camps manufactures a disagreement that neither party is having, which is a different complaint from saying anyone got their sums wrong.

Worse, three of the four say so themselves, in the documents the trackers do not quote from.

The Budget Lab, writing in May: the CPS "is best suited to analysis of broad groups and **somewhat underpowered for subgroup analysis**, like the 22-27 year old recent college graduates that have been a special focus in some AI labor market impact discussions." And then, plainly: "**If labor market effects of AI are currently limited to a narrow slice of the workforce, other datasets and research designs would be better suited to identify them.**"

Stanford, in its August revision, Fact 1: "**We find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement.** This aggregate stability is itself informative: claims of economy-wide AI-driven job losses are not visible in payroll data through June 2026."

So Yale says its instrument cannot resolve Stanford's subgroup, and Stanford says the economy-wide number is a null. **That is the same statement, made twice, from two ends.** It is agreement, and it has been reported for a year as a fight.

ADP, for its part, makes no AI claim at all. Its dynamism work contains no identification strategy for AI and one sentence noting that certain sectors "have made headlines." Putting it in a list of AI measurements does work the source itself declines to do.

## The size of the thing against the size of the ruler

Now the arithmetic, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion, which is why I want to walk you through it, because even for me it gets confusing fast. Suffice to say, the numbers tell a story, for sure, and that story may or may not be as meaningful as you expect it to be or as the media represents it as. Of course, if you are one of those impacted by the job cuts who hasn't found a new job, the impact is nearly immeasurable, and I don't want to suggest differently, even for a minute, but against the entirety of the US workforce, the implication of these numbers is just a bit different than what they are generally made out to be.

The number at the center of the argument comes from Challenger, Gray and Christmas: **116,175 announced job cuts in which AI was the reason given, January through August 2026**, from their report of 3 September. Against total US nonfarm employment of about 159 million, that is **roughly seven basis points**. Seven hundredths of one percent. Call it fourteen and a half thousand a month.

Here is what the measuring apparatus around that number looks like.

The household survey's own published significance threshold is **678,000**. That is BLS's stated figure for how large a one-month change in household employment must be before the agency will say it is statistically distinguishable from noise. It is **5.8 times** the entire eight-month AI-attributed total.

The establishment survey's ninety percent confidence interval on a monthly change is **122,000**, which is **1.05 times** the effect. The routine annual benchmark revision, at the agency's own ten-year average of 0.2 percent, is **318,000**, or **2.7 times**. Last year's final benchmark revision was **898,000**, nearly **eight times**. One month of ordinary gross separations in the US labor market is **5,351,000**, which is **forty-six times** the thing being measured.

**Six of the eight error terms I can quantify in the US statistical system are larger than the entire eight-month effect.** I want to be exact about the two that are not, because the honest version of this is stronger than the tidy one. The actual first-to-third revision to May 2026 payrolls was 109,000, which at eight months is now 0.94 times the effect rather than larger than it. And this year's preliminary benchmark revision, at 79,000, is 0.68 times it. Those two crossed over during August, as the AI-attributed total accumulated and the monthly run rate fell. **The survey instruments, the ones that actually estimate employment rather than correct it after the fact, all remain larger.**

And the claim this supports is narrower than the one you will see made with these numbers, so let me state it precisely. **The defensible sentence is not that AI displacement is smaller than the error bars.** It is that *an effect of this magnitude, if it existed as a net change in employment, could not be detected by these instruments.* That is a statement about the instruments. It is true whether the effect is real or not, and it is true whether the effect is ten thousand jobs or a million. It is the truck scale against the envelope, in a statistical frame.

One more, and it is the paragraph I keep coming back to. **On 6 August, Challenger reported 112,713 AI-attributed cuts across seven months. On 7 August, BLS revised away 103,000 jobs across two months.** The correction was roughly nine-tenths the size of the phenomenon, it covered two-sevenths of the period, and it landed the following morning.

And then, because I am not going to show you a revision running one way and leave it there: **on 4 September, BLS revised those same two months back up.** June up 11,000, July up 44,000, **55,000 higher combined than previously reported.** August itself came in at 162,000, against an average monthly gain of **31,000** over the prior twelve months. Regardless of BLS' competence or accuracy, the key point is that the stated job loss number from Challenger is roughly equivalent to *a BLS adjustment.* Certainly in comparison to the 159 million jobs across the economy, the two sets of numbers are "much of a muchness", as a friend of mine would have it.

**That is the point rather than a concession to it.** Inside five weeks the same instrument moved the same two months down by 103,000 and then up by 55,000, and printed a month nearly five times its own twelve-month average. Both moves are ordinary. Both are comparable in size to the entire phenomenon under discussion. **An instrument that corrects itself by the width of the thing you are trying to see cannot be used to see it, and that is true in whichever direction the correction happens to run.**

## Fifty households out of sixty thousand

The passage that changed how I think about all of this does not come from a critic. It comes from BLS, in a report to the Appropriations Committees on modernizing the Current Population Survey, published in April.

> "At current sample sizes, a net change of fewer than 50 survey responses could move the headline unemployment rate by 0.1 percentage point."

> "For example, 20 years ago the CPS could detect that an over-the-month change in the unemployment rate as small as 0.18 percentage point was statistically significant, while now it would take two months of data before one could determine that this same change is statistically significant."

The CPS sample is **60,000 eligible households, the same as in 1981**, against a US population up sixty-one percent since. BLS prices the fix at around sixty million dollars a year and warns that proceeding without it would put the estimates at considerable risk.

**Fifty households out of sixty thousand move the number the entire argument is conducted in.**

It is getting worse, and the deterioration is documented rather than inferred. The CPS response rate has gone from **86.4 percent in February 2016 to 65.9 percent in February 2026**, with a series low of 64.0 percent in November 2025. The establishment survey has gone from **60.0 percent to 43.3 percent** over the same decade, which means fewer than half of sampled establishments now respond at all. In case you don't remember this because the media hasn't had a lot to say about it for quite a while, **October 2025 does not even exist**: BLS's own language is that "CPS data were not collected for October 2025 due to the lapse in appropriations and were not collected retroactively." JOLTS, CPI, PPI and the October Employment Situation went with it.

And one detail that I want to bring particular attention to, which is checkable in the BLS archives by anyone who wants to: **the stated confidence interval on the monthly change in household unemployment widened by forty-two percent in a single step**, from plus or minus 300,000 through 9 January 2026 to plus or minus 425,000 from 11 February onward, with the unemployment-rate interval going from 0.2 to 0.3 percentage points on identical conditioning. The ruler got coarser -- a lot coarser -- and the argument did not notice or change as a result.

## What landed in a single week at the BLS

In the last week of August, three federal releases landed within two days of each other, and between them they say the quiet part in the agency's own words.

**The preliminary benchmark revision, Friday 28 August.** Total nonfarm, March 2026: **−79,000, or −0.1 percent**, against a ten-year absolute average of 0.2 percent. Total private, −178,000. The release describes itself, verbatim, as "a preliminary measure of **the total error** in CES employment estimates from March 2025 to March 2026." It offers no decomposition of that error at all. **The instrument reports a residual and declines to say what is in it.**

The second-order point is better than the first. The 2024 preliminary benchmark came in at −818,000 and the final landed at −598,000. **The correction to the correction (in 2024, when it is acknowledged that the measurement was more accurate than it is now) was 220,000 jobs, roughly twice the entire AI-attributed total for this year.** Again, the entirety of this population can be described within a historic adjustment that moved the numbers only a handful of basis points. And a fact that cuts hard against the direction most people want to take this: **Information, the most AI-exposed supersector there is (supersector being the BLS's top-level industry grouping), was revised UP by 87,000, or plus three percent, the largest percentage revision in the table in either direction.** Professional and business services came down only 76,000, three-tenths of a percent. The large downward revisions sit in retail trade and in education and health. **So the benchmark did not show that the white-collar sectors had been overstated. On the one sector the displacement case names first, it showed the opposite.**

I have to immediately complicate my own exhibit, though, because the honest version of this cuts three ways rather than two. **In the August Employment Situation, published 4 September, information employment fell by 23,000, following losses averaging 8,000 a month over the prior twelve.** So the benchmark re-anchored the March 2026 *level* of that sector upward by 87,000, and the monthly series has that same sector shedding jobs through August. **Both are true, they are different quantities measured on different clocks, and anybody who quotes one at you without the other is either not paying attention or is misrepresenting the truth for their own purposes.** Which, if you have read this far, you will recognize as the entire argument of this piece arriving on my own side of the table, which it generally should, since it's my piece (just kidding). Regardless, what other numbers are there to consider? If we know the instruments are flawed for the purposes to which they are being addressed, surely there must be a better one somewhere?

**Worker Displacement, 2023 to 2025, Thursday 27 August.** This is the Displaced Worker Survey, the one federal instrument that measures displacement by name. Here is its complete closed-response set for why the job ended: plant or company closed down or moved; insufficient work; position or shift abolished; seasonal job completed; self-operated business failed; some other reason. Aside from the largest of these being "position or shift abolished" -- which could mean almost anything -- not really much of a help.

Maybe something with AI categorization as its baseline?

**There is no technology category. No automation category. No AI category. There is no causal-mechanism dimension in the instrument at all.** A job eliminated by a language model is recorded as "position or shift abolished," in the same bin as offshoring, restructuring, a private-equity cost program and an ordinary reorganization. That bin is **44.4 percent of long-tenured displaced workers**, the largest of the three. AI, automation and technology are mentioned **zero times** in the release and its technical note.

No sample size fixes a missing question. You could triple the survey and the answer would be exactly as unavailable. How about Employment Projections, then, does that give us any insight that we might be able to extrapolate from better than what we can observe as ground truth now?

**Employment Projections 2025 to 2035, also Thursday 27 August.** BLS declined to incorporate AI-driven productivity acceleration into its ten-year projections, on the record, because doing so "would be **highly speculative**" and uncertainty about potential impacts "remains **very high**."

That is the third instrument saying, in a technical note, that it is not measuring this. Nope.

## The three things that make this an argument and not just me tilting at windmills

I am going to spend a section arguing against myself, because without it the above is just a comfortable story for people who would rather not think about any of this (there's no directed number, therefore it's all made up, therefore I don't need to make it my problem).

**First, the displacement survey's results are not null.** Long-tenured displacement rose about twenty-nine percent. The share of displaced workers reemployed at equal or better pay fell from around sixty-two percent to around forty-nine percent. Something real and bad happened to displaced workers in that window. My claim is that the instruments cannot *attribute* it, not that nothing occurred, and **the silence of an instrument is not evidence that the effect is small.** If you take one thing from this section, take that sentence rather than the arithmetic.

**Second, BLS does name AI, twelve times, in both directions.** So "the instruments say nothing about AI" is not sustainable for the projections. The accurate version is narrower: BLS makes qualitative, hedged, directional judgments about AI while explicitly declining to quantify its effect. Watch the asymmetry in the agency's own verbs, which is a tell worth borrowing: AI "is expected to support" demand, confidently, and "may dampen" it, hedged.

**Third, the numbers here have a directional problem for me, not just for the other side.** The projections show the three occupational groups everybody seems to mean by "entry-level white collar" **growing by 279,500 over the decade**, up 0.78 percent. Business and financial operations up 628,600 and computer and mathematical up 403,100, against office and administrative support down 752,100. That is composition shifting from clerical toward analytical, inside an aggregate that is roughly flat. It is not a contraction story, and I am not going to write it as one, though there is an argument that is an opportunity story, but we can leave that for another time, or you can refer back to my earlier pieces about pulling up the ladder behind us. If these numbers make anything seem more tractable, it is that the ladder has been being pulled up for a while, and that AI is likely just accelerating that process, not creating it.

And while we are in that table, one more thing, which I think is the single cleanest illustration of everything above. **CPS Table 11b puts those same three occupational groups at 33,328,000 people. The Employment Projections base puts them at 35,654,800.** Same three groups, same year, same agency, **2.3 million apart**, about seven percent, roughly twenty times the entire AI-attributed announcement total. Neither is wrong. They are built on different universes on purpose, one counting persons who report an occupation and the other constructing jobs. **You can hold two BLS publications side by side and they cannot agree on how many people are in the population the argument is about.** And that is what "no shared denominator" looks like once you go and check.

## The month the streak ended, and the cattle shortage

The fourth instrument gets off lightest in most coverage, because its number is the one the argument wants.

Challenger's August report, published 3 September, contains a genuinely newsworthy fact that got almost no pickup. **AI fell to the fourth most-cited reason for announced cuts in August, with 3,462, its lowest monthly total since December 2025.** That ended a five-month run as the leading monthly reason, beginning in March. Year to date, AI remains number one at 116,175, about twenty-two percent of all cuts. **Both halves are true and neither one is the story on its own.**

The AI share of the month's announced cuts went from 32.8 percent in July to 6.5 percent in August, a fivefold drop, in a month when total announced cuts rose fifty-eight percent.

Three readings are available, and the tracker cannot distinguish between them. Fewer cuts were actually driven by AI. The same cuts were described differently. Or the month's cuts genuinely landed in food and consumer products rather than technology. **And that indistinguishability sits inside the dataset the argument is built on.**

It is not a cheap shot, either, because Challenger, who publishes the number, proposed the mechanism himself, in July, and has not retracted it: "**Naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors while pushing current and prospective employees away. That's why the messaging has swung from hedging to aggressively citing it.**" August is the first month consistent with that swing running in reverse, and Challenger's August commentary is about hiring velocity and manufacturing. The AI development sits in the body text, uncommented. I am reading that as an observation, not an accusation, but it certainly seems like a substantial change in tone and in the way industries are positioning AI (which I could argue is probably ALSO representative of a greater sensitivity to the political issues around AI but that's a topic for another day).

Two more details from that report, and I offer them as texture rather than evidence. Challenger's separate "Technological Update" category recorded **20,219 cuts for all of 2025 and twelve so far in 2026**, while the AI category took on 116,175. The categories moved; the phenomenon may or may not have. And the sixth most-cited reason for announced job cuts in August 2026, at 2,500, was a **cattle shortage**. That is a concrete reminder of what this instrument is: a tally of whatever companies announced, not a measurement pointed at technology.

The report still carries no sampling frame, no confidence interval, no revision policy and no reconciliation of announcements to actual separations. It has acquired exactly one line of method, a footnote to Table 1, and it is worth having: "**Some reductions are announced as a range or over multiple years; Challenger records the announced figure.**" A multi-year plan books entirely, at announcement, in the month it is announced.

**A number with no error bars is not more precise than one with wide ones. It is less.**

## How long has this ever taken

There is a question a scientist would ask first, and I have not seen it put to a single panel on this subject. How long has it ever taken, historically, for a general-purpose technology's displacement effect to become statistically visible?

Electrification: the first central station opened in 1882, under five percent of factory mechanical drive was electrified in 1899, fifty percent diffusion arrived around 1919, and the measurable productivity acceleration is dated 1919 to 1929. Paul David (the economic historian who wrote the definitive account of why electrification took forty years to show up in the numbers), peer-reviewed in 1990: factory electrification did not reach full fruition "before the early 1920s. **This was four decades after the first central power station opened for business.**"

Computers: Solow's paradox (the observation that computers were visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics) was formulated in 1987, two decades in, and the software J-curve literature was still describing effects that "began in the 1990s and have not waned" as of 2017.

Industrial robots: the robot stock quadrupled between 1993 and 2007, the outcome window in the canonical study runs 1990 to 2007, and it was published in 2020. **The coefficient, after all of that, was two-tenths of a percentage point** on the employment-to-population ratio per robot per thousand workers.

ATMs: installed from the 1970s. Tellers per urban branch fell from twenty to thirteen between 1988 and 2004, while urban bank branches grew forty-three percent, and full-time-equivalent teller employment **rose** at about two percent a year from 2000. The aggregate sign was positive throughout the period remembered as the automation of bank tellers.

Large language models arrived in November 2022. The best identified estimate covers two years. The best descriptive series covers forty-three months.

**A commentator in 1985, with fifteen years of ATM data already behind him, would have had precisely nothing to work with, and would have been wrong in both directions at once. The same commentator, in 1885, would have had to question whether electricity was even truly a transformation product.**

And I will add the sentence that makes this a claim rather than a shrug: there is, as of today, **no peer-reviewed identified estimate of LLM employment displacement in existence.** The peer-reviewed literature on generative AI and work contains essentially one setting-specific productivity result. Everything on employment is working-paper stage, and both of the leading papers carry their authors' own causal disclaimers.

## None of this means nothing is happening

This is the turn, and if the piece stopped one section ago it would be a debunk. That is not what I think is true.

Four datasets, built from completely different raw material, agree on one thing. Stanford: the gap "operates primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations." A Harvard team, working from résumé data covering sixty-five million workers at more than two hundred and eighty thousand firms: "driven primarily by slower hiring rather than increased separations." ADP: separations at a nine-year low. Indeed: seventy-one percent of the net increase in postings between May 2025 and May 2026 was in senior roles.

**Four instruments, one mechanism, and it runs through the hiring margin at the junior end.**

Which is exactly the mechanism every instrument above is structurally worst at seeing. Separation-rate measures are blind to it by construction, because nobody separates. A hiring freeze and a healthy labor market produce nearly identical aggregate statistics for about two years. And the one cohort where it would show up first is the one the Budget Lab has told us, in writing, its own data source is underpowered to resolve.

So the honest position is uncomfortable in both directions at once. The claim that AI is visibly destroying employment is not supported by any instrument capable of supporting it. And the aggregates staying calm is precisely what year three of a general-purpose technology is supposed to look like whether or not something real is under way. The Danish authors who produced the best-known null result **retitled their own paper** to stop it being read as "nothing is happening," and closed with: "Technological change reshapes work well before it surfaces in earnings or hours."

This is where it all gets a bit twisty for me. Anecdotally, I think almost anyone in the technology space would acknowledge that there is *incredible* pressure to do more with less, and there always has been. AI compounded that very heavily early on -- the narrative about not needing developers any more because AI could "do it all" was a very infectious thought meme. There are now two different paths being discussed, much like our much-maligned "K-shaped economy" here in the US: the belief on the part of Chief Executives and CFOs that the failure to accomplish more cheaper faster with less resource is a management failure within their team, rather than an effective impossibility at the moment, and the belief on the part of most of the technologists in the space (CTOs and founders like me, I mean) that the technology is absolutely a game changer in many, many ways, but that a lot of what it does is move work around to different places in the business.

And I will add to that my own take, which I have seen a number of thought leaders discussing of late -- "AI hasn't broken your business processes, it has highlighted where they have been broken all along." I think that one of the things that is really impactful at this particular moment in time is that most technology-facing businesses have a large restructuring on the horizon (or already present, depending on your industry), and that some of those businesses -- and I am particularly thinking of consulting companies, and accountants, and lawyers -- are going to have to fundamentally rebuild their business models to stay alive. But what I do not believe is that the rebuild means less people, because I think the human element will become even more important. I just think those companies will become far more dependent on soft skills than they have tried to be for years, and that will require they emphasize the quality of their own HITL (human in the loop -- the person who actually reviews what the model produced) and why it delivers a better outcome than the AI alone.

There's an opportunity here for me to talk about how all this relates to the failures of measurement that we have discussed many times on these pages in other contexts -- the audit that missed the thing until it was reconsidered, the piece of code that was approved by everyone right down the line until an aggressor action was performed against it, all of those different concepts. I am rather going to go a different direction, one that is driven by my own personal experience. Two of my kids are squarely in the employment zone that no one can seem to agree how badly AI is impacting it; and in both cases, they have been badly impacted in the last four years, and have had to change on the fly. Only one, though, is because of AI; the other is because of the failure of measurement and the political impacts thereto, and I would actually argue that we are rapidly getting to a place where the politics around AI are becoming more important than the direct economic effects because the blast radius of their impact is so large. That's a topic for another day, though I will explore some of it on Tuesday, and I will also be talking further about the economic impacts on Thursday.

## One question and one date

So, two things to take away from all of this (and a crap-ton of statistics, and we all know what Disraeli and Twain had to say about those), and neither of them takes long.

**When somebody hands you one of these numbers, ask what it is divided by.** Not whether the number is right. Whether it has a denominator, and what is in it. If the answer is a composition index, it cannot tell you about a cohort. If it is a cohort, it cannot tell you about the economy. If it is postings on one job board, it is a statement about advertising. If it is announcements scraped from press releases, it is a tally of what companies said, with a cattle shortage in row six. **Every one of those four is a perfectly good instrument for the question it was built for, and none of them was built for the question in the room.** And if the mechanism really does run through the hiring margin, then your own requisition and offer data is a better leading indicator than any of the four, arrives monthly, and you already own it. This is a thirty-second question and I have yet to see it asked on a panel, and it's *certainly* not something I have seen the media looking at -- presumably because it's a bit too subtle for most common consumers to turn into action.

**Then put one date in your calendar, and go and look for yourself.** The February 2027 Employment Situation carries the final benchmark revision for March 2026, and it will tell you how far off the −79,000 preliminary estimate was. Last time that gap was 220,000 jobs, roughly twice the size of the entire phenomenon under discussion. You do not need permission, a subscription, an analyst or a vendor briefing to read it. It is published at half past eight in the morning on a fixed date and it is free. However, you don't need to wait until then -- the September 2026 Employment Situation publishes on Friday 2 October, and you can run the same exercise on it for a credible perspective on ground truth.

## The table this lands in

Here is the test, and I want to leave you with it rather than with a conclusion, because a conclusion is the one thing this subject cannot currently support. The claim on the table is a fifty percent reduction in entry-level white-collar employment over a window closing in May 2030. Roughly sixteen of those sixty months have elapsed. The denominator, built from CPS Table 11b, is about **2.4 million people aged twenty to twenty-four** in office and administrative support, business and financial operations, and computer and mathematical occupations. The claim is therefore that this figure reads about **1.2 million in May 2030**. It is not refuted today. It has, however, spent its slack: on the like-for-like measure it now needs roughly a fourfold sustained acceleration, measured on the most exposed slice of that cohort, beginning soon and holding for four years. A back-loaded path is a legitimate structure for a diffusion claim and the people calling it a complete fantasy at the quarter mark are making a stronger claim than their own evidence supports. What do I mean -- I mean that getting from 2.4 million down to 1.2 million is 1.2 million jobs, and May 2030 is about forty-four months away. That is roughly **27,000 jobs a month, every month, for nearly four years**. The AI-attributed announcement tally is running at about **14,500 a month**, which is a little over half the required rate -- and that tally counts announcements rather than jobs actually gone, so if anything it flatters the case. It is not really a number any of us wants to "strive" for, but while it is achievable, it would require a sustained dropoff not yet visible in any instrument available to us.

But the number lands in a public table, on a fixed date, whoever is right. **CPS Table 11b, May 2030, three occupational groups, ages twenty to twenty-four, against a 2.4 million baseline.** Anyone can check it, and it is the one weight in this argument that the scale was actually built to take. That is the whole of what I want from this argument: not that we settle it now with instruments that cannot settle it, but that the people making the claims put the table, the date and the number on the record, the way the agency that publishes the data has already named its own margin of error, its own falling response rates and its own missing month. **Four organizations publish four numbers to the decimal point. Not one of those numbers is an instrument for the question being argued about.** Both sentences are true at once, and the people who publish the numbers say so themselves: the Budget Lab in its own footnote, Stanford in its own Fact 1, BLS in a report to Congress and in a technical note. **It is in the quoting that the second sentence goes missing.** The failure may be real, it may not be, none of us know; but everyone who parrots that number needs to attribute it, and the attribution needs to be the same, and it needs to be binding. Otherwise, it is no longer me tilting at windmills...
