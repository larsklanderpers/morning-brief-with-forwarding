Parents buy a thing to switch the internet off at bedtime. To set it up, somebody types an email address into a form.

That form is the whole of this piece, and the piece does not really begin until about five years later, inside a company that parent had never heard of.

Last Saturday was about a number you have to go and measure yourself, because nobody else is measuring it. This Saturday is about something quieter than that. It is about a thing you are already holding, that you never measured, because it never occurred to you that you had it.

## An employee answered the phone

In March of this year, Aura published a statement about a security incident. Aura sells identity theft protection, which is the sort of detail that got the story most of its coverage, and the irony is real, and it is also the least interesting thing about what happened. So here is that clause, spent, and we can move on.

What actually happened is small. One employee was, in Aura's own words, *"the victim of a targeted phone phishing attack."* Somebody called them, got them to hand over access to their account, and had that access for *"approximately one hour."* Aura's product was not touched. The company says so directly, and says the employee in question *"did not have access to any of the systems or databases that support our online safety and identity theft protection product."* I have no reason to doubt any of that, and it does not help.

Because in that hour, roughly 900,000 records went out the door. Names and email addresses mostly, and in some cases home addresses, phone numbers and IP addresses. Have I Been Pwned, which indexed the data independently and counted 903,100 accounts, also lists **customer service records** among the exposed fields. That category appears nowhere in either of Aura's two statements, which is worth noticing on its own, and I will come back to it.

## Who the rest of them were

Here is the number that turns this from an incident into an argument.

Of those roughly 900,000 records, Aura says fewer than 20,000 belonged to active Aura customers, and fewer than 15,000 to former ones. Fewer than 35,000 people out of about 900,000 had ever had any relationship with the company whose name is on the breach notice.

So who were the rest?

Aura's press release describes them as *"a contact list from a company Aura acquired in 2021"* and stops there. It does not name the company. That is the page nearly all the coverage quoted, and it is why this story got filed as a breach story rather than as what it is.

Aura published a second page, separately, and that one names it. The data came from *"a sales and marketing database used by Circle Media Labs, Inc. ('Circle')."* Circle is a parental controls company. Aura bought it in December 2021 and said so at the time, calling it *"the #1 parental control and screen time management solution for families."*

Which brings us back to the form. The overwhelming majority of the people in that file were not Aura customers who got unlucky. They were people who, at some point, gave their details to a company that made a product for keeping kids off the internet at bedtime, and then a completely different company bought that company, and the list came with it, in a transaction those people were not party to, did not hear about, and had no mechanism to object to even if they had.

Neither of Aura's statements says how old those records are, or when they were collected. I could not establish it from anywhere else either, so I am not going to guess at it, and you should treat any number you see attached to it with suspicion.

## The obligation did not stay with the seller

There is an instinct, when you read something like this, to file it under bad luck. A list came across in a deal, it sat there, somebody got phished, that is life.

It is not, actually, how any of the relevant law sees it, and the clearest statement of that is six years old and comes from a regulator rather than from anybody selling anything.

In October 2020 the UK's Information Commissioner fined Marriott £18.4 million over a breach of the Starwood guest reservation database. Marriott bought Starwood in 2016. The intrusion had begun in July 2014, two years before the deal, and was not discovered until September 2018, two years after it. Marriott's position, recorded in the penalty notice, was that during the acquisition it had been *"only able to carry out limited due diligence on the Starwood data processing systems and databases."*

The Commissioner's answer to that is one sentence: *"The need for a controller to conduct due diligence in respect of its data operations is not time-limited or a 'one-off' requirement."*

That is a legal finding, but it works better as an operating instruction, because that is really what it is. Diligence is not something you perform at close and then have performed. The data arrives, and the obligation arrives with it, and the obligation keeps running for as long as you hold the data, whether or not anybody has looked at it since.

Australian readers have the same principle in a blunter form. APP 11.2 requires an entity to take reasonable steps to destroy or de-identify personal information once it is no longer needed for any purpose for which it may be used or disclosed. The OAIC's guidance is explicit that this bites wherever the entity has *"the right or power to deal with"* the information, which is a deliberately wide test. There is no carve-out for information you did not collect. There is no carve-out for information you forgot you had. The duty is continuous, and it does not care how the data got to you.

And now the part where Aura's case is harder than Marriott's, not easier.

Marriott inherited a system that was already compromised on the day the deal closed. There was something there to find, and the ICO's view was that Marriott should have looked harder. Aura, as far as anything published shows, inherited a database that was completely fine. Nothing was wrong with it in December 2021. Nothing was wrong with it in 2022, or 2023, or 2024. It became a liability on a Tuesday in March 2026 when somebody in a different department answered a phone call.

There was no moment at which better diligence would have found a problem, because for five years there was not a problem. There was only a holding. That is the harder case, and I would suggest it is also by far the more common one.

## Circle is still selling today

The obvious explanation for how a thing like this survives five years is that everybody forgot about it. A dead company, an abandoned list, a folder nobody opened.

That explanation is wrong here, and the way it is wrong is the actually useful part.

Circle is not dormant. meetcircle.com is live as I write this, selling a parental controls subscription, and the footer reads *"© 2026 Circle Media Labs, Inc., an Aura company."* The brand was not shut down and quietly warehoused. It was integrated, and it kept trading, and it is trading right now.

Which means the sales and marketing database was never orphaned. It was attached to a going concern the entire time, and it had an owner on the org chart the entire time. And that, rather than neglect, is why nobody ever looked at it: **a live system with a current owner does not generate a review.** Nothing about it was overdue, because nothing about it was scheduled. Nothing about it was flagged, because there was nothing to flag it against.

That is the part I want to separate out carefully, because there is a much more familiar failure that looks like this one and is not it. The familiar failure is the disclosure nobody reads: the thing is written down, in the document, on the page, and the reader does not get to it. This is not that. **There is no document here to fail to read.** No artifact was ever produced that says *we took on a marketing database with roughly nine hundred thousand people in it, and here is what it is for.* You cannot find it by looking harder, because looking harder is a thing you do to records, and no record was created. The holding entered the company as a side effect and was never written down as anything at all.

This is also why the certifications do not catch it. Aura's own incident page lists ISO 27001, SOC 2 Type II and PCI-DSS. Every one of those is a real control framework and I am not knocking any of them. None of them asks the question *what did you take on, and is it still for anything.* They ask whether the things you are doing are done properly. A dataset you are not doing anything with is, by that measure, in perfect compliance.

## Now your side of it

Stand on the other side of it now, because most of us are on both.

Whatever you run, you are holding data you did not choose to collect. It does not take an acquisition. A team got merged into yours and their shared drive came along. A product was sunset and the customer records stayed, because deleting them felt irreversible and keeping them felt free. An agency you stopped using in 2022 handed back an export. Somebody ran an event, once, and the attendee list is still in a folder with their name on it.

None of that was a decision. That is the thing worth sitting with. Every one of those holdings arrived as a side effect of a decision about something else, and at the moment it arrived it went into the ledger with a plus sign in front of it, because a contact list is an asset. Nothing in the ordinary run of business ever goes back and revisits that sign. The asset depreciates to roughly nothing over five years. The liability does not depreciate at all. It sits at full value, indefinitely, waiting for an hour like Aura's.

No, a marketing list is not a credit file, and I am not going to pretend the stakes here are what they would be if this had been the identity vault. Aura is right about that and says so, and Have I Been Pwned notes that 90% of the addresses were already in earlier leaks. But 900,000 people had their details moved between companies without a conversation, and the field list that HIBP published includes customer service records, which means somebody's complaint, in their own words, about a product they bought for their kid. Nobody consented to that going anywhere.

## Ask what it is still for

Three things, and the first two take a Saturday morning.

**Name the last thing your part of the organization absorbed.** Not the biggest, the most recent. A merged team, a migration, a sunset product, a vendor you stopped using, an acquisition if you had one. There is always one, and you will think of it faster than you expect to. Whatever came with it in the way of names, addresses and contact details is what we are talking about.

**Then ask what it is still for, and time the answer.** This is the whole test and it is deliberately crude. If the answer to *what is this data for* takes longer than one sentence, it is not for anything. It is being kept, which is a different verb, and the reason it is being kept is that nobody ever had to say out loud that they wanted it. A holding nobody can justify in a sentence is a holding nobody would have chosen to acquire on purpose.

**Then change what happens next time, because that is the only part that lasts.** The reason inherited data accumulates is that it arrives with no expiry and no owner who has to renew it. So give it both. When something comes across in a merge, a migration or a deal, the data that comes with it gets a date on which a named person has to say *yes, still needed* -- and if nobody says it, it goes. That is not a policy project. It is one line added to whatever transition checklist you already use, and it converts an indefinite liability into one that has to be actively renewed by somebody willing to put their name on it.

The nice thing about the third one is that it is cheap precisely because it is boring. Nobody will fight you over it, because nobody in the room wants the old list either. They have just never been asked.

## What it costs to keep

The economics of this are strange, and they are strange in a way that is worth naming, because the strangeness is what makes it invisible.

A contact list acquired in a transaction has a value on the day it arrives, and that value decays fast. Five years on, a sales and marketing database from a company you absorbed is worth close to nothing operationally. But the exposure attached to it does not decay with the value. It stays exactly the size it was, and in Aura's case it turned out to be more than twenty-five times the size of the company's own customer exposure. The cheapest thing in the whole transaction, the line item nobody negotiated, ended up being the entire story.

You will not find that anywhere in your risk register, because your risk register is organized around things you are doing. This is a thing you are merely holding, inherited from a decision somebody else made, in a room you were not in, about a business that is not the one you are running today. It has an owner who has never been asked about it, no review date, and no purpose anybody has had to state out loud, and the only moment it will ever ask you for attention is an hour you do not get to schedule. So go and find out what it is still for. If nobody can tell you in a sentence, you already have your answer.
