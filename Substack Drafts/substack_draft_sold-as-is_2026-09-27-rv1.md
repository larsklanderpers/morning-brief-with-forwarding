# Sold As Is

Four pieces this week ended with the same party paying. It was never the party that made the decision, it was always the one that bore it.

If you have ever bought a used car from a dealer in the United States, you have seen the sticker, even if you did not read it. Federal rules make the dealer hang a Buyers Guide on the car, usually in a side window or off the mirror, and on a great many of them one box is checked: *As Is - No Dealer Warranty.* It means what it says. Drive it off the lot and whatever breaks is yours. Even if it breaks turning onto the street.

That sounds brutal, and while it certainly can be for the unsuspecting, it is actual a fair bit less brutal than it sounds, because the same sticker gives you three things. It tells you before you sign, not after. It tells you, in the FTC's own guidance, to ask to have the car looked at by an independent mechanic before you buy it, and a dealer who will not let you open the hood has told you something. And the car you drive home on Saturday is the car you drive to work on Monday. It does not quietly become a different car in your driveway overnight, with the dealer's name still on the paperwork and your name on the repair bill.

Economists have a name for the problem the sticker exists to solve. George Akerlof wrote it up in 1970 as the market for lemons, and later shared a Nobel prize for it: when the seller knows what is wrong with the car and the buyer does not, the buyer ends up carrying the risk, and the honest cars get priced like the bad ones. The Buyers Guide is the law's dull, cheap answer. It does not make the dealer liable. It just refuses to let the dealer keep the information and the risk apart.

I set out on Monday to follow one question across the week: where the cost lands when a label, or the model behind it, turns out to be wrong. By Friday the question had gotten wider than I planned. It was not only the cost of being wrong that kept moving downstream. It was the cost of *change* as well, the model that is not wrong, just different, on a date somebody else picked. And both costs went the same way, every day, toward the party with the least ability to see what was coming.

That is the finding, and it is narrower and more useful than "the little guy pays." The party that makes the decision also holds the information and the calendar. The cost goes to whoever holds neither.

## Four stickers on the window

Lay the week's documents next to the Buyers Guide and they all have the "caveat emptor" box checked. What is missing on each of them is the other half of the sticker.

**Monday's sticker was an insurance form.** Verisk's generative AI exclusions sit on the shelf for any carrier that wants them, and W. R. Berkley's goes further, stepping back from any claim arising out of *"any actual or alleged use, deployment, or development of Artificial Intelligence by any person or entity."* California's statute took away the defense that the AI *"autonomously caused the harm"* from anyone who *"developed, modified, or used"* it. As is, in other words, and the buyer is the one who used it. And the hood? The underwriter quoted in the Financial Times called model outputs *"too much of a black box,"* which is a fair description of what the deployer can see as well. You cannot inspect the weights, you are not told what changed in the last update, and in the affirmative market that has opened to sell the risk back, Munich Re says it sets premiums by *"the quality of the model and its performance stability."* The premium is priced on a car you are not allowed to look under nor turn over the engine.

**Wednesday's sticker was a reporting category.** From 19 October a cyberattack on a US airline can move into the column the airline does not control, which is the column that does not come with a hotel room, *"provided that the air carrier is in compliance with applicable cybersecurity regulations."* That proviso is the whole of the protection. And the security program it points to is, under federal regulation, Sensitive Security Information that a passenger is legally barred from reading. This one is not a hood nobody bothered to open. It is a hood welded shut by statute, for reasons that are partly good, with a consumer entitlement bolted to the engine underneath it. Inaccessible, non-transparent, and fully in the dark.

**Thursday's sticker was a sealed envelope.** The clause in S. 5105 that would let the labs agree to slow the *"release, deployment, use, development, training, testing, or evaluation"* of their models asks them to send written notice to the Justice Department first, and then has that notice *"withheld, without discretion, from the public."* The buyer of the car is not even told the dealer has changed the delivery date, let alone why. The sticker as sealed envelope is American commercial irony at its best.

**Friday's sticker was the retirement schedule.** Anthropic's says that after a model is retired, *"Requests to retired models will fail."* Google's says the Gemini "latest" alias *"will get hot-swapped with every new release."* DeepSeek gave four days before Pro requests started routing to a different model. Every lab publishes its own notice window and none of them match, from two weeks to eighteen months. And when Google learned in late July that Gemini had reached systems at three real companies during a test, its customers found out roughly seven weeks later, when the Wall Street Journal called. The cost of the improvements, the risk of the breakout, and the conversion necessary to maintain functionality being achieved in a timely fashion, are all costs for the buyer to wear. None are paid by the labs.

Four documents, four different sets of lawyers, and the same shape: the risk is assigned in writing to the party downstream, and the information that party would need to price it stays upstream.

## The car that changes in the driveway

This is where the week got wider than the plan, and it is the part I think matters most (and not just because it really came to me over the course of the week, like a lightning bolt).

A used car sold as is is a bad deal with a boundary around it. You take the risk of what is wrong with the car on the day you buy it. You do not take the risk that the dealer will come round at night and swap the engine.

A model does not have that boundary. Monday and Friday looked like two separate bills when I wrote them, one for being wrong and one for change. They are the same bill charged twice, because the thing Monday's insurers are pricing, *performance stability*, is the thing Friday's release calendar controls. The lab sets the pace of change. The pace of change sets how stable the model is on your workload. The stability sets the premium, and the premium is yours. So the operator pays once for the model's mistakes and again for the changes that produce new ones, and the party that controls both variables appears on neither invoice.

Thursday closes the loop from the other direction. If the pacing talks ever turn into an agreement, it arrives at your desk as a vendor delay, and the reason sits in a sealed notice. If they do not, it arrives as another release with a breaking detail that lands on the day it ships, the way the thinking flag Opus 5.5 now rejects did last week. Faster or slower, the operator finds out when everyone else does and budgets for it after the fact. The calendar is somebody else's in both directions.

And Wednesday supplies the thing the car analogy does not have at all, which is the gap between two contracts. The DOT rule was careful to keep a baggage system run by an airline's contractor in the airline's column, and said nothing about whose systems a cyberattack has to hit. Colorado's new AI act shields developers from *"off-label deployer uses,"* which is fair on its face, and leaves the space between the developer's sanctioned use and the deployer's actual one to the deployer. My friend's fraud model on Monday fell straight into that space: the business case was not clearly written down, so neither the integrator nor the model provider was on the hook, and the adopter paid for the configuration nobody owned. On Friday it was the implementer who gets to bill you for fixing the calls a model change broke. Every time two contracts meet, the space between them belongs to whoever signed both.

Its been a long week, in more ways than one. Over the course of the week I am sure you caught some of the tone shifts when I would hit a wall and realize that this whole program was the old "2 for me and none for you" model. At scale, it feels like a cash grab of a insane order of magnitude. At local scale, it feels like the model providers are just another entity trying to make it hard to do business, because they are adding disproportionately to your cost basis. At the end of it all, I guess capitalism tells us what this means -- the front has the lock on the models that do what nothing else can, and so most people de facto go for the greatest level of available intelligence. That move, and the constant flow of releases and changes around that selection, means that when the time comes that you *must* switch, and just like every other transactional asset (like that fax machine that hasn't been used in five years but contains toxic elements so it can't just be thrown away) you control, its just a matter of time. The difference is that most fact machine contracts at the end you pay $1 and then you don't pay anymore, so the cost to replace is much lower. 

## Every one of these has a defense

I want to be fair about this, because I do not believe that any of it is villainy (a statement I seem to be making in some form or fashion over the last four weeks), and the case is stronger if you can see the other side of each piece.

**Each rule has a reason that holds up on its own.** Insurers cannot write a policy for a risk that could produce ten thousand losses on one afternoon, which is Aon's point and a correct one. Nobody sensible wants an airline's network segmentation plan published. A notice that spells out exactly which capability the labs are holding back, and why, is a specification for the people it is meant to stop. A model that fails loudly on a flag it no longer supports is arguably more honest than one that silently ignores it. Congress, not DOT, wrote the list. Take any one of them alone and I would sign off on most of the reasoning.

**Some of it has not been tested.** As far as anyone has published, no insurer has yet denied a claim on the grounds of AI model error, and a great many policies still carry what the industry calls silent coverage. *Buist* has not been answered. The DOT category does not start until 19 October. The duty-of-care bill being negotiated in the Senate would push liability back toward the developers, if it ever gets a number and a vote and remains as written.

**The developer is not untouchable.** Texas settled with Pieces Technologies over its accuracy claims, and *Mobley v. Workday* is testing whether a vendor can be sued alongside the employer. There are routes upstream. They are just narrower and slower than the ones pointing down.

So the finding is not that any one party is behaving badly. It is that four separate sets of reasonable decisions, made by insurers, Congress and a regulator, two senators, and the labs' own product teams, all put the information on one side of the table and the cost on the other, and nobody in the chain is responsible for the pattern, because no party upstream ever sees all four at once. The only party that meets every one of them is the one at the bottom of the chain, paying for each.

## Ask for the rest of the Buyers Guide

Nobody is going to take the As Is box off AI, and at this point I would not waste my breath asking them to. The labs will not warranty a model on your data, and the insurers have told you in writing they will not either. What is worth asking for is the rest of the sticker, which is cheaper for everyone upstream to give and a great deal more useful to you.

**Trade for sight, not for indemnity.** In the next renewal with a model vendor or an implementer, stop spending your negotiating capital on liability language you will not get, and spend it on visibility you can. Release notes before the release rather than on the day, including the breaking parameters. Access to the new version on your own workloads before the alias moves. Friday's disclosure clock belongs on the same list. None of those costs the vendor a dollar of exposure, which is exactly why they are winnable, and every one of them is something your underwriter can price in your favor.

**Give the space between contracts a name.** Wherever one of your AI systems sits across two agreements (the lab and the implementer, the platform and the integrator, the airline and its check-in vendor), pick one person inside your organization who owns what falls between them, and give that person a seat when versions are adopted and retired. It is not a new idea. An MIT CISR briefing from May, written by academics with no product to sell, makes the same recommendation for the data underneath AI: one accountable owner, decided in advance, who is in the room for update and retirement decisions. The adopter in Monday's story would have had a much cheaper year if one person had owned the configuration that fell between the integrator and the model provider.

**Rehearse the change and the error together, once.** Put Monday's scenario and Friday's in the same room, because that is how they will arrive. The model your customers depend on changes on a Tuesday, and on Wednesday it starts getting a category of decision wrong. Walk it through with the people who would actually be on the call: who notices, how long it takes, who fixes the code, which policy responds and which exclusion is attached, and whose budget carries the week. Do it once. The two or three places where nobody in the room can answer are your exposure, and you will have found them for the price of a meeting rather than the price of a claim.

## The sticker is not itself the problem

A used car sold as is is at least honest about the deal. You see the box before you sign, you can ask to open the hood, and the car in your driveway on Monday is the one you bought on Saturday. This week the box was checked on every document I read, and the other two protections were missing from all of them: the hood was closed by a black box, a statute or a sealed envelope, and the car kept changing after it left the lot.

The As Is box, on its own, is a fair enough deal to sign. The welded hood is the part to refuse.
