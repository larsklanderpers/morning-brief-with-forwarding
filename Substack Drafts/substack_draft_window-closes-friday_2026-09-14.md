A word is a word long before it is in the dictionary. The dictionary is still the thing people reach for when they want to settle whether it is one.

That gap is ordinary and mostly harmless. Nobody is hurt by the two or three years a new word spends in common use before an editor puts it in an edition. But there is a second kind of dictionary, one almost nobody outside a statistical agency has ever opened, and the gap in that one is not harmless at all. It is the official list of jobs. Every government keeps one. Yours decides which occupations exist, in the only sense that institutions recognize: which ones get a code, and therefore which ones get a training pathway, a visa category, a salary benchmark, a projection, and a line in somebody's workforce plan.

This week I want to follow one mechanism through five days, because it turns out to be the same mechanism at five different scales. **Somebody or something applied a label, the label decided what happened next, and there is usually no name attached to the labelling.** By Wednesday that is a severity score no human assigned. By Thursday it is a vendor choosing which of two filing cabinets an incident goes in. By Friday it is a contract clause that never fires because the label upstream of it was never defined.

Today is the one place in the week where the labelling has a name on it. It has a named committee, a published notice, a docket number, and a deadline. I am starting here on purpose, because you cannot see what is wrong with the other four days until you have seen what the accountable version looks like.

And the accountable version has a problem of its own, which is that it runs on a decade and the work does not.

## Seven hundred and thirty-nine

Start with the number that got me into this, and start by being honest about it, because it is the weakest thing in the piece and I would rather hand you the weakness myself.

Over the last year a job title has been going around: the AI orchestrator, the person who runs a stable of agents rather than doing the task. There is exactly one labor-market figure behind it. **Lightcast counted 739 AI orchestrator postings in February 2026, up from roughly 53 a year earlier.** Lightcast sells labor-market data, which is worth saying out loud, and the figure was provided to a single trade publication rather than published in any report of its own. It is not independently checkable. The percentage that travels with it, a headline-friendly 1,294 percent, is what any series does when it starts near zero.

So: 739 postings. A real signal, and a rounding error. Both of those are true at once, and the second one is why the story is not the one everybody has been writing.

Because when you go and look for the orchestrator in the official lists, it is not there. Not in the American one. Not in the Australian one. And the interesting question is not whether it should be. **The interesting question is what would have to happen for it to get there, and how long that takes, and whether anybody can still ask.**

## The list nobody reads

The American list is the Standard Occupational Classification, and it sits underneath more of your working life than you would guess. Federal statistics use it. So do wage surveys, workforce projections, licensing regimes, and a large amount of the private benchmarking that tells you what to pay somebody. If a job is not in it, the job does not stop existing. It just stops being legible to every system that runs on it.

Here is the part that surprised me. I assumed, before I went and read the thing, that the orchestrator had no code because it was too small. Seven hundred and thirty-nine of anything is not much. That assumption is wrong, and the committee says so in writing:

> *"In evaluating collectability and reportability, however, the SOC Policy Committee does not use a specific employment size cut-off."*

Small and concentrated can qualify. Large and scattered can fail. The actual test is the ninth of the classification's published principles:

> *"For a detailed occupation to be included in the SOC, either the Bureau of Labor Statistics or the Census Bureau must be able to collect and report data on that occupation."*

Which is a fair rule, and a rule with a name on it, published in a document anybody can download. **The orchestrator is absent because the work has not settled into something a survey can ask about in a single question, not because 739 is a small number.** If you have ever tried to write the job description, you already know why. Ask five companies what their AI orchestrator does and you get a prompt engineer, a platform engineer, a program manager and two people whose real job is talking their colleagues down.

## The clock, which is the actual story

Now the timing, and this is the beat I would carry out of today.

The 2028 revision of the American list opened with a Federal Register notice on **12 June 2024**, docket BLS-2024-0001-0001. It asked the public what should change. **That comment window closed on 12 August 2024.**

Look at those dates against the only number we have. Lightcast's base year is February 2025. The role reached 739 postings in February 2026. **The one public window in which anybody could ask for this job to be named closed roughly six months before the job measurably existed, and eighteen months before anybody was writing about it.** Nobody shut a door in anybody's face. The door was open, on a published schedule, in the Federal Register, and the thing walked up late.

It gets sharper. The committee's own published request for input lists ten categories of information it wanted from the public, and **artificial intelligence does not appear in any of them.** The nearest it comes is asking whether *"the tools and technologies mentioned in existing SOC occupation definitions"* are up to date. In mid-2024, that was a reasonable thing not to ask about. It reads differently now.

A second notice is coming, carrying the committee's recommendations to the Office of Management and Budget, and it will bring a second public comment window. Its date is listed as to be determined. The final structure is projected for 2027 and implementation for 2028. And the cadence of this thing, 2010 to 2018 to 2028, means that a job title which misses 2028 is looking at the late 2030s.

**A worker choosing what to retrain into, and a hiring manager trying to get a headcount line approved for a role the finance system has no category for, are both waiting on a book whose next edition lands in 2028 and whose one after that lands around 2038.** The technology underneath them is on an eighteen-month cycle. That is not a scandal and nobody involved is behaving badly. It is two clocks, and they are not close.

## Australia already wrote it down

Here is the turn, and it is the reason this piece is worth your Monday.

While the American list waits for its second notice, the Australian one has been rebuilt from the ground up. **On 6 December 2024, the Australian Bureau of Statistics replaced ANZSCO with OSCA, the Occupation Standard Classification for Australia.** If you work in this area and you have been saying ANZSCO recently, and I have, the label has been superseded for the better part of two years.

And OSCA is already being updated. The Bureau ran a public round from **11 March to 24 April 2026**, asking which occupations *"have evolved, changed or are not currently separately identified."* It received **265 submissions**, from government, business, skills councils, industry bodies, unions, academics and individuals.

Out of that came a draft carrying **31 new detailed occupations**. One of them is an artificial intelligence engineer. Another is a site reliability engineer, a title that spent roughly fifteen years as an internal Google word before anybody official wrote it down.

**There is no orchestration role in the draft.** There is no agent role and no prompt role.

And the draft is open for comment right now. The Bureau's language is plain: *"Consultation is now open and will continue through to 18 September 2026."*

That is Friday. Four days after this goes out.

So the same question, put to two governments, comes back with two completely different shapes. **One asked before the job existed and will not ask again this decade. The other asked six months ago, got 265 answers, drafted 31 names, included artificial intelligence engineer, left orchestration out, and is taking arguments about that until Friday afternoon.**

## The strongest version of the other side

I should put the counterargument up properly, because it is good and I half believe it.

Maybe the orchestrator should not get a code. Maybe it is a job-ad keyword and not an occupation, a word recruiters reach for because it sounds current, describing work that is really platform engineering, or program management, or the ordinary evolution of a developer's job. Classification principle nine exists precisely to keep that kind of thing out, and a list that admitted every fashionable title would be worse than useless within a decade. The 2010 edition of the American occupational data added 152 new and emerging occupations; not all of those aged well either.

I think that is right about the orchestrator specifically and wrong about the general case. **The cybersecurity analyst was a recruiter's word once.** Four cybersecurity occupations went into the 2019 taxonomy, years after the work was real, and the interval between the work existing and the name existing was an interval in which nobody could benchmark a salary, build a degree, or write a workforce plan for any of it. We have all lived through that one. The lesson I take is not that every new title deserves a code. It is that the argument about which ones do is worth having in the room where it is being had, rather than afterward.

## What to actually do, and it takes about ten minutes

**If you are in Australia, or you employ anybody there, the Bureau is taking submissions until Friday 18 September.** This is the whole point of the piece, so let me be concrete rather than inspirational. The consultation is on the draft OSCA update and it is run through the Bureau's consultation hub. You do not need standing, an institution or a submission template. The 265 responses to the earlier round included individuals.

**Say what the work is, not what the title is.** The committee decides on work performed. A submission that says "please add AI orchestrator" is the weakest possible version, because it argues about a name. A submission that describes the duties, says how they differ from the nearest existing occupation, names the tools, and says roughly how many people in your organization do it, is arguing on the criteria the classification actually uses. If you think the work is genuinely platform engineering under a new name, **say that instead** and say why. A well-argued no is worth as much to them as a yes, and there are fewer of those.

**If you are in the United States, you do not have a window open today and I am not going to pretend otherwise.** The second Federal Register notice has not published and carries no date. What you can do is watch docket BLS-2024-0001-0001, because when that notice lands it will carry the last public comment window before the 2028 structure is fixed, and on the observed cadence the one after it is a decade out. That is a thinner instruction than I would like. It is also the honest one.

## Two clocks

The orchestrator may well not deserve a code, and I am not campaigning for one. What I cannot get past is that the argument about it is being had almost entirely in venture firm memos, vendor blog posts and conference panels, where it settles nothing and commits nobody, while the actual proceeding that decides the question runs quietly in two government consultations that our industry does not attend. One of those consultations closed before most of us had heard of the job. The other closes on Friday and has 265 submissions in it, which in a country of that size is a small room, and a small room is the easiest kind to be heard in.

The rest of this week is about labels with nobody's name attached: a severity score that no person assigned, a taxonomy fork chosen in private, a contract trigger that never fires because the word upstream of it was never defined. Today is the opposite case, and it is the one worth holding the others against. **The naming here is done by a committee you can identify, under principles you can read, through a process you can join, on a schedule published years in advance.** Everything that is wrong with it is wrong in the open, which is the only kind of wrong that anybody outside the room can fix. The deadline is Friday, the form is free, and the argument about what your job is called is currently being had by 265 people who are not you.
