You can own the building, buy the equipment, hire every person in it, and still not own the numbers. Anybody who has run a franchise knows this in their bones.

The fryers are yours. The lease is yours. The staff roster is yours and the payroll comes out of your account. And every night the till reports to head office, because that was in the agreement you signed, on a page you skimmed, in a paragraph that was not hiding anything from you at all.

I have been arguing for three weeks that governance is something you build rather than something you receive. On Sunday I said that a guardrail you cannot point at in a system diagram is not a guardrail, and I promised that this week would be the part where we actually build one. So let's start with the thing you build first, and then let's be honest about how much of it is yours.

## The layer that was always the real product

On the fifth of August I wrote that the wall that failed was never inside the model. A model can talk its way around its own instructions. It cannot talk its way around a barrier that lives outside it and holds. The industry has a name for that barrier, and it is the harness -- the tool permissions, the sandbox, the approval that cannot be prompted away, the checks that run on what came back.

I said then that the wall that holds only protects you if you are the one holding it. That was the right sentence, but I left half the job undone. I told you the harness was the thing that mattered, in this specific context because it is, at its root, *how you hold on to something far too large for anyone to inspect directly.* I did not tell you what is actually in it, or how much of it you are holding, or how much leverage you have, or how it can be changed based on the model itself, despite being independent of it.

So here is the honest version. When people argue about which model to use, they are arguing about the least differentiated part of the stack. I know that sounds crazy, but it's true. Models converge. We've seen that more clearly in the last three months than almost anything else. The models get cheaper roughly every quarter (and from anecdotal reports, when they *don't*, like Fable 5, adoption is very low), they leapfrog each other on benchmarks nobody outside the labs can reproduce, and by the time your procurement cycle finishes the ranking has changed anyway. The model is the commodity. The harness -- what the thing is allowed to touch, what happens to what it produces, what context it is able to maintain and take advantage of, and where all of that is written down -- is the product. It is also the only part you can build. As we discussed last week, it is where your governance actually gets built. And regardless of whether you are in a regulated industry or not, AI Governance is something that will impact you, and everyone you work with, sooner not later.

Which is why it matters enormously what you actually get when you go and take it back.

## So I went and read the terms

Anthropic shipped Managed Agents this year: long-running agent sessions that hold their state on the vendor's side, resume after a pause, and keep going. It is a good product and it solves a real problem, which is that agentic work is long and brittle and falls over if nobody remembers what happened an hour ago. It is still in beta -- every endpoint requires a beta header with a date in the name -- and I want to be fair about that, because beta means the shape can still move. 

Here is the part I went looking for, on the feature eligibility table, in the vendor's own documentation, in plain sight:

Managed Agents is **not eligible for Zero Data Retention. It is not eligible for a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement.** Neither of those is a policy choice somebody could reverse with a phone call. The reason given is architectural: *"Sessions are stateful resources; transcripts persist until you delete them."*

Read the shape of that rather than the words. Deletion is a customer action. It is something you have to remember to do, build a process around, prove you did, and be able to demonstrate to somebody who asks. It is not a retention posture. A retention posture is a thing the system does whether or not anybody is paying attention, which is precisely what makes it worth something to a regulator.

The overview page says the same thing in longer form: the service is *"stateful by design"*, and sessions *"store conversation history, sandbox state, and outputs server-side."*

None of that is hidden. None of it is a scandal. It is a documented consequence of a design decision that buys you something real. But it is a rung on the harness that you do not hold, and if you are in a regulated environment you need to know which rungs those are before you build on top of them.

Go one step further. On the eleventh of August, xAI shipped Grok Bot, which gives an agent a persistent cloud computer, its own logins, and an always-on runtime. By the reporting on it, clicking "teach a task" drops the bot into a recording state on its own screen while you walk a workflow through, step by step, until it can repeat the sequence without you. That is a stateful agent built out of your own working patterns, and you have no control over the exhaust.

Two days later OpenAI went considerably further, and this is the one I would actually pay attention to. Computer History sits on top of Computer Use, and it does not wait for you to teach it a workflow. It records what you do. Clicks, typing, keyboard shortcuts, app switches, and whatever else macOS hands over through its accessibility layer, running in the background, turned into memories the model carries forward into later conversations. It replaces an earlier feature called Chronicle that did the same job with screenshots, and I want to give OpenAI credit where it is due, because the rebuild deliberately does *not* capture your screen, your microphone, your system audio, or private browsing.

Now read the architecture, because it is the same shape as everything above. The event files sit on your Mac for up to forty-eight hours. Then they go to OpenAI's servers to be turned into memories, and the memories come back and live locally as plain text. OpenAI says it does not keep the event files after processing unless the law requires it, which is a better posture than most and I will say so plainly.

It is also, precisely, the pattern. The record is generated on your machine and it is processed on somebody else's. The compute is yours. The transit is not.

Three details worth having before you decide how you feel about that. It is off by default and each person opts in individually -- which sounds reassuring right up until you ask who the person is, because on a work Mac the individual deciding whether to switch on continuous interaction capture is the employee. Business and Enterprise administrators have to grant access first, and that is the control that actually matters. Pro does not have an administrator.

The second is OpenAI's own warning, and it is more candid than most vendors would print: the files *"are not encrypted by Computer History, and other programs running as your macOS user may be able to access them."*

And the third is that Computer History is unavailable in the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the United Kingdom. So is Record and Replay, the feature OpenAI shipped back in June that turns a demonstrated workflow into a reusable skill. Two products, one carve-out, drawn along a regulatory line rather than a technical one. Somebody has already had this argument internally, and they had it with a lawyer.

## The part where I was wrong, and it is the important part

Here is where I need to correct something, because I had assumed the opposite and I suspect a lot of people reading this have made the same assumption.

In the context of Managed Agents from Anthropic, self-hosted sandboxes exist (and I presume Grok and Chat GPT offer something similar, though I have been unable to confirm it). You can run the execution environment on your own infrastructure. That has been available since May, it is a genuinely good piece of engineering, and it is exactly what somebody in a regulated environment reaches for when they want the workload inside their own perimeter. My assumption -- reasonable, widely shared, and wrong -- was that self-hosting was the carve-out. Bring the sandbox home and the retention problem goes home with it.

**It does not.** The eligibility note is explicit, and I am going to quote it exactly because paraphrasing it would soften it: the ineligibility *"applies to all Managed Agents sub-features, including Self-hosted sandboxes."*

The self-hosted documentation explains why, and this is the sentence that actually matters: *"Tool inputs and outputs still flow to Anthropic's control plane (where Claude runs) so the model can see results and determine what to do next."*

Of course they do. The model has to know what the tool returned or it cannot decide what to do next. That is not a loophole and it is not a compromise somebody made to save money -- it is the only way an agent can work at all. The compute moved to your building. The conversation did not. And that sentence is why I was confident the products above have the same limitation before I went and checked -- because functionally, if you are running on somebody's inference, *you have to be intersecting their control plane somewhere.* Computer History simply documents the round trip instead of leaving you to infer it.

**You move the compute and you do not move the transcript.**

I want to be scrupulously fair here, because there is a version of this paragraph that is cheap, and easy, and rabble-rousing and I do not want to write it. Anthropic markets self-hosted sandboxes for compliance and data residency, and that marketing is accurate. Your code, your files, your internal endpoints, your network -- those genuinely stay where you put them. That is a real guarantee and it is worth real money. It is simply a different guarantee from the one about what is retained. Two different promises, both true, sitting on two different documentation pages.

The story is not that somebody lied. The story is the buyer who read the first page and assumed the second, signed off on the architecture review, and told their board the workload was contained. That buyer is not careless. That buyer read a vendor's compliance page and drew the inference a reasonable person draws.

## Where the model runs, and where the data sits

There is a related thing I need to correct in public, because I have said a version of it myself and it stopped being true ten days ago.

"You cannot control where the model runs" is dead. Strike it wherever you find it, including in things I wrote. There is now a per-request setting for it.

But look at what the setting actually offers, because this is the part that matters more than the headline. The inference geography accepts exactly two values: `global`, which means it may run anywhere available, and `us`, which means it runs on United States infrastructure. That is the entire menu. There is no EU. There is no Australia.

So for a non-US regulated buyer, the choice is "the United States" or "unspecified." Both of those are offshore. The control exists, it works, and it answers a question you were not asking.

And then there is the one that actually bites, which is not about inference at all. Where the data sits at rest is a separate setting, and the documentation is blunt about it: *"Currently, `us` is the only available workspace geo."* Then, in the next breath: *"Workspace geo is set when you create a workspace and can't be changed afterward."*

Sit with the second half of that. Not a default somebody forgot to change. Not a setting an administrator can go and fix on Monday morning. A decision your organization already made, permanently, on whatever afternoon somebody clicked through the setup, almost certainly without knowing a decision was being made at all.

There is a cost line here too, and it is worth having because the economics are usually where these arguments actually get settled. Pinning inference to the US is *"priced at 1.1x the standard rate across all token pricing categories"*, and each of those tokens *"draws down 1.1 tokens from your committed"* capacity. Residency has a meter on it. Ten percent is not a lot, and it is also not nothing at scale, and it is the first time I have seen the sovereignty conversation carry an actual number on the invoice rather than a hand-wave about enterprise pricing.

Before we move on, I want to be clear that this is a governance story, and a regulatory story, as much as it is an operational and economics story. To try and make it not that is understating -- badly -- the implications. And it is a governance story built, as so many real ones are, out of the tiniest of tiny details, the kind that mean nothing to a non-technical person and scream at a senior engineer. The direction of travel in both Australia and Europe has been toward the same answer, that the consequential part has to happen somewhere you can actually see it, and none of these stories put it there.

Then look at what the pricing does with that, because it is perverse. You pay MORE to pin down where the model computes. Where the data actually sits is not on the menu at all -- one country, no alternative, and no surcharge, because there is nothing to charge you for. You are paying a premium for the control you can exercise, and the one that actually matters is not for sale. And that one country is the center of the Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime I keep talking about. This is not an academic exercise. It is effectively surrendering your information as a hostage to your good behavior toward the frontier companies and, by extension, the US government, in return for...no guarantees.

And I want to put a citation under the phrase "by extension," because it is doing real work in that sentence and I do not want it read as rhetoric. Since March of 2018, under section 2713 of the US code, a US-headquartered provider has been obliged to produce data it holds *"regardless of whether such communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the United States."* That is eight years old. It is structural. No Australian data centre has ever changed it, and any vendor who tells you otherwise is selling you the remedy. The 2024 bilateral agreement between Australia and the United States -- which gets misdated and misdescribed almost every time it comes up, including in material that has crossed my desk -- did something narrower and in one respect the opposite: it built a reciprocal direct-order channel so our agencies could stop waiting on mutual assistance, barred orders made under it from intentionally targeting Australian persons, and gave providers a route to resist US process over non-US persons.

Here is the part nobody has answered, and I am not going to pretend that I can. That statute was written for stored communications. Whether it reaches *inference* -- a prompt processed offshore, in transit, never stored anywhere at all -- is genuinely unsettled, and I have not found anybody willing to put their name to an answer in either direction. So the single most consequential question about where your work actually goes is, as I write this, open. That is not a comfortable place to end a section and I am not going to dress it up as one.

I talked about this issue indirectly before, in discussing the implications of this kind of AI for Native American enterprises, where sovereignty over all stages of the compute process is not just a preference -- it is both an operating necessity and a privacy protection for their customers that in some ways exceeds even traditional PII. And for enterprises like that -- or any enterprise that prides itself or markets itself on privacy and protection -- the very nature of how these things work, regardless of how you manage the harness, because you still have to interact with the control plane, *puts you at risk.* I cannot make that point strongly enough, despite all the soft-shoeing around that particular outcome.

## The part you genuinely do run

I have spent two thousand words telling you what you do not hold, which is the East Coast in me and I make no apology for it. Now the other half, because there is a piece of this harness that is unambiguously yours, and it is the most literal example I have found of governance you build rather than governance you receive.

This technology is called "inference hooks." You point the platform at a server that you operate, and every governed prompt is held -- before inference begins -- until your server returns a verdict. Not logged afterward. Not reviewed in a dashboard on Thursday. Held, in the request path, waiting on a decision made by software that runs on your infrastructure and answers to you. So, you can control what actually gets to the control plane, which is at least a step on the path of protection.

Your server sees the transcript text, the tool calls and their results, and text pulled out of attachments. It does not see raw file bytes, system prompts, or the vendor's internal context. Verdicts are allow or deny and nothing else -- you cannot rewrite a prompt or redact it on the way through, which is a real limitation, especially when it comes to end-user functionality, so it is probably pretty important you know it now and not when you are taking a pilot to production. Almost as importantly, your decision has to be *fast*: There is a five second default timeout. It is Enterprise-only (which doesn't sound like much but is actually doing a lot of lifting here), it is beta, it excludes voice and a few other paths, and it is not available if you are coming through Bedrock or Google Cloud.

That last set of limits matters, and not in the direction you would expect. Because this is Enterprise and signed-in only, it does nothing whatsoever for the person running a personal subscription on a work laptop, which is a population I have flagged multiple times, and which has not gotten smaller. So this is not a general answer. It is a specific, powerful control for a specific tier of user spending large sums of money and paying for the privilege of having a say in how it is used.

Now here is the sentence that made me want to write this piece.

**Whether the hook fails open or fails closed is your setting.**

The vendor ships the mechanism. You decide whether it is actually a control. Set it to fail open, and on the day your verdict server is slow or down or misconfigured, every prompt sails through and the system keeps working beautifully and nobody files a ticket, because from the outside a control that is not holding looks exactly like a control that has nothing to stop. You will be able to point at it in the system diagram. It will be in the architecture doc. It will have a box and an arrow and a name. 

I will come back to that on Friday, because it turns out to be the whole week's problem in miniature.

## The obvious objection, and how much of it I will take

The sharpest objection to everything above is that I have described the ordinary condition of buying anything. Every vendor relationship has terms. Every managed service holds something you would rather it did not. You do not get to outsource the operation and keep all of the control, and grown-ups have known this since the first payroll bureau. Welcome to procurement.

That is fair, and I will concede most of it. I am not claiming vendors are hiding things -- every retention and residency fact above came out of public documentation, in under an hour, with no special access, and I have quoted it rather than characterized it precisely because none of it needed a leak.

What is different is the gap between where the marketing sits and where the constraint sits. The residency story is on the page you are shown during evaluation. The retention story is on a feature eligibility table you reach by following a link from a footnote. Both are true. Only one is in the room when the decision gets made. That is not deception, but it is an architecture of attention, and the effect on a busy buyer is indistinguishable from one.

And the second objection, which is better: none of this stopped anybody from shipping anything. Correct. It should not. The point of knowing which rungs you hold is not to refuse to climb.

## Four questions, and none of them take long

**Write down your harness, on one page, in four columns.** What the agent can touch. What happens to what it produces. Where that is retained and for how long. And who at your organization can change each of those. Most people I know can fill in the first column immediately and stall on the third. The stall is the finding.

**Then go and read the eligibility page for whatever you are actually running.** Not the product page. Not the security overview. The table with the yes and no columns in it, for the specific features you have turned on, including the ones you turned on because they solved a problem in a sprint eight months ago. This takes about twenty minutes and it is the cheapest thing on this list.

**Check what geography your workspaces were created in, and stop looking for the setting to change it.** If it is fixed at creation, then the remedy is not configuration, it is a new workspace and a migration, and that is a project with a budget rather than a checkbox. Better to learn that now than in the middle of a due diligence questionnaire.

**And if you have hooks available to you, find out which way yours fails.** One question, one answer, and it tells you whether you have a control or a diagram.

## The one you are building by accident

The reason to know all of this is not so you can be disappointed. Every one of these products is genuinely good, the documentation is genuinely honest, and I would rather work with a vendor that publishes an eligibility table with a "No" in it than one that publishes a brochure. And there is plenty out there to disappoint you, I don't want to make it worse.

The reason is that you are going to build (and more importantly, run) a harness whether you decide to or not, whether you even know what it *is* or not. Some of it you will assemble deliberately. The rest of it will be assembled for you, out of defaults you never saw, on afternoons nobody remembers, and it will work perfectly well right up until somebody asks you to describe it and you realize it's been a diagram all along.

The building is yours. The equipment is yours. The staff are yours.

Go and find out where the till reports.
