Nothing drifts toward control. Everything drifts away from it. Some very famous people have referenced this as the first law of thermodynamics, but it applies to all systems, everywhere.

Leave a garden alone for a season and you do not get a tidier garden -- you get a field. Leave a budget alone and you do not get savings. Leave a codebase, a contract, or a country's AI policy untended, and the same thing happens every time: it does not hold its shape, it takes the shape of whoever is leaning on it hardest. Control is not the resting state of anything. It is a thing you install against the current, and the moment you stop maintaining it, the current takes it back. Arguably more importantly, at least in our areas of concern, *if you aren't controlling it, who or what is?* But that's a slightly different question.

That is the whole of what this week was about, and I did not fully see it myself until I lined the five days up next to each other. So let me put them together, because the through-line turned out not to be that control is hard. It is that control is never the default, and it only ever exists where somebody sat down and designed it in.

This piece publishes on 2 August, and I could not have picked the date if I had tried. Today was supposed to be the day one of the most carefully designed pieces of technology law on earth switched on its hardest obligations. It does not.

## Four capitals, one week, four different designs

Start outside your building, because four governments spent this exact week proving the point for me.

The European Union built the AI Act -- the most complete AI framework anyone has written, the one every other government points to when it wants to look like the adult in the room. Its high-risk rules -- the obligations that actually bite, the ones covering AI that makes consequential decisions about people -- were due to apply today. Their start date was pushed back before it ever arrived. Standalone high-risk systems now have until 2 December 2027; high-risk AI built into regulated products, until 2 August 2028. The delay was finalized in the Digital Omnibus, published in the EU's Official Journal on 24 July, and it followed sustained pressure from the large technology companies -- reported plainly, not something I am inferring.

Now notice what did not move. What lands today, on schedule, is Article 50 -- the transparency layer. The duty to tell a person when they are dealing with an AI system, and to label content a machine generated or altered. That is worth having. But set it against Friday's distinction and see what it actually is. A disclosure obligation is a sign. It tells people what is happening; it does not stop anything from happening. The most designed framework in the world put a date on the governor, moved that date out by sixteen months to two years under industry pressure, and shipped the sign on time. *Even the adult in the room shipped the sign and deferred the engine.*

Hold that next to the country I spent Wednesday calling framework-less. Australia is the heaviest per-capita user of AI on earth and still has no national AI law -- Wednesday's whole piece was the gap between those two facts. But "no framework" turns out to be too generous in one direction and too harsh in another. Australia has a binding obligation with a hard date: from 10 December 2026, under changes to the Privacy Act, any organization the Act covers must disclose in its privacy policy which substantially automated decisions its systems make about people. It is enforced by the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner -- the OAIC, the federal privacy regulator -- and it binds a full year before the EU's deferred high-risk rules. The jurisdiction everyone holds up as the grown-up moved its hard part to 2027 and 2028. The jurisdiction I called framework-less put a firm date on a real obligation in 2026. "No framework" meant "no framework yet, and a hard date regardless."

Then there is the design nobody was talking about a month ago. On 13 July the United Kingdom did something no major economy had done: it reached past the regulated companies and put their cloud providers directly under financial oversight. The Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority, and the Financial Conduct Authority now supervise four named "critical third parties" -- Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Oracle -- requiring them to map dependencies, test resilience, and report incidents to the regulators directly. For years the rule was that a bank was responsible for its vendors and the regulator only ever spoke to the bank. Britain reached through the customer and took hold of the provider, because more than sixty-five percent of UK firms sit on the same handful of clouds, and a risk that concentrated stopped being something the customer could plausibly own alone. Watch this one propagate -- Australia's prudential regulator and the EU are both looking straight at it.

And then the United States, which spent the summer proving that improvisation scales all the way up to a superpower. Thursday walked through it: four separate attempts to restrict Chinese open-weight AI models, all four reached for and quietly put back down; a State Department cable in mid-July telling allies there is no "magic button" and no kill switch; and, seven days later, two members of Congress introducing the AI Kill Switch Act -- a bill to build exactly that button, fitted to the five American labs big enough to already answer the phone. One arm of the government told the world the switch does not exist while another drafted its specification. That is not a framework. That is several hands grabbing at the same lever, none of them checking whether it is still attached to anything.

Four governments. One week. Four completely incompatible designs -- and the incompatibility is the tell. If control were the default state of the world, four of the most capable regulatory machines on earth would not have arrived at four different answers in the same seven days, with the most sophisticated of them deferring the part that matters. Control showed up in each of them only to the exact degree that somebody built it in. The EU designed an enormous amount and switched on a sliver. Australia designed one hard edge and it binds. Britain designed a reach nobody expected. Washington designed an argument with itself. Nowhere did control simply arrive because the technology did.

## The regulator who wrote the thesis

Which brings me to the standard that states this week's argument more plainly than I have managed to all week, because a regulator wrote it into law before I wrote it into a title.

Australia's prudential regulator, APRA -- the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, which supervises the banks, insurers, and pension funds -- runs an operational-resilience standard called CPS 230. It is in force now. It never mentions AI. What it does is define a "material" service functionally -- a service the entity relies on to run a critical operation, or one that exposes it to material operational risk -- and then it does the thing that makes it the week in miniature: it hands you the determination. APRA does not classify your AI vendor for you. It does not hand you a label. It tells you that materiality is yours to determine and yours to defend, and if an offshore model doing your inference is material, the fact that no regulator ever pointed at it does not get you out of anything.

I will be straight about this one, because I got it wrong in this newsletter twice this month before I read the standard properly -- once loosely enough that it needed a correction. I would rather you leave with the mechanism than watch me apologize for it a second time. The looseness was mine; the standard was never vague. And stripped of the acronyms, what it says is the exact sentence this whole week has been circling: nobody hands you control, and nobody even hands you the classification that would tell you where you need it. You determine it. You defend it. It is a design decision, written into a prudential standard by a regulator who declined to make it for you.

## Now come home

Come back from the four capitals, because the same physics runs inside your own building, and that is where the week started.

Monday I put a number in front of you -- two to five humans supervising fifty to a hundred agents -- and showed you it was an anecdote nobody who produced it will defend as measurement. The default there is not control. It is a headcount plan drawn against a slide. Tuesday I showed you the leak: every correction your best people make to a model teaches someone else's system how your business actually runs, continuously, in a currency nobody on your side has ever audited. The default there is not control either. It is an always-on drain, and it runs whether or not anyone decided it should. Friday I showed you the governance document -- the acceptable-use policy, the model card, the checkbox in the settings pane that my friends at that startup ticked correctly, right before their coding tool uploaded their entire codebase anyway. The default there is a sign in a shared drive that nothing in the code path is obliged to read.

Three defaults, and not one of them is neutral. That is the part people miss about a default: somebody chose it -- just not you, and not for your benefit. Nobody sets a factory default to protect the person who never changes it.

And then Saturday, the one piece of good news in the whole run, which is also the one control this entire mess cannot take away from you: the people. The junior seat you were about to automate is the only place a person ever learns to catch a confident machine being wrong, and that skill is about to be the most valuable one in the building. You do not buy it. No vendor ships it. You grow it, on purpose, on the rung -- which makes it the purest example of the week's argument. It is control that exists only if you design it in, and it is the one nobody else can sell you, defer by sixteen months, or reach through your customer to grab.

## The invoice date, not the invoice

Which is the moment to talk about the bill, because there is one, and it is where the second meaning of the word "default" quietly walks in.

When the EU deferred its high-risk obligations, it moved the date. It did not touch the price. The penalties are exactly where they were: up to thirty-five million euros or seven percent of global turnover for the prohibited practices, up to fifteen million euros or three percent for transparency and high-risk breaches. The invoice date moved. The size of the invoice did not. And the same is true of every default we have walked through this week -- the unverified ratio, the unmetered exhaust, the governance sign in the shared drive. None of them is free. They are financed. The phrase I keep coming back to, from my own notes this week, is that firms which have not made the design decision are accruing governance debt that converts to penalty exposure. That is the double meaning hiding in the title. "Default" is two words wearing one spelling -- the setting you get when you never choose, and what happens when a debt you have been ignoring finally comes due.

And the clock is running even where the rulebook is not finished. The OAIC has not published its final guidance on automated-decision transparency -- it is still in consultation, and I am not going to pretend the guidance exists when it does not. But the regulator is not waiting for its own guidance to start enforcing the posture behind it. It is running a sweep of privacy policies right now; it is drawing a hard line between telling people something and getting their consent to it -- "notice is not consent," in its consultation language; and by the read in my own briefing this week, it has moved from guidance mode to enforcement posture -- building a case file, not issuing warnings. You do not get to wait for the framework to be finished before the cost of not designing anything starts to accrue.

## Three decisions, and the one question left

So here is what you can actually do, because after a week this long you have earned something more useful than "be careful" -- which is not a control any more than a policy PDF is.

**Decide where your learning loop lives.** Not whether you use AI; that decision is behind you. Whose system gets smarter every time your people correct it, and what would it cost to keep that loop at home? If you cannot answer, that is the first thing to design, because the default answer right now is "your provider's," and it was chosen for you, and in many ways, as a result, it is the *WORST* possible outcome.

**Decide your model exposure on purpose.** Which models does your business actually depend on, under whose jurisdiction, and what is your fallback the quarter one of them gets pulled off the market by a memo -- the way two of them already were this year? An option nobody has priced is not a fallback. It is a hope with a budget line missing. You would attack with diligence and rigour an uninsured truck in your fleet, or a hole in the roof of your manufactory: this needs to be treated exactly the same.

**Decide what actually enforces your rules.** Friday's test, which I said then and will say again, is the single most critical test for any of us, and the only one I would apply to any AI governance program I was shown this year: which system in your stack would actually deny the operation? Not the policy that says it should not happen -- the thing in the path that makes it not happen. If the honest answer is a document, a diagram, or a checkbox, you have a sign, and you learned this week what a sign is worth at two in the morning on an empty road. Don't depend on the side. Build rules, lean into them, test them, strain them, make sure they hold up, and then wait a week and do it again.

None of these is a purchase. Every one of them is a decision, and that is the load-bearing point of the whole week. Control was never going to arrive with the technology, or with the framework, or with the vendor's next release. It is not the default, and it never was -- not for you, and this week, not even for four of the most powerful governments on earth. It was always true, but particularly when it comes to this new class of tools (or whatever you want to call AI), *the only control you get is the control you set, and the only protections you have are the ones you make or demand for yourself and your enterprise.* The drainage is real; you have to plug it.

So the last question is the simplest one I have asked all week. Point at the thing in your business that someone built, on purpose, to keep control at home. If you can, you are ahead of most of the room and all four of those capitals. If you cannot, that is not a gap you have -- it is a debt you are carrying, and the one certainty in a week with very few is that the invoice date is the only part of it anyone is willing to move. But trust that, as it always does, it will one day come due.
