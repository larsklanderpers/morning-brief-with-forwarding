You drive somewhere new with the directions on. You arrive faster than you would have, with no wrong turns, and you feel fine about it. Then a month later you drive there again with the phone dead, and you realize you have absolutely no idea where you are, or how to get where you are going.

Both of those very disparate outcomes are true at once, and you can be relieved that you are by no means alone in having this experience. The directions made the first trip better and the second trip impossible. And here is the thing about it that I keep coming back to: the first outcome gets measured every single time, by the app, in minutes saved. The second outcome gets measured never, because nobody ever takes the phone away on purpose to find out if they can it without. And if they do, and they get lost, most people don't want to track or admit that after the fact.

Last Saturday the skill was reading somebody else's claim -- who published it, what they sell, whether the number matches the noun. This Saturday sits a step above that. It is not about how you read the work. It is about how you design it, and specifically about which of the two numbers you have decided to look at. It's also about one of the most important things, IMHO, that each of needs to watch out for, and which we have discussed in the past, which is the impact that using these tools has on cognitive function and how you protect yours.

## The number everybody measures

The evidence that these tools make people better at the task in front of them is, at this point, not really in dispute. If it still is, for you, then the rest of the piece is probably not the one to dig in on. That said, *tasking* and *understanding* are two radically different things, especially when it comes to knowledge work.

The cleanest study I found this week that evidences this counted measure and its growing prominence amongst professionals, is a randomized trial first posted as a preprint in June of last year and since published in *npj Digital Medicine*. Seventy US-licensed physicians -- thirty-nine residents, forty-one attendings, almost all internal medicine -- worked through diagnostic vignettes in one of three configurations. In one, the AI gave its opinion first and the doctor worked from there. In another, the doctor formed a view first and then saw the AI's, and determined which conclusion to hold after already taking a position. In the third, conventional resources only, so the doctor has only their own education, experience, and judgment, to rely on.

The numbers: AI-first, 85% diagnostic accuracy. AI-second, 82%. Conventional resources, 75%.

Ten points of difference. On a task where ten points is a great deal, to put it mildly. Both AI configurations beat the control convincingly, and the one where the machine went first did best of all.

The version of this Saturday I had originally planned was going to argue that the way to use these tools well is to make them ask before they answer -- to form your own view first and let the tool critique it. That is a nice idea and I still like it (and, as we will discuss in a bit, there are really, really sound cognitive and quality of work and life satisfaction metrics that support that methodology). But the best trial I can find on the subject, when it comes to true life and death matters, points the other way on accuracy: the doctors who got the answer first did slightly better than the doctors who tried first. You might argue this could come from pride (which doctors have a little of) or you might argue it comes from confirmation bias or competitive interpretation, but regardless of how it happened, it was still the best performing outcome, and that matters. 

And then, while I was writing this, a second study landed that says much the same thing in a completely different room, and this time the authors say the quiet part out loud.

Posted to arXiv on the first of September -- four days ago -- by a team at ETH Zürich and the MIT Media Lab, and accepted for a human-AI interaction conference in November. Fifty people, students on the MIT campus, learning nuclear safety protocols from scratch, because nobody walks in knowing those. Three conditions: an unrestricted ChatGPT-style bot that would answer anything; a Socratic bot built never to give the answer, only questions and hints; and a no-chatbot mode that adapted its questions to a brain-sensing headband. Ten-minute video, pre-test, thirty minutes of assisted study, immediate post-test. A really interesting and challenging experiment, and if you find yourself curious about it, you can find the paper. It's a fascinating read, and I am not just saying that due to my own confirmation bias.

The unrestricted bot won. Not narrowly. Its users gained roughly twice as much on the test as the Socratic group, and the team's own hypothesis -- that being made to think would produce better learning -- did not survive its own experiment. 

Here is why I am telling you about it. More than half of the interactions in the unrestricted condition were people pasting the exercise into the chatbot and transcribing what came back; a quarter of their final answers were near-copies of the bot's text. One participant called it *"a bit like cheating."* And the authors, in their discussion, write that the unrestricted bot's success is *"not evidence of deeper learning, but rather a result of the immediate post-test evaluation."* They measured how much people could reproduce ten minutes later. They did not measure what those people would know in two weeks. They say so, and they list the two-week test as the thing they want to run next. I am fully prepared to put good money on the prediction markets that the outcome of such a test would be radically different than the ten minute outcome.

The Socratic bot, meanwhile, did not fail because asking does not work. It failed because people quit. Their engagement, on the headband, fell below their own resting baseline by the last question. Fifty-eight percent of the Socratic exercises were never finished. The reviews were *"it felt annoying and frustrating"* and *"I just gave up."* The authors put it precisely: this path *"can only be beneficial if learners persist through the discomfort, which many did not."* And I think, just as a personal aside, that this is a statement on the way people are made to learn these days. My father was certainly not the most Socratic of men, but he and my mom would go out of way to force us to reason our way to the answers, not just give them to us, regardless of how much additional insight they might have had. I think that creates a certain level of mental discipline that *forces* you to engage with it more; but if you are used to being given the answers on the asking instead of the other, then **of course** it's annoying and frustrating.

It is a preprint, fifty people, one topic, and a machine graded the answers. Hold it at that weight. But two studies, in medicine and in education, with different people and different tasks, both find that being handed the answer beats being made to work on the immediate measure -- and the second one says, in its own words, that the immediate measure is the reason. So the argument has to be a different argument. And since we all know how much I love a good argument, let's see where we can take this one.

## The number almost nobody measures

That first trial measured one thing: how accurate the doctor was *with the tool in the room*. It measured it well. It is a real result. And it is the number every vendor study, every pilot, every productivity claim you will read this year is also measuring, because it is the number that is easy to measure and the number that sells (and for nothing, it is the number that guarantees their tool will be in the room because that is where the success confidence is highest).


There is a second number. It is how good the person is at the task *after* months of using the tool, *when the tool is not there*. Like you, in your car, with the dead phone, trying to find a place you have only been to once, from memory, when its possible that the route *never even truly entered your mind* (that one, though, is for smarter people than me doing nuero-sciences to determine, but it sure feels right on first blush). And I can find almost nobody measuring that dependency and its cognitive impact, at anything more solid than an anecdotal level, except one group in Poland who did, and what they found is the what makes the new argument really resonate.

Four endoscopy centers, September 2021 to March 2022. One thousand four hundred and forty-three colonoscopies. The centers introduced AI-assisted polyp detection partway through. The researchers did not look at the AI-assisted procedures. They looked at the ordinary ones -- the colonoscopies the same endoscopists performed *without* the AI, before and after they had started using it.

Before exposure to the AI, the adenoma detection rate in unassisted procedures was 28.4%. After, it was 22.4%. Six points, with a confidence interval that does not cross zero. The authors, in *The Lancet Gastroenterology and Hepatology*, put it in the careful language the finding deserves: continuous exposure to AI *"might reduce"* detection in standard colonoscopy, *"suggesting a negative effect on endoscopist behaviour."*

Might. It is one study, retrospective, in one country, over six months, and the commentaries since have pointed at the obvious confounds. I am not going to overstate it, and neither did they. But notice what kind of study it is. It is one of the very few that measured the second number at all -- and it moved, in the direction you would fear, in a population of trained specialists doing the thing they are trained for, but who now have a partner of sorts to share the cognitive load with.

So: with the tool, you are better, faster, you might even feel smarter. Without it, after a while, you may be worse than you were before you had it. Again, just as with the directions, both statements are true and very accurate. Neither contradicts the other. Rather, they are answers to different questions, and one of the questions is almost never asked.

## Why the second number is so easy to lose

There is a study from 2023 that I think explains the mechanism, and it is worth being precise about it because, as we know, details matter, and all too often the pure signal of the original study is lost in the telephone game of misquotes and segmented understanding.

Researchers at the University of Deusto ran a series of experiments in which people did a made-up diagnostic task -- classifying tissue samples in a fictional disease -- with the help of an AI that had a deliberate, systematic error built in. Then the AI went away, and they did the task alone.

In the third experiment, with about two hundred online participants, 80.7% of them noticed the AI was making mistakes. Eight in ten. They saw it. And then, in the researchers' words, *"many of them still relied on the AI recommendations to perform the task,"* and when the AI was gone, they kept reproducing its error on their own. They had judgment, their judgment identified a problem, but they didn't trust their judgment, and so they got the wrong answer because they waived their own judgment for the AIs. I won't hammer that point any more than I just did, but if you have been reading along, you know that this is a theme of the new tools that I have been emphasizing for your consideration for months.

These were not doctors and it was not a real disease, which is certainly material to the impact of the study, because a lab result with lay participants is exactly the kind of thing that gets dressed up as more than it is. But the individuals still had judgment, and they *knew* it was wrong, and 10 years of education [to become a doctor] doesn't necessarily obviate that outcome, in any way. But the shape of the finding is what matters. Noticing that a tool is wrong and being able to override it are two different capacities. The first one is cheap and most people have it. The second one requires you to have an independent view of what right looks like -- and that is precisely the thing the second number measures, and precisely the thing that erodes when the tool does the work. The second one also requires that you have a belief that you can override something when you know it is wrong, and for many of us, this is exactly the kind of belief that corporations work to stomp out.

So the mechanism runs like this. The tool improves your output. Your output is what gets measured. Your independent judgment, which nobody is measuring, gets less practice each time. And by the time the phone dies, you have a long record of good outcomes and no way to produce one on your own. And think about that in the context of the ladder being pulled up, and the loss of a whole layer of the job market that we have talked about: if the people who have done the job a long time, and know it like the back of their hand, are at risk of losing their knowledge and skills, just purely from the exhaust of the AI running beside them, and we are not making massive efforts to train new people, both in how to do the job, and also in how to mitigate this cognitive rot, then the real probably in the working world isn't this year or next, its five years or ten years from now when no one remembers how to do something without the AI in the middle.

## The people already defending the second number

I said this was a Saturday and I haven't yet lost so much of cognitive processing to not remember what day it is, so here is the part that goes the other way.

Practitioners are not stupid about this. A Microsoft Research study published last October -- and it is worth knowing it is Microsoft, because Microsoft sells the assistant these people use -- surveyed 860 software developers on where they want AI help and where they do not. The pattern was clear enough that the authors named it: developers want the help on the mechanical work, and draw *"clear limits for identity- and relationship-centric work (e.g., mentoring)."* They will let the tool write the boilerplate. They will not let it be the person who teaches the junior. That is a group of people protecting the second number without being told to, on the work where they have understood it matters.

And there is at least one study pointing at a design that helps rather than hurts, and it is worth the numbers.

A CHI paper from 2023, out of the MIT Media Lab, put 204 people in front of ten socially divisive statements -- violent video games cause aggression, that kind of thing -- and asked whether each one was logically valid. Unassisted, they scored 44%. Worse than a coin flip. Told the answer with a causal explanation from the AI, 57%. Asked a question instead -- *if one person played violent video games and was aggressive, does it follow that everyone who plays them will be?* -- 67%. And the advantage of asking over telling showed up specifically on the flawed statements, which is where discernment is the whole job.

The part I find most useful is a smaller finding underneath that one. When the AI *told* people the answer, they reported feeling they had enough information and were less inclined to look further. When it *asked*, they did not. Being told made them stop looking. That is the Deusto result from the other side: the mechanism by which the second number erodes is not that the tool is wrong, it is that being handed an answer switches off the search.

Two things to hold it lightly with, and the authors say both themselves. The AI in that study was always right, so it says nothing about what happens when the tool is wrong. And it was one sitting; nobody came back a month later to see whether the effect had stayed. One lab study, in the right direction, with a mechanism attached. Not the foundation of anything. A real hint that the design space exists.

Now the really sad, and difficult thing, about this, is that it is so, so easy to abrogate that responsibility. When I first started using Claude Code, I went a little mad. From a developer perspective, it gives you the ability to attack a hundred little problems that you knew you would never get to, in a very compressed time frame. But if you don't have the judgment, if you aren't watching it and knowing what the expected shape is *and making sure that's hwere it lands*, then its kinda like having a fixer-upper and then you suddenly do every outstanding improvement chore you had, in one long jag, over 72 or so, without ever checking if any of the individualized components were done properly. You can certainly do it if you choose to, but you've just built yourself a self-detonating mine field, *and you don't even know where you placed the mines/*

## Measure the second number yourself, and keep measuring it, because nobody else will, and the discipline is how you protect the judgment

Here is the constructive part, and it is genuinely constructive, because unlike almost everything else in this newsletter it is entirely inside your own control and it costs nothing.

**Pick the one or two things you are accountable for and would be embarrassed to be unable to do alone.** Not everything. The reconciliation you sign. The architecture call on a component design. The diagnosis, the clause, the estimate. The work where, if the tool were wrong and you could not tell, it would still be your name on it, and it would be a problem if you got it wrong.

**Then, on a schedule appropriate to the regularness of the task, but generally at least a month after you outsourced it for the first time, do one with the tool switched off.** Once a month. Once a quarter. Whatever you can actually keep to. Not as a purity test or a virtue signal or anything else performative -- but, rather, as a measurement. You are not proving you do not need the tool. You are finding out whether the second number has moved and you can no longer perform the task with the same level of skill, before the day it matters is concurrently the day you find out you don't know how to do it properly anymore.

**And when it has moved, treat that as information, not failure.** The Polish endoscopists were not lazy. The doctors in the trial were not careless. The students who pasted the question in were doing what the test rewarded. The second number moves because the first number is being optimized, and that is the correct thing to optimize most of the time. The point is only to know which of your capacities you have quietly stopped exercising, so that you can choose to keep the ones you cannot afford to lose.

That is the whole design decision. Use the tool. Let it make you better on the day. And keep one honest rep on the work where being unable to do it alone would be the thing that ends badly.

The reason to do this now, rather than later, is not that the tools are dangerous. It is that the second number is cheap to measure and expensive to recover. A morning a quarter keeps it. A year of not looking loses it. And each of us, as far as I can tell, is the only person in our organization with any reason to check. *No one is going to come along and task cognition to your job description. Its implied by your sitting in the chair, except not any more.*

That last part is not a throwaway. It is tomorrow.
