Five people. One hundred agents.

That is the staffing ratio corporate America is currently reorganizing itself around. It comes from McKinsey's "The Agentic Organization" report: a human team of two to five people can already supervise an agent factory of fifty to a hundred specialized agents running an end-to-end process -- onboarding a customer, launching a product, closing the books. It is a spectacular number. Boards have seen it. Consultants have built decks on it. Somewhere right now, a headcount plan is being drawn against it.

Now go read the sentence it lives in, because it opens with three words that should stop you cold: *"In our experience."*

Not "in our study." Not "across our benchmark of client deployments, measured over such-and-such a period." In our experience. The ratio that is redrawing org charts is an anecdote from client work, published back in September 2025, and it has been compounding through boardrooms ever since without anyone going back to check what was actually underneath it.

Think about how every other dangerous ratio in your life got set. Your local pool posts one lifeguard per however-many swimmers because somebody, somewhere, did the drowning math -- insurers, regulators, decades of grim actuarial homework. Nurse-to-patient ratios are fought over in legislatures precisely because everyone knows what happens on the wrong side of them. The ratios we trust with consequences are the ones somebody was forced to defend.

The agent-factory ratio is not one of those. It is a brochure number. And this week I want to pull on what is standing behind it -- because the answer, five days from now, is going to turn out to be the question of who actually controls anything in this new arrangement.

## The seat you fought for

Two weeks ago I spent five days arguing that certain human seats are load-bearing -- the explainer, the qualified approver, the boundary-seer, the Grower. Last week I argued that the account you can keep around a machine is the fence you build, not the path you can't trace, and that a qualified human has to stand on that wall. If you have been reading along, you already know who the two-to-five people in McKinsey's ratio have to be. They are exactly the people from "Now the Hard Part: The Seats That Can't Go Empty." We spent July establishing that they must exist and what they must be able to do.

Consider that argument won. Here is the harder question the winning bought us, and it is this week's question: *what does that seat actually control?*

Because a seat and control are not the same thing. A pilot's seat with no yoke in front of it is a chair with a view. This week -- the models, the exhaust, the regulators, the enforcement layer -- is one long walk through everything flowing through that seat that the person sitting in it does not currently govern. Today's job is just to show you the cockpit.

## Nobody will stand behind the number

Start with the ratio itself, because the story gets better the closer you look.

Three months ago, Deloitte looked at the same question -- how many agents can a human actually supervise -- and did something consultancies almost never do: it declined to answer. Its operating-model work poses "How many digital agents can a human manage and be accountable for?" as an open question. Read that again. One consultancy asserts a span of control from anecdote; the other, staring at the same market, says out loud that nobody knows.

The rest of the industry isn't converging either. McKinsey itself now says it runs some 25,000 agents alongside roughly 40,000 humans -- a firm eating its own ratio at scale. Rivals at EY and PwC have publicly pushed back on agent-count as a metric at all, on the sensible grounds that a thousand trivial agents are worth less than five good ones.

The most honest number anyone has produced so far comes from Replit, which instrumented its own organization and reported 2.9x code output per engineer with quality metrics flat -- and even that comes with its caveats attached, which I respect them for publishing: the headline metric is lines of code (a thing agents inflate happily), and Replit sells agent tooling, so this is a company reporting favorable numbers about its own product category. It is the best data point available. It is also, notice, not a span-of-control number at all.

So here is where we actually are. The one number reorganizing work in 2026 is the one number nobody who produced it will stand behind as measurement. Every profession that supervises dangerous things at scale -- aviation, nursing, air traffic control -- earned its ratios through bodies and lawsuits and decades. We are setting ours from a slide.

## Whose humans are they, anyway

It gets one turn stranger, and this is the detail I can't stop thinking about.

While everyone argues about how many humans the agent factory needs, the hyperscalers have quietly started paying to *be* those humans. Microsoft has committed $2.5 billion and AWS a further $1 billion to programs that embed their own AI engineers physically inside customer organizations -- not selling to your team, sitting *on* your team, building your agent factory from a desk in your building.

On one reading, that's generosity: the talent barrier is real and someone is removing it. On the reader-of-this-newsletter reading: walk back through the two-to-five seats we just established are load-bearing, and now imagine a couple of them wearing a vendor's lanyard. The person defining what your agents do, judging their output, and correcting their mistakes -- the person in the seat we spent two weeks fortifying -- is on someone else's payroll, building on someone else's platform, and going home with everything they learned about how your business actually runs. Your data governance, your model provenance, your exit planning: all of it now has a third party standing quietly in the middle of it, and he brought his own chair.

## The part Nadella already told you

Which brings me to the thing the ratio conversation never mentions, and the reason this is a week-long argument rather than a Monday complaint.

Those two-to-five humans have one core job: correcting the machine. Catching the agent's mistake, fixing the draft, refusing the bad output, steering the process back. That is the load-bearing work. And in mid-July, Microsoft's own CEO published a warning about exactly that work: every correction your people make to an AI system teaches the provider's model something about how your business operates. He called it the Reverse Information Paradox -- you pay for the intelligence with money, and then pay again with the institutional know-how you must reveal to make the intelligence useful.

Sit with the shape of that for a second. The fewer humans in the factory, the more concentrated their judgment -- and the more valuable each correction they make becomes, to somebody who is not you. Your five survivors are simultaneously the control layer *and* the leak. That is not a side effect. That is the architecture. Tomorrow I am going to take that apart properly, because the CEO who issued the warning happens to run the company selling the product.

And there's one more line item for the cockpit inventory, because it decides whether any of this is sustainable: where do the five humans come from? The rung that has always grown them is the one being cut. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from ADP payroll data, finds workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen a 16 percent relative decline in employment since late 2022, controlling for firm-level shocks -- while older colleagues in the same jobs held steady or grew. Young software developers alone are down about a fifth from their late-2022 peak. The survivors in your ratio came from somewhere, and we are dismantling the somewhere. Saturday's piece is about how to rebuild it on purpose.

## What you control from the seat

So, Monday inventory. The ratio governing your headcount plan: an anecdote nobody will defend as measurement. The humans in the ratio: increasingly, on loan from your vendor. Their daily work: exhaust flowing to a model provider under an agreement you probably haven't read with that question in mind. Their replacements: not being grown. And all of it sitting under governance documents that -- Friday's piece, and I'll prove it -- mostly can't stop any of this at runtime.

That is the week. Tuesday, the exhaust. Wednesday, what happens when an entire country adopts at record speed with a framework that is still a press release. Thursday, the fight over who even gets to decide which models exist. Friday, what a control actually is, technically, when someone finally builds one. Sunday, what it adds up to.
