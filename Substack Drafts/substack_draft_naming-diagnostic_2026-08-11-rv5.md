Two documents came out of the same company this year. They do not agree with each other. And read together, they tell us a whole lot about the current state of AI in most organizations.

The first was a press release in the spring, announcing a restructuring. It used the word "efficiencies," it used the phrase "AI-enabled," and it said a number of roles were being eliminated because the work they did was now being done by software. The second was a job posting in the summer, on the *same company's* own careers page, hiring for that work. For those exact same roles, in fact. Same function. Slightly different title. Salary band a little higher, because they had to go get someone back, and so it ended up costing more to bring them in off the beach.

I am not going to name them, partly because I would have to pick one. Nobody sent out a release for the second document. There is no press cycle for a walk-back. But of the two, the second one is the honest one, because a press release costs nothing to write and a headcount requisition has to survive a finance review. One of those is talk. The other is money leaving the building.

## The declaration and the container

I imagine that almost everyone reading this has filled out a customs form. You get the little card on the plane, or the kiosk asks you on the way through, and the question is always the same. Anything to declare? And you tick no, because you are carrying socks.

The interesting thing about that question is not that people answer it honestly. Plenty do not. The interesting thing is that the answer is *checkable*. Somebody can open the bag. What you wrote down and what is actually in there are two separate objects, and the space between them has a shape, and that shape is the only reason the system functions at all.

Scale it up and it is the entire apparatus my industry spends its compliance budget on. A shipping container crossing a border carries a declaration: what goods, what quantity, what value, whose they are. Customs, duty, AML, all of it rests on somebody having written down what was in the box and signed their name under it. Not because declarations are always true. Because a declaration is falsifiable, and an organization that has to write one down has to decide what it actually believes before it can lie about it.

"We're using AI" is a container with no declaration on it.

That is the whole argument of this piece, so I will say it plainly before I spend the next few thousand words on it. An organization that can tell you which specific tasks moved to an agent, and which specific judgment calls stayed with a person, and who signed for each side of that line, is doing the work. An organization that can only tell you it is "using AI" or "we're an AI-forward business" has not told you anything you can check. And a claim you cannot check is not a claim. It is a costume, in the guise of something knowable, in a situation where it is *intended* to be knowable, and that in and of itself should tell you a lot.

## What the numbers actually say, and what they do not

Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracks announced job cuts in the US. In May, of 97,006 announced cuts, 38,579 were attributed to artificial intelligence. About forty percent. And May was the third of what is now five straight months in which AI led every other stated reason, a run that carried through July.

Now the part almost nobody says out loud. Challenger counts *stated reasons.* The stated reason comes from the employer, in the announcement, written by the same communications function that wrote the press release. It is a superb measure of what companies are saying. It is not a measure of what happened to the work. My own notes from July already had this flagged, in fewer words: the AI attribution measures the press release, not the P&L.

And you do not have to take my word for it, because the firm that publishes the number says so itself. Andy Challenger, their chief revenue officer, put it plainly in the July release: naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors while pushing current and prospective employees away, and that is why the messaging has swung from hedging to aggressively citing it. He went further, and this is the part that should worry anyone who plans to keep counting: as regulation develops, companies will get more careful about what they announce, which makes the real impact harder to track rather than easier.

So what does the inside of the container look like? Orgvue asked three hundred US HR managers what actually drove their AI deployments, and I will be honest about the size and the timing of that survey in a minute. Only twenty-six percent said cost saving was the real rationale; economic conditions and restructuring both scored higher. And sixty-nine percent said AI is being used to justify a broader range of change initiatives than the ones it is actually driving.

That is the load-bearing finding and it did not make a single headline I saw. More than two thirds of the people closest to these decisions are watching AI get used as a reason-shaped object. Not as a cause. As a justification, applied afterward, to a decision that had already been made for reasons that are older and duller and much harder to put in a press release. Interest rates. A bad quarter. A restructuring that was coming anyway. Sam Altman, who has rather a lot of incentive to say the opposite, conceded the point in February: there is some AI washing, he said, where people are blaming AI for layoffs they would have done anyway, and there is also some real displacement. He added that he did not know what the exact percentage was. I find I trust the hedge rather more than I would have trusted a confident number.

And from the same work, the one that made me sit up. Twenty-three percent based those layoff decisions on general assumptions about AI rather than any role-specific analysis. *Nearly a quarter of them never opened the container at all.*

The community has landed on a name for this, and it is being called "AI washing". I mean the workforce version: using AI as the publicly acceptable reason for a cut you were making anyway. Not the securities version, which is a different and legally sharper thing that the SEC has been settling cases over for a couple of years now. Same phrase, different crime, and only one of them currently has a regulator attached.

## Then the receipt arrives

Here is where it stops being a communications problem.

On his daily brief last week, Nathaniel Whittemore -- who runs the AI Daily Brief, and is one of the handful of people in this space I listen to every morning -- picked up a line from Julie Averill, who used to run technology at Lululemon and therefore knows from the inside exactly what she is describing. Writing in the New York Times, her point was that the AI layoff is frequently a cut where the efficiency does not exist yet and what is really being freed up is cash. Whittemore's compression of that was a cash grab wearing an efficiency costume, and it is the version I have not been able to stop thinking about since. He put a number next to it: roughly a third of hiring managers who cut a role for AI have already rehired for the same or a similar one.

A third. Already. Not eventually, not in some slow correction over a decade. These cuts are months old. The rehires are weeks old. The gap is pretty much exactly how long it takes for senior management to realize they have cut not fat, but muscle and bone.

Now, I promised I would be honest about my numbers, and this is the place to do it, because I am about to argue that you should distrust unverifiable claims and it would be a fine thing indeed if I passed you one on the way past, in the tradition of pretty much every talking head ever.

That one-in-three figure traces to Robert Half. Their published wording is that more than three in ten US hiring managers who eliminated positions after implementing AI later had to add those roles, or very similar ones, back. It is first-party research and the phrasing lines up exactly with how it is being quoted. It also has no sample size, no fielding date, and no methodology statement published by Robert Half itself beyond "conducted by an independent research firm." One outlet has since reported a sample of two thousand hiring managers, which Robert Half has not published anywhere I can find. I went looking. It is not in a press release, it is not on the wire, it is on a marketing page. And Robert Half is a staffing company, which is to say that the business they are in is placing the rehire.

I still think it is probably directionally right, because the separate Orgvue work found thirty-two percent of organizations that made redundancies on the cost-saving promise of AI have had to rehire. But those are two different firms measuring two different things, and the fact that they land on similar figures is a coincidence rather than a corroboration. I have seen at least one outlet stack them in the same paragraph as though they confirmed each other. They do not. Two independent estimates landing in the same place is not the same as either one being checked. 

So: the rehire number is the hook. It is not the argument. The argument is the twenty-three percent who never did the analysis, and the sixty-nine percent watching the language get borrowed for other purposes. Those I will stand behind.

The sharpest version of the receipt is not a survey anyway. It is Ford. Bloomberg reported, quoting Charles Poon, the company's vice president of vehicle hardware engineering, that Ford has been bringing veteran engineers back, something on the order of three hundred to three hundred and fifty of them, after concluding it had let its most experienced people go before their knowledge could be used to train the AI systems meant to replace them. Poon's own account of it is the least defensive thing I have read from a manufacturer in years: mistakenly, he said, they thought that introducing artificial intelligence and feeding it the design requirements they already had would produce a high quality product. The company reckons the detour cost it about three years.

Read the shape of that. The cut destroyed the training data for the automation that justified the cut. That is not a story about AI being oversold. That is a story about an organization that could not name what it had, and so could not tell the difference between a headcount line and thirty years of knowing which failures matter. You cannot put that on a spreadsheet, which is precisely why it went out the door first. And it is a corollary argument to my whole series of "Build the Rung" structures, from the other side -- removing the rung can be just as bad for the current and the future of the enterprise, regardless of where the rung is on the ladder. There was a reason it was there in the first place: you better be sure you knew what it was and what's the real replacement before you rip it out.

And the cost of this AI Theatre is not theoretical. You pay severance. You pay a recruiter. You pay above the old band, because you are hiring back into a market you just told the world you had automated, and everyone in that market read the press release too. You pay for the ramp. Then you pay the opportunity cost of those three years, which in this particular window is the most expensive currency there is.

I have been there for this conversation. I know how hard it is to fight back when finance, and the board, and everyone else says "we need to cut the headcount", and it doesn't matter if its sinew or not. And then watched the contracts land to support the headcount a month later and know that operationally you were starting back over from scratch. It's disheartening for everyone -- not least of all the people who watched their peers walk out and someone else walk in for no reason beyond a couple of weeks -- and I have felt the loss of the performance, the knowledge, and tribal integration that comes along with it. No matter how much the financial piece bites (and it does), the cultural piece bites even harder.

## Read the posting, not the headline

The good news is that this is diagnosable from the outside, and it takes about twenty minutes.

Go look at what a company is actually hiring for. Not what it announced. What it is paying a recruiter to fill this quarter. A job posting is an expensive, slow, internally-negotiated document that has to get past a hiring manager, a finance approval, and a compensation band. It is the closest thing to a declaration a company produces without meaning to.

And the postings are legible now in a way they were not eighteen months ago. There is a whole cohort of roles that exist to hold the line this piece is about. There is a job whose entire function is to sit between what the business wants and what an agent can be trusted to do, and write that down explicitly. There is one whose function is to ask whether a given AI system is making money or just consuming budget, which is a question nobody had to ask in 2024. There is one whose function is to check the machine's reasoning and document how the thing operates, so that a person can be accountable for an output they did not personally produce.

I spent a recent Thursday on what those roles mean for a career, and I am not going to re-run it. Today they are evidence of something else entirely. Every one of those postings is a company writing down where the line is. You do not create a role to audit a decision boundary unless someone has decided where the boundary sits. You do not hire a person to own the economics of an agent unless you have decided the agent has economics worth owning.

So the diagnostic is a comparison, and you can run it on any company you like, including the one that pays you. Put the announcement next to the requisition. Does the org that told the market AI absorbed a function have anyone on staff whose job is to know where that function's edges are? Or did the work simply get quieter, redistributed onto people who now do it in the gaps between their real jobs, with nobody named, until it fails somewhere expensive?

## Where this argument is weak, and I would rather say so

There are three good objections and I want them on the table, because I would rather hand you the holes than have you find them.

**You cannot read a company's insides from its outsides.** This is the real one. A press release is written by communications people who were not in the decision, and plenty of serious organizations say nothing useful in public while doing perfectly rigorous work behind the door. Equally, a company can produce a beautiful task inventory and still get it catastrophically wrong. Ford almost certainly had detailed plans. Naming is necessary, but alone, it is nowhere near sufficient.

The answer is that the declaration is not the press release. The declaration is whether anybody inside can produce the list on demand. That test is internal, and it is available to you about your own employer, today, for free. For companies you do not work at, the careers page is a better instrument than the announcement not because it is more honest in intent but because it is more expensive to fake. Talk is cheap; on the other hand, *headcount* is a costly signal. For many tech organizations especially, the costliest. That distinction is doing most of the work here, and it's subtle, but material. Take it under advisement.

**Rehiring after layoffs is ordinary.** It is. Companies have always cut too deep and hired back; the general boomerang rate has been studied for years and has nothing to do with AI. If you want to accuse me of finding the business cycle and dressing it up as a morality tale, that is a fair swing (maybe). My answer is that I am not claiming rehiring is new. I am claiming that *this* stated reason was uniquely falsifiable. "The market softened" cannot be proven wrong next quarter. "An agent can do this job" can, and a third of the people who said it have already found out that it in fact couldn't. Not yet, at least.

**Maybe nobody can name it in advance.** The strongest objection, honestly, and it comes from Ethan Mollick's point that a job is a bundle of tasks whose seams only become visible in practice. If that is right, then demanding a task-level inventory up front is demanding a precision that does not exist yet.

I think that is true and I think it strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. You cannot know perfectly in advance. That is exactly why naming has to be a practice instead of a document. The organizations doing this well name it provisionally, ship it, find out where they were wrong, and revise. An org that produced the list once in January and has not touched it since is washing on a longer timescale than the one that never wrote it down. The tell is not the existence of the list. It is the date on the last revision, coupled with the date of the announcement.

## And since this is not the theatre

There is a version of all this that plays well on a stage. A function got absorbed. The org chart got flatter. The future arrived, and it arrived here first.

The actual version is a document nobody will ever tweet. It is a list of tasks with a line drawn through the middle of it. On one side, the things an agent now carries, named individually, with someone accountable for checking the output. On the other, the judgment calls that stayed, named just as specifically, with a person's name against each one. A date at the top. A date on the last revision, which should be recent. It is an inventory rather than a vision, and inventories are boring, and the boringness is the point, because it is the only part of this that is checkable.

## Since it's a Tuesday, and Tuesdays are the days to do miscellaneous things

So here is the thing to do with this, and it costs you one email.

Ask your own organization for the list. Which tasks moved to agents. Which judgment calls stayed with people. Who signed for each. When it was last revised. You are entitled to ask, whatever seat you are in, and the asking is not hostile -- if the work has been done, someone will be pleased you noticed.

If the list comes back inside a week, you work somewhere that is building, and you should take that seriously as a piece of information about your employer that you did not have on Monday. If it does not come back, or if what comes back is a paragraph about the company's commitment to innovation, then you have your answer too. It does not mean AI is doing the work. It means a decision got made, and AI was handed the microphone afterward.

Anything to declare? Everybody ticks no. The container is the same either way. The only difference is whether anybody was willing to write down what was inside it.

Every serious control I have ever trusted looked like that. Not a statement of intent. A list somebody signed, codified, secured, and distributed. That is a control.
