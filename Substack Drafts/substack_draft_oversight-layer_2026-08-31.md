Every AI governance framework in circulation ends the same way. The last box says the board oversees it.

It is the final line of the runbook. Escalate to the board. Every maturity model, every vendor deck, every internal policy I have read this year terminates in that sentence, and it gets treated as the part you do not have to explain, the way a fire evacuation plan does not bother to explain the street outside. The assumption sitting underneath it is that somebody is at the end of that escalation path who can take the call, follow what they are being told, and tell whether the answer they are getting is a good one or just a well-delivered one.

I went looking for that person in the disclosure data, because that is the only place you can look for them without asking anybody how they feel about themselves.

They are not there.

## What the S&P 500 wrote down about itself

The Conference Board went through S&P 500 company disclosures as of December 2025. Over a single year, the share of companies naming AI as a material risk went from 12% to 83%. Disclosed director technology expertise went from 20% to 51%. Disclosed cybersecurity expertise went from 15% to 27%.

Disclosed AI expertise went from 1.5% to 2.7%.

Put the first number and the last number next to each other and hold them there for a second. 83% of the S&P 500 has told its own investors, in a filing, that AI is a material risk to the business. 2.7% of the directors of those companies disclose any AI expertise at all.

I want to be careful about what kind of number that is, because it matters and because most of the numbers on this beat are the other kind. This one is disclosure-derived. Nobody surveyed a group of directors and asked how confident they felt. Somebody read what the companies themselves filed, under the rules that govern what a company is allowed to say about its own board, and counted. Companies have every incentive to disclose expertise they have. The 2.7% is not a measure of what directors know. It is a measure of what their own employers were willing to put in writing about what they know, which is a floor rather than a ceiling, and the floor is 2.7%.

Heidrick & Struggles looked at the incoming class rather than the sitting one. Board Monitor US 2026, covering 383 Fortune 500 seats filled during 2025: only 6% of newly appointed directors had previously served as a chief technology officer, chief information officer, chief data officer or chief AI officer. That is the level, and I am going to resist the sentence everybody writes next, because I cannot support it -- I have seen no evidence that number has fallen. It has not needed to fall. It has been low the whole time, while the risk disclosures went up by a factor of seven.

## The part where everybody is sure they are fine

Here is where it gets uncomfortable rather than merely structural.

BCG surveyed 351 chief executives and 274 directors in March of this year. About 60% of those CEOs said their boards are rushing AI transformation. Not pushing, not urging, not demanding more ambition. Rushing, in the sense you would use about somebody taking a corner too fast.

The directors do not agree. Fifty-four percent of them said the organization is moving at the right pace, and 18% said it is moving too slowly and that this concerns them.

Two things about that survey before I build anything on it. The 60% is 60% of the 351 chief executives, not of the 625 people in the study, and those are different populations answering different questions. And the sample is 44% US-based and 76% private companies over $100 million in revenue, with no Australian sub-sample disclosed at all. So this is not a statistic about listed-company boardrooms, and it is certainly not one about ours. It is a real measurement of a real disagreement inside a specific kind of company, and that is all it is.

What makes it worth the space is the line underneath the headline, which BCG published and almost nobody has quoted:

> "frustration with the pace of adoption is highest in those who have a more permissive view on governance."

And next to it:

> "lower levels of self-assessed AI understanding may be fueling board FOMO... Board members with less confidence in their AI knowledge are more likely to believe their organizations are moving too slowly."

Sit with that one. The directors pressing hardest to go faster are, on the same instrument, the ones who report understanding the least about what they are going faster at. That is not a character finding and I do not want to read it as one. It is the ordinary human response to being in a room where you cannot evaluate the thing under discussion: you cannot assess the risk of moving, so you assess the risk of being left behind, which is the only risk in the room you are qualified to see.

The same survey found that 75% of board members believe their AI knowledge is on par with or more advanced than their peers'.

That is a self-assessment and the 2.7% is a disclosure count, and they are different instruments measuring different things -- one is what people think of themselves, the other is what their company filed. I am not going to pretend they are the same number seen twice. But the distance between them is the actual finding of this piece, because three-quarters of a group rating themselves at or above the middle is not a claim about AI. It is a claim about the room. It means the room has no calibration, and a room with no calibration cannot tell a good answer from a confident one, which is the entire job.

## Australia, where the directors are telling you the truth

The Australian data is smaller in reputation and better in construction.

The AICD's Director Sentiment Index for the first half of 2026 ran through Roy Morgan, fielded to 828 company directors between 20 February and 10 March. More than half of them said the pace of change is faster than their organizations can keep up with. Almost two-thirds reported that AI tools have already delivered productivity benefits, so this is not a group of skeptics talking themselves out of the technology. And they put vulnerability to cyber-crime, data security and the potential misuse of AI at the top of the risk list.

American chief executives report being rushed by their boards. Australian directors report being outpaced by the world. Same year, opposite direction, and the Australian sample is the sounder of the two -- 828 directors in one jurisdiction, a named fieldhouse and a stated fieldwork window, against 351 executives spread across an undisclosed mix of markets.

I find the Australian answer more useful, and not only because it is my second audience. It is the answer of people who have correctly identified that they are behind. That is the beginning of governance. The other answer is the beginning of a purchase order.

## Your own signals directorate already wrote the questions

On 5 August, the Australian Signals Directorate (the agency that does signals intelligence and national cyber security, so roughly the NSA and CISA in one building) published *Frontier AI cyber threat considerations for boards of directors*, developed with the AICD.

Three weeks before this piece. Aimed directly at the people in the room.

The document is careful with its verbs and the care is worth copying. On attack outcomes it hedges: frontier AI **can** make cyber attacks faster, more effective and easier to carry out, and this **may** rapidly invalidate an organization's current risk tolerance. Then, on the underlying change, it stops hedging entirely:

> "Overall, frontier AI models are currently, and will continue to, fundamentally transform both offensive and defensive cyber capabilities."

That is a signals agency writing in the present indicative about transformation and in the conditional about consequences, which is exactly the right way around and almost the reverse of how it reaches most boards.

So the gap I have been describing is not a foreign statistic any more. Your own national security agency has written down the questions your board should be able to answer, and put them somewhere free and public, and dated them three weeks before you read this. The gap is now measurable at your own table, in an afternoon, with a document nobody has to buy.

## What the obligation actually is, and what it is not

The AICD, with the Human Technology Institute at UTS, publishes a separate document -- *A Director's Guide to AI Governance*, second edition, June 2026 -- and its central sentence is the standing obligation:

> "Boards are ultimately accountable for the adoption, implementation and outcomes of AI technologies in their organisations."

The same guide is realistic about what that requires. Directors "do not need to be AI experts. But they do need a minimum viable understanding of AI to ask the right questions." That is the correct bar and it is a low one, which is what makes 2.7% and 6% so awkward. Nobody is asking a board to build the model. They are asking it to notice when the answer it just received was fluent and hollow.

I want to be precise about the legal position, because this beat attracts overreach and I would rather under-claim it. In Europe, NIS2 Article 20(1) is actually law and actually bites: management bodies of essential and important entities must approve the cyber risk-management measures, oversee their implementation, and "can be held liable for infringements." That is a hard obligation with a hard consequence.

Everything else you have read is doctrine, not case law. There is no decided AI-Caremark case. Not one. The Delaware oversight authority everybody cites in these pieces predates all of this by years, and the legal commentary is careful with its modals in a way the summaries of it are not: liability **may** arise, a board **may** find, AI does not transform the oversight duty so much as stress-test it. Every headline telling you that directors are now liable for AI failures has taken a *may* and quietly promoted it to a *does*. The doctrine is solid and the exposure is real and the cases have not happened yet, and you can hold all three of those at once.

The AICD's guide also characterizes ASIC as extending director duties to AI adoption. Note the shape of that sentence: it is the AICD's reading of the regulator, not the regulator's own words, and I have not been able to foot it to an ASIC primary. Treat it accordingly.

## What we did last time

We have built a missing oversight layer before, and it is worth remembering how, because it was not by hiring geniuses.

After the accounting failures of the early 2000s, the fix for boards that could not read their own financials was not a requirement that every director become an accountant. It was a disclosure rule: say whether your audit committee has a financial expert, and if it does not, say why not. Comply or explain. Nobody was compelled to have one. The rule simply made the absence visible, and visible absences get filled, because no board wants to write down that sentence twice.

That is the whole mechanism. It is unglamorous and it worked.

Read the 2.7% again with that in mind. AI risk disclosure is now near-universal, and it sits in the same documents, filed under the same rules, read by the same investors. The expertise disclosure line is right there next to it. The absence is already visible. What has not happened yet is anybody being made to explain it.

## So go and find the end of your own escalation path

**Read your last AI paper to the board again, and mark every sentence a reasonable person could not check.** Not the ones that are wrong. The ones that are unfalsifiable from the chair: the maturity ratings, the "on track" statuses, the risk scores with no arithmetic behind them. That is the material your oversight layer is currently being asked to oversee, and if most of the page is unfalsifiable then the meeting was a briefing rather than a review, whatever the minutes say.

**Then take the ASD document to the next meeting and just work through it out loud.** It is free, it is short, it is written by your own government rather than by anybody selling you anything, and it converts an argument about board composition into an afternoon of specific questions with specific answers. You will find out very quickly whether the gap at your table is knowledge, capacity or attention, and those three have completely different fixes.

**And ask what your own filings say about your board.** Not what your governance framework says. What the disclosure says, in the document your investors actually read. If your company is in the 83% that names AI as a material risk, look at the expertise line a few pages away and decide whether the two pages are describing the same organization.

The oversight layer is not missing because anybody decided against it. It is missing because every framework in circulation treats it as the part that was already there, the way you assume the street outside the fire door exists because the plan has an arrow pointing at it.

So the question is not whether your board has an AI expert, and I would be suspicious of anybody selling you one. It is narrower and considerably harder. Who in that room can tell a good answer from a confident one, and how would you find out before it is the answer that matters?
