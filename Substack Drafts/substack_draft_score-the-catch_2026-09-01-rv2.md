Every framework designed more than six weeks ago for deciding what to hand an agent scores the same thing. It scores the *work*.

There is a control in every finance function called "four eyes" (no, not the childhood insult). Two people have to approve the payment before it moves. It is a genuinely good control -- good primarily in its simplicity -- and it has one failure mode that everybody in the building knows about and nobody writes on the control matrix: quite often the second pair of eyes belongs to somebody with no practical way of knowing whether the number is right. They are not lazy and they are not negligent. They are a signature, and the signature is what makes the transaction auditable, and the auditability is now the thing being produced rather than the checking. (To put this into our AI-focused context, this is where the HITL just approves things because every thing before it has been OK so this one must be too.)

Last Tuesday the skill was working out what an agent is allowed to see before you worry about what it is allowed to do. This one is the question that arrives immediately afterward, and it is the one I have not seen a single fully-adopted framework ask: whether you would know if it got it wrong. It is, however, from what I can tell, one that is quickly becoming an audit focus among the AI-pilled businesses all across the US.

## The audit, and it is the best of them

The cleanest version of the delegation question I have come across ran on Nathaniel Whittemore's AI Daily Brief a couple of weeks back, framed as a deputization audit. You score a task on five dimensions, zero to two each. How often you do it. How teachable it is. How checkable it is. What the stakes are. And how much it really has to be you. Eight or above and you hand it over.

It is a good instrument and I want to be fair to it before I take it apart. It is concrete, it takes four minutes, and it gets people to do the thing that actually matters, which is looking at their own work as a set of separable pieces instead of as one undifferentiated pile called "my job." The episode does not claim any underlying research and does not attribute it to anyone, which I read as honest rather than sloppy -- the shape is the standard automation-suitability matrix that has been in operations textbooks for decades, and offering it without a fake pedigree is the correct move.

Now look at the five dimensions again and ask what each one is a property of.

Frequency is a property of the work. Teachability is a property of the work. Stakes are a property of the work. How-much-it-needs-to-be-you is a property of the work, dressed up in the second person.

Only one of the five is about you at all, and it is checkability, and it gets asked with a stopwatch: how long does verifying this take compared to just doing it?

That is a cost question. The binding constraint is not cost.

## The difference between expensive and impossible

I can verify a restaurant bill in fifteen seconds. I cannot verify a structural engineering calculation in any amount of time, and neither can you unless you are a structural engineer, and no stopwatch in the world will tell us that, because the stopwatch measures the check we are capable of performing rather than the check the work requires.

Verification cost and verification capability are different quantities. Every AI-task delegation framework I have seen measures the first one and silently assumes the second. And the assumption is doing enormous work, because the tasks that score highest on all of these instruments -- frequent, teachable, low individual stakes, does not have to be you -- are precisely the tasks where your own capability is thinnest, for the entirely rational reason that you stopped paying close attention to them years ago. That is why they scored well. That is what "it does not have to be me" means.

So the honest version of the checkability question is not how long would this take me to check. It is something considerably less comfortable. If this came back wrong, would I know? By what specific mechanism would I know -- a failing test, a customer, a reconciliation that stops balancing, or my own read of it? And if I found out, what would I actually do at two in the morning?

A task can score a nine and still fail all three of those, and when it does, handing it over is not a productivity decision. It is a decision to *stop knowing*.

## The rubber stamp is worse than doing nothing

Here is the part that I think matters most, and it is the reason I am writing this as a security person rather than a productivity person.

A task that scores well and gets rubber-stamped is worse than the same task done badly by hand.

Not equally bad. Worse. Because the rubber stamp produces something the unchecked work does not produce, which is a record that a human being reviewed it. That record is now in your ticket history, your approval log, your change management system and, eventually, somebody's evidence pack. It says a named person with authority looked at this and signed off. Every downstream control that trusts that record is now trusting a signature that carries no information.

You have not just failed to catch the error. You have manufactured the artifact that stops anybody else from looking for it. In a system with layered controls, that is the single most expensive thing you can do, and it is completely invisible, because everything looks exactly like compliance.

## The regulators already drew this line, and nobody's framework has

The thing that struck me hardest while I was reading around this is that the distinction the delegation frameworks are missing is not a new or exotic idea. It has already been drawn, in plain language, by people who were thinking about it earlier and harder, and in one case it is written into law.

France's CNIL, with CIANum, published a joint exploratory note on agentic AI on 20 July. The sentence is worth having in the original French, since it is entirely possible my translation misses some nuance:

> "l'intervention humaine doit être réelle, effective et exercer une influence sur la décision finale ; une validation purement formelle ou automatique est insuffisante."

**Human intervention has to be real, effective, and actually influence the final decision. A purely formal or automatic validation is insufficient.**

That is the rubber stamp, named as a failure of compliance rather than a failure of diligence, by a data protection regulator, three weeks before the framework that needed it went out. Three weeks, and the two never met.

The EU AI Act does the same job from the other side. Article 14 is about human oversight, and it names three things that no delegation audit I have seen mentions once: automation bias, which is the documented human tendency to defer to a machine's output specifically because a machine produced it; a standing authority to override, meaning the person is actually permitted to say no rather than merely present at the moment of approval; and a stop button. Those are capability requirements. They are asking whether the human in the loop can do anything, not whether the human in the loop exists.

And a DeepMind paper from February makes the same point in research language. Its own criticism of the field is that existing task decomposition and delegation methods "rely on simple heuristics," and what it proposes instead explicitly incorporates the "transfer of authority, responsibility, accountability."

Read that list slowly, because the three do not travel together. Authority is what you hand over when you let the agent act. Responsibility is your duty of care to make sure it is done right. Accountability is what stays with you whatever you hand over. Every framework in circulation is a procedure for moving the first one, and I have not found one that mentions the third at all. The second will never move, and you should not want it to, because it will serve you up into the third if you do not continue to carry it. The scoring instrument transfers authority and is silent on accountability, and the silence is not an oversight in the drafting. It is the entire reason the instrument feels so good to fill in.

I am going to give you a really clear example of what I am talking about here. When I started writing again, after the first week, I went back and looked at the pieces I had written. And I found, to my great displeasure, certain recurring tells in my writing (much as I have in my speech) that I was inadvertently repeating, treating each piece as a standalone rather than as part of a corpus. So, I used my AI buddies to build an editorial panel that watches out not only for the things I know but also the things I do not. The writing voice, the critic, the skeptic, the industry insider, the researcher -- all of these voices on the panel help me not only clarify my writing and make it better, but also be sure that my facts and figures are all footed and well-researched (and I still miss stuff from time to time, and the panel does too!) This editorial panel is a delegation of a responsibility that is better served, in general, by something else not me doing it (that is why writers have editors in the first place). And it is a delegation with a bar attached, and the bar IS the catch -- six seats, a numeric floor, a categorical fail condition, and a loop that does not stop until it clears my minimum criteria for clarity and accuracy. I did not build this to make drafting faster, and I certainly did not build it because I do not have my own opinions -- I defined it because I could not catch my own tell. And the truth, despite tweaking that panel almost every week as I find more and more things that I perhaps had not considered at the onset, *is that the panel still misses things*, that I only find those things myself when I re-read it again (which I always do before publication, and usually three or four times), and so the final review is always mine, the final responsibility is always mine, and the accountability when I get it wrong is, and remains, always mine. No matter how many elements I delegate, those three items always remain true.

## And the small thing that proves the whole point

One more, and I am offering it in the spirit of the argument rather than as a takedown, because I think it is the most useful accidental demonstration on the subject.

The same episode that hands you the checkability framework recommends, as a place to start, ChatGPT's Computer History feature, and describes it as learning from everything you do on a computer.

OpenAI's own documentation says something narrower. The feature records interaction events and does not capture your screen or your audio. It is off by default for Pro, Business and Enterprise users in the desktop app on macOS. The raw event files are retained for up to 48 hours and are not used for training.

And then the part that the one-line summary and the reassurance both miss: the memory files the feature derives from those events sit on your own filesystem until you delete them, and the chat content that results may be used to improve OpenAI's models subject to your data controls. Two different tiers with two different rules, which is neither the scary version nor the comfortable one.

A framework about checkability, recommending a tool it did not check. Nobody was careless and nobody was selling anything. It is just genuinely hard, and it is hard in exactly the way the framework is trying to tell you it is hard, and as with so many things in this newly complex world where rules-driven computer software is becoming a thing of the past, there is no easy answer. You need to know the problem exists and you need to be conscious of it to navigate it, and because the two practices seem internally inconsistent, that can be incredibly hard in its own right.

## Sort them into two columns

Take the last five things you handed to an agent this week (and if you do not have five, take one, or two). Real ones, from your actual history, not the ones you would like to be handing over.

Put each one in one of two columns. Left column: if this came back wrong, something other than my own judgment would tell me. A test fails. A total stops matching. A customer replies. A build breaks. Right column: the only thing standing between wrong and shipped is me reading it and feeling like it is fine.

The left column is finished. Those are properly delegated and you can leave them alone and stop thinking about them, which is the actual prize here.

The right column is the whole job, and it is almost certainly shorter than you expect, which is the good news I did not anticipate when I started doing this myself. You do not have to become expert enough to catch everything. You have to know which of these things you can catch, and then do something structural about the handful you cannot -- give one to somebody who can, put a real check underneath another one, or make the third small enough that being wrong is survivable.

That is it. Not a program, not a maturity model, not another thing to fill in.

The frameworks are all scoring the task because the task is the part that holds still and can be scored. Your ability to catch the thing is the part that moves, and it is the part that decides whether any of this works, and nobody is going to hand you a rubric for it. You may not even be able to build your OWN rubric. It will depend on human judgment right down the line in most cases, and that is the one thing you cannot reliably (at least not yet) delegate to the machine.
