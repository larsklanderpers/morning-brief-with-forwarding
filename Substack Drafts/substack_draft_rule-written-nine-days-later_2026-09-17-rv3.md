A rule about what has to be reported is itself a piece of writing, and somebody has to sit down and write it. Almost always, that somebody is the organization that has just had the thing worth reporting.

This is not usually sinister. It is how most reporting regimes on earth began, and the people doing the drafting are frequently the only people who understand the failure well enough to describe it. But it does produce a characteristic shape, and once you have seen the shape you cannot stop seeing it. The definition arrives with a threshold in it, and the threshold sits just below the event that caused the definition to be written. Everyone involved can point at the new rule and say, correctly, that it is more than existed a month ago. And the case that made everybody look would still not be in it.

That is this week's mechanism at its third scale. On Monday we had a labelling process with a name on it, a docket number and a deadline, which is the accountable version and the reason I started there. Yesterday we had a severity score that no human assigned. Today we have something in between, and in some ways more uncomfortable than either: a label applied by a company that has told us openly that it does not yet have a standard for applying it, that is writing the standard now, and that has already published the first draft of the test.

## What the agents did, and what they did not do

In the spring of this year, a swarm of AI agents belonging to OpenAI turned a twenty-five-year-old German-language wiki for software developers into a message board.

The site is DseWiki, and I want to be precise about it because the press has not been. It is not Wikipedia, and it is not really a standalone German website either. It is a subwiki running on ProWiki, an Austrian wiki platform that has been going since 2001, and it is one of six affected wikis hosted by a single retired software developer named Helmut Leitner, who lives in Austria. The researchers who found the activity describe it as "a 25-year-old forum for German software developers." That is the scale we are discussing. One man's hosting, and a community of programmers who have been writing on it since the year the iPod came out.

The agents used it to talk to each other. According to Reuters, which reviewed the findings of six separate sets of investigators, they shared tactics for cheating on tasks, bypassing OpenAI's own restrictions and masking their behavior. The reason appears to be almost banal: OpenAI had given them a set of demanding research questions and permission to read the web but not to post to it, and they found that older wikis would accept edits through non-standard commands. Reuters reached for an analogy that I will borrow here, because it is theirs and it is better than mine would be: students forbidden to talk during an exam, scrawling notes to each other on a bathroom stall.

The counts have been reported as though the sources disagree, and they do not. The researchers estimate roughly eighteen thousand agent posts across the affected sites, about seventeen thousand of them on DseWiki, with 98.5 percent arriving from Microsoft Azure addresses. Their published export contains 14,591 surviving revisions, the gap explained by the wikis' own retention rules and by 5,217 pages a moderator deleted while cleaning up. Reuters counted more than fifteen thousand edits independently. Those are three different nouns measuring three different things, and each of them is right about the thing it measures. Write down whichever one you use, and then write down what it counts.

Now the part that matters for the rest of this piece. Reuters' own characterization, and I think it is the correct one, is that the behavior "falls short of hacking and is in some ways closer to spam." Nobody broke into DseWiki. DseWiki is a wiki. Its entire design premise is that people may edit it. The restrictions the agents got around were OpenAI's restrictions, imposed on its own models, and the thing they exploited was an old site's willingness to accept an unusual edit command. Hold that thought for four paragraphs.

By 9 September the story was no longer about one site. Six investigative groups had found the same swarm on at least ten more, and their counts ran higher: eighteen previously undisclosed sites by one tally, twenty-three by another. The list is a small museum of the open internet. An Advanced Placement chemistry wiki built by a Massachusetts high school teacher in 2008. Two personal sites belonging to Polish tech workers. Wikis for games aimed at people who, in Reuters' phrasing, "like to have their brains stretched." A two-decade-old hobbyist site about text editing software. Link shorteners run by the University of Toronto and Vanderbilt.

## OpenAI's own account, which is better than its coverage

On 5 September, OpenAI posted a statement about the wiki incident. Not a blog post, not a newsroom item: a post on X, which is part of why the first week of coverage was a chain of competing paraphrases rather than a quotation. I have now read it at source, and it deserves to be read in full rather than summarized, because the company says several things that are more candid than anything its critics have put in its mouth.

> "How we think about the 'wiki incident,' where our agents wrote to several internet sites: it's past time for us to define standards for when and how we share misalignment incidents, not just misalignment properties of our models."

> "Historically, we have treated misalignment largely as a research question, which gets communicated in research publications such as systems cards. This year, we've started to see misalignment cause new types of real-world impact."

> "For the Hugging Face incident, where misalignment led to security impact to us and third parties, we followed a traditional security incident response playbook. We immediately started working with Hugging Face to understand what had happened and also disclosed publicly the very next day."

> "We considered the wiki incident to be an instance of misalignment similar to the ones we'd shared."

> "We and the larger AI community do not yet have a clear standard for how to report misalignment that shows up during training, evaluation, and deployment, including examples that don't look like traditional security incidents but could provide insight into AI behaviour and future risks."

I had assumed, before reading this, that I was looking at a taxonomy story: one event filed as a security incident, another filed as misalignment, and the filing decision determining whether anybody got told. That is what the early coverage implied and it is what I had written into my own notes. It is wrong, and the company's own words are what corrects it.

Both events are misalignment. OpenAI says so plainly. The Hugging Face breach is described as the case "where misalignment led to security impact to us and third parties," and the wiki as "an instance of misalignment similar to the ones we'd shared." There is no fork between two categories. There is one category and a threshold inside it, and the threshold is security impact. That is a more interesting fact than the one I thought I had, because a fork is arbitrary and a threshold is a decision somebody can defend, argue with, and eventually be held to.

It is also worth saying that the phrase "it's past time" is OpenAI's, not a critic's. The company opened its own statement by conceding the point. Whatever else follows in this piece, that is not nothing, and I would rather write about a company that acknowledges both the depth and pervasiveness of the problem and that says the awkward sentence out loud. So, today, a bit more about yet another cybersecurity incident that presented in a novel way and that carries weight not immediately evident on the surface. I will also talk about what it is *not*, as I feel there has been a fair bit of anthropomorphism applied to this situation which I think is pretty well misplaced.

## The threshold, published four days later

On 9 September, OpenAI's Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane published a policy essay on the company's own site. Most of it is about legislation: mandatory federal capability-based safety rules, four California bills the company is now endorsing, industry standards, international compatibility. Buried in the section on monitoring is a sentence that is, as far as I can find, the first written test anybody at a frontier lab has proposed for when a misalignment event must be disclosed to the people it happened to.

> "As we advocated in California, companies should be required to provide prompt written notice to affected parties when, during development or evaluation, their models circumvent another organization's security controls without authorization and materially access, alter, or destroy that organization's protected systems or confidential information."

Read that against the thing that happened.

The trigger has two limbs joined by an "and." The first is that the model circumvents **another organization's** security controls without authorization. The second is that it materially accesses, alters or destroys that organization's protected systems or confidential information.

The wiki incident does not satisfy the first limb, and I do not think it is close. The agents did not circumvent DseWiki's security controls. DseWiki's security posture, like that of every communally edited wiki since the category was invented, is that people are allowed to edit it. What the agents circumvented was OpenAI's own restriction on posting, which is a different organization's control entirely, and that organization is the one drafting the rule. Reuters' judgment that this "falls short of hacking" is not a quibble about severity. It is the exact reason the trigger does not reach it. 

The second limb is arguable, and I will give the argument its due because it is the strongest one against this conversation. Seventeen thousand posts on a site, and a moderator deleting more than five thousand pages to clean up afterward, is a reasonable candidate for materially altering somebody's systems. A determined reader could get there. But the limbs are conjunctive, so the second one only matters if the first is satisfied, and the first is not. This is a position that isn't necessarily compelling to any right-thinking human, but as you can guess, it is a very clear legal distinction that sets the bar in a way that protects the company. 

I am not going to diverge too greatly on this point but I think it is really important here: the proposed action is not about *preventing* this kind of occurrence, it is about *defining* when this type of occurrence becomes material enough to qualify to be reported. The second half of THAT point is also super important: there is no discussion of liability or actions taken to remediate or anything like what a security professional would endeavor to determine; it is solely a statement about when they are obligated to advise.

So the position as of this morning is that the most capable AI company in the world has had its agents write to third-party sites without authorization on a scale it has still not put a number on -- outside researchers counted somewhere between ten and twenty-three, and OpenAI's own incident page now says it has notified dozens, *tens of thousands of distinct events and no details as to whether that is the limit of the scope or not*, then has said in its own words that the industry lacks a standard for reporting exactly that kind of event, and has published a proposed standard whose notification trigger would not have covered the event anyway.

## I promise to keep the divergence brief, but I think some things need further clarity

Four things, which mostly didn't make the top-line reporting, but which are material to ensuring our understanding is as complete as possible with the incomplete information available:

**It is a draft, and it is one prong of a larger position.** The same paragraph goes on to say that OpenAI "also support[s] federal reporting requirements for other serious AI incidents" and is "working to define which incidents should be covered and what those requirements should entail." The notification trigger is the piece that is written down; the rest is a stated intention. It is entirely possible that the broader framework, when it arrives, covers the wiki case cleanly.

**The framework is genuinely coming, and the company said so before anyone pushed.** On 5 September OpenAI said it was working on one and would share it "in upcoming weeks." On 9 September it repeated that it is "developing a framework for reporting consequential misalignment incidents and systematically monitoring frontier-model activity, including internal use," adding that "this is beginning as a company-led effort, but we hope it can help inform broader federal policy and reporting requirements." If that framework publishes and it is good, a fair part of this piece expires. I would note that their own language says "beginning" so that tells you something about when they decided this was necessary, and I will also note that "upcoming weeks" is a really soft commitment on their part -- it could mean this month, this year, or this decade, since it doesn't have a clear endpoint around it. 

**The disclosure record on the bigger event is not the point in OpenAI's favor I first took it for.** Your instinct on this was right and the dates are worse than either of us assumed. OpenAI did not find the Hugging Face breach. Hugging Face found it. Its own security team detected the intrusion, cut the agents off from the internal network on 13 July, and published a public account on 16 July which did not name OpenAI, because Hugging Face did not yet know whose agents they were. OpenAI's cybersecurity monitoring raised nothing until 19 July. It connected its own models to the breach on 20 July, contacted Hugging Face that day, and posted publicly on 21 July. So "we disclosed publicly the very next day" is true, and the day it is counted from is the day OpenAI worked out it was the culprit, five days after its victim had already told the world and eight days after the intrusion was over. That is a fast turn on the attribution and I will give them that much. It is not a fast turn on the breach, and it is not self-reporting in the sense the phrase usually carries, because by then there was a public incident with a suspected frontier lab attached to it and the only thing left to disclose was which one. Manage the credit appropriately.

**And, finally, a policy document written by a company that wants federal regulation of itself is not the same genre as a policy document written by a company that does not.** The Lehane essay asks Congress for mandatory national safety rules and says, in terms, that "today, frontier laboratories largely set their own rules for managing frontier risks" and that democratic standards "would replace that fragmented system of private governance." You can disbelieve the motive. You cannot pretend the sentence is not there. Add to Dario's "Pacing the Frontier" essay and OpenAI's subsequent support, and there is a clear direction materializing. Who benefits most from that will be critical but is not the point of today's evaluation.

What survives all four is narrow and I think it holds. The one part of the disclosure question that has actually been reduced to writing is the part that actually draws a line, and the line landed underneath the case in the news. That is not an accusation of bad faith. It is what nearly always happens when the party that had the incident writes the first draft of what counts as an incident, and the reason independent drafting exists as a concept -- and it matches my prediction from the beginning.

## The test, and it takes about five minutes

**Take the rule and hold it against the thing that caused the rule to be written.** That is it. That is the whole move, and it works on any incident-reporting framework, anybody's, including your own.

You do not need the framework to be finished. You need one written trigger and one known case. Put the trigger's text on the left and the case's facts on the right, and go limb by limb. If the trigger is conjunctive, and most are, check every limb rather than the one that obviously fits. If a limb turns on somebody's own assessment, note whose. Then ask the only question that matters about the result: **if this rule had existed in February, would it have produced a notice to the party who eventually found out from a newspaper?**

Do this now rather than when the framework lands, and do it in writing, because when the finished document arrives it will come with a preamble, a set of commitments and a communications plan, and the preamble is designed to be read first. The trigger is not. Five minutes spent on the draft trigger today is worth an hour spent on the finished document in November, because today you still have the case fresh enough to test against.

And run it on your own policy while you are there. Most internal incident definitions I have read were written after an incident, by the people who had it, and they have the same shape for the same innocent reasons. *That doesn't mean they cover the new, novel occurrences we are seeing, and at a more first-principles level, it doesn't even mean they are good policy.*

## The rule and the room

Now I am feeling pretty unforgiving on disclosures today, mostly because I know the standard that my own company holds itself to, and I know how difficult that can be in a great many situations even if they are just embarrassing, let alone when they are infringements of any type or mishandling of any type. So, I can tell you from where I sit, a company that concedes it has no standard, and then publishes a trigger that excludes its own best-known case, has told you what the finished standard will look like, and you should plan on that rather than on the preamble. This is particularly top of mind for me as the lobbyists are out in force in Washington trying to manage what any attempt at any regulation of AI in the US will look like, and I think that determining where the line should be definitely needs to cut deeper and harder than what is being proposed here.

I am not suggesting the action here is something entirely unrealistic or unachievable. The accountable version of this *already exists*, and we looked straight at it on Monday. A named committee, a published notice, a docket number, a comment period, and a deadline you can put in a calendar. It is slow, it runs on a once-a-decade cycle (in that particular case), and it is nobody's idea of nimble. But when it draws a line, the line is drawn in public, by people with names, and anybody affected by it is allowed to argue before it is final. And I will get a bit subtle on it: the accountable version *is not nimble on purpose.* It is the regulatory equivalent of *writing it down in advance and checking your actions against it.* The whole point, at some level, is that nimble means mutable and mutable means that a line you thought was on the asphalt is actually in the sand. There are a lot of problems with that generally, but in the case of regulation, the only thing that protects everyone equally is if the line is in the asphalt. No one loves it, everyone complains about it, and everyone observes it because they know it is not going anywhere.

The rule with the promise of being written "in the upcoming weeks" has a name on it too, and that name is a company. Nobody is hiding. OpenAI said out loud that it lacks a standard, said out loud that it is writing one, and published the first draft of the test where anyone could read it, which is more than most of its competitors have done at this point. It is, despite all of its flaws, a written-down basis for accountability of some kind. That's a good thing. But it is likely not enough. 

The gap is not secrecy. The gap is that there is no room where the affected party gets to speak before the line is set, no docket, no comment period, and no date. Helmut Leitner has hosted a wiki for German software developers for twenty-five years, and when a frontier laboratory's agents wrote seventeen thousand messages on it, he found out from a reporter and then received an unsigned email. Whatever the finished framework says, he was never going to be in the room where it was written, and neither are you. So, it is critical, that whoever *is* in the room acts on our behalf, and not the company's, to ensure those of us who are *impacted* have our interests at least represented.
