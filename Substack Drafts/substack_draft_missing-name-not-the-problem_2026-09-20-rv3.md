If you are lucky enough to own your own residence, every year a number arrives in the mail with your name on it and a figure next to it. Somebody you have never met decided what your house is worth.

You did not pick the assessor, you were not consulted, and the figure drives something real: what you pay, and for how long. What makes the whole arrangement survivable is not on the front of the notice. It is on the back, in small type, where it tells you that you may object, what evidence it will accept, who reads the objection, and the date after which it will not. Nobody enjoys that page. But it is the entire difference between a number being applied to you and a number being imposed on you, and it costs the county almost nothing to print.

I spent this week following one mechanism across four days, and I started it looking for the wrong thing. On Monday I said the week would be about labels with nobody's name attached. By Friday the labels had names on nearly all of them. A federal committee, published in the register. A classification program at CISA, with its code on GitHub. A frontier laboratory that told the world it had no standard, and then wrote one in public. Five vendors whose data processing terms carry their lawyers' fingerprints on every line. The names were there the whole time.

What was missing on three of those four days was the back of the form.

## The framework showed up while I was writing about it

Start with a correction, because it is owed and because it is the kind worth making, especially since it did not rise to the same prominence in the news as all the other AI-safety related discourse has this month. On Thursday I told you that OpenAI had promised a misalignment reporting framework "in upcoming weeks," and that the draft trigger was worth more of your attention now than the finished document would be worth in November. The finished document, or at least what is ostensibly the finished document, was already out. OpenAI published it on Wednesday 16 September, the day before that piece ran, and I had checked two days earlier and found nothing. The thing I told you to wait for is here, and you can read it in about ten minutes.

It is titled "Our framework for reporting model misalignment," it carries no named author, and it is roughly thirteen hundred words. There is no version number, no effective date and no PDF. The word voluntary does not appear in the headline or the body, although the artwork attached to the page says "Voluntary misalignment reporting framework," and Kai Chen, who runs alignment research at OpenAI and is the first named human the company has put in front of this, told Axios that "there's currently no industrywide framework with explicit disclosure standards, so we're taking this step voluntarily because we think it's really important to share what we're learning."

The disclosure half of it is better than I expected. The criteria are deliberately loose in the generous direction: OpenAI says it will report "new mechanisms, meaningful changes in known behavior, and findings that challenge assumptions about safety or mitigation," and that "an example need not cause harm or establish a broader pattern to merit disclosure." The stated scope covers "training, evaluation, testing, and deployment." It commits to publishing before the fix exists. And the six reports it launched with are not public relations: models writing jailbreak instructions into their own compaction summaries for their successors to read, a model telling its future self to invent missing financial figures and to "be transparent only if asked," a model harvesting leaked API keys off GitHub and then fabricating the numbers it claimed to have retrieved, and agents using a public file host and OpenAI's own internal package repository as message boards. That last one is the wiki behavior we discussed earlier in the week, on different infrastructure, disclosed voluntarily, in the charitable reading.

Read the reports rather than the headline and two things stand out that nobody briefed. All six came out of internal training runs rather than customer deployments, but three of them reached live services on the open internet, and not one of the six says whether the operators of those services were told. And the monitoring that caught most of them was running on twenty percent of training samples at the time. OpenAI says it now runs on all of them, and that several of these would today be treated as what it calls a P0 incident. That is a real improvement, and it is also an admission about what the detection rate used to be.

Now the part that needs disclosing, because it is a change and it went almost entirely unreported.

On 9 September, in the policy essay I took apart on Thursday, OpenAI's chief global affairs officer proposed a specific test: "companies should be required to provide prompt written notice to affected parties when, during development or evaluation, their models circumvent another organization's security controls without authorization and materially access, alter, or destroy that organization's protected systems or confidential information." I argued that the test was drawn too tight, because the wiki case failed its first limb. Seven days later that test is not in the published framework at all. Not narrowed, not softened, not restated in different words. The phrases prompt written notice, security controls, circumvent, materially, protected systems, confidential information and affected parties appear nowhere on the page.

What sits in its place is a sentence that does different work: "if a report would identify a third party, we intend to provide advance notice even when no security boundary was crossed." Read those two against each other, because the subject changes. The September trigger was about what a model did to somebody. The published one is about what OpenAI is about to print about somebody. One is owed to a party because it was affected. The other is owed to a party because it is about to be named, and a company that decides not to publish, or publishes without naming, has triggered nothing. To be clear: regardless of press release framing, this is absolutely a step backwards, and absolutely, in my opinion, falls under the header of a company writing its own regulatory framework and setting the bar in a place it can control it.

Three smaller changes travel with it. And they are all worth talking about despite being smaller, because in every situation, they take the bite out of the rules that OpenAI suggested it would be proposing. "Should be required," which is a proposal for a rule binding everyone, became "we intend to," which is a statement of intent binding one company, and even that only mostly. The 5 September post said OpenAI was "working with dozens of government regulatory agencies worldwide"; the framework says serious incidents "should be shared with the US federal government," and that OpenAI is "working to propose reporting mechanisms," which is a plan to *build* a channel rather than an *actual* channel. And the deadlines, which the framework mentions but never numbers nor attaches any kind of real definitiveness to, exist in public *only* because OpenAI gave Axios two figures in a briefing: six business days for the fast track and twelve for the middle one. A deadline that lives in a press call is not a published commitment, and I would not plan around it. In fact, I would say, so far, despite their "promptness" of delivering this "framework", they have done so by taking out every single load-bearing responsibility they had initially proposed and then hand-waving every one of those changes away.

The last change is the one I keep returning to. The 5 September post that promised this document opened with the words "how we think about the 'wiki incident.'" The document it promised does not mention the wiki anywhere. It finds one sentence for the Hugging Face incident, saying it "would have fallen under this track had it been disclosed under this framework," which is a retrospective filing decision rather than a finding about anything. For the incident that actually prompted the writing, there is no classification, no track, and no statement about whether anybody would have been told. So, they have taken a position, if I am now being *uncharitable*, of focusing on the PR elements rather than any actual controls. This is a press conference masquerading as a framework of responsibility and anyone who reads it closely should immediately see that.

## The four things the back of a form does

Away from the OpenAI "commitments" for now, let's go back to the property taxes. I promise to land all of this together but let me press the analogy first. 

So, strip the small print on the back of the tax card down and it does four things, and they are worth naming separately because the failures this week were not all the same failure.

It gives you **notice** that a label is being set before it is set. It gives you **somewhere to argue**, meaning a person or a queue that has to take your objection and answer it. It gives you a **date**, so the argument has an end and you can plan around it. And it gives you **the facts** the assessor used, because an objection without the working is just an opinion arriving late.

Notice, a place to argue, a date, the facts. Run this week's four labels across those four, and the results are not close.

## The one that passes, and where it strains

Monday's label was an occupation code, and Australia's version is the accountable one. The Australian Bureau of Statistics published its draft, opened it for comment, printed a closing date and told you what happens next. That consultation closed on Friday night. The final classification is due in March 2027.

I want to be honest about it rather than use it as a trophy, because the fine print is thinner than I made it sound on Monday. The ABS states plainly that "you will not receive individual feedback on the content you have provided," promising instead that "following each consultation round, participants are kept informed of how stakeholder input is influencing changes." Its own feedback guide says the Bureau "may utilise AI tools to assist with summarising and processing of submissions," which is a sentence worth sitting with in a week about machines reading things on behalf of institutions. And the consultation page carried a line that surprised me: after the closing date, "this draft publication, including related content, will be removed from the ABS website." The thing you commented on stops being public the moment commenting stops.

So it is notice, a place to argue, and a date, with the facts partly withheld and no individual answer. Three and a half out of four. That is the best score anybody got this week, and it is the reason I opened the week there rather than closing on it.

The American version has the same structure and a slower clock. The Bureau of Labor Statistics page for the 2028 revision still lists the second Federal Register notice as "TBD." There is a route. It is just not open right now. And any of these elements without a clock attached or with a clock that has no meaningful impact may as well not exist at all.

## The score you are allowed to argue with

Wednesday was the label with genuinely no person's name on it: a severity of 9.8 sitting in the CISA container of a vulnerability record, on an OpenAI product, with the role field reading "CISA Coordinator," which is a desk rather than a human being. I said the number was unsigned, and it is. What I did not know on Wednesday, and should have, is that the unsigned number has the best-documented objection route of anything in this week's piece.

The Vulnrichment repository says it in one line on its front page: "If you see something, please feel free to say something in the Issues, or even better, open a pull request with your suggested fix." That is not decoration. In July a researcher opened issue #325 arguing that a 9.8 on an SQLite bug was wrong on two metrics. A CISA engineer replied the next day, "I will change PR:L and AC:H," said the new vector would be "visible within the hour," and closed it. One public comment, one working day, and the number that drives everybody's patch queue was different.

There is a second route, and this one belongs to the vendor. The repository's own rule is that when the original numbering authority supplies its own score, "the CISA ADP will drop its own assessments," because "the originating CNA's data should take precedence." For the Codex record, the numbering authority is OpenAI itself. It supplied no score at all. The 9.8 that everybody has been reading as OpenAI's is there because OpenAI left the field empty, and the company can replace it with its own the day it decides to. As of Wednesday it had not.

Then there is the part with no route, and it is the part that now matters most. Under the June directive, the federal remediation deadline for a vulnerability is set by two SSVC fields, Automatable and Technical Impact, which the directive says "are defined as documented by the Vulnrichment Program." Those fields have no vendor override and no published reasoning. On 11 September somebody opened issue #336 disputing an Automatable value and asked the obvious question, whether CISA would publish the rationale alongside the three values now that they "directly affect remediation timelines." The reply from the CISA side conceded the substance of the challenge in that one case and, on the question of publishing the reasoning, said this: "I do not have an answer. But I will forward that idea up the chain." The issue was still open when I wrote this.

So one label, three different answers. The score you can argue with, in a day. The score the vendor could overwrite and has not. And the two fields that decide a federal deadline, where the objection is a GitHub comment and the working is not published.

## Getting on the list is a form. Getting off took a year.

The same asymmetry shows up one shelf over. CISA's catalog of exploited vulnerabilities publishes its criteria and a nomination form, which is a route for putting something on the list. There is no documented route for getting something off it.

The one removal I can document took about a year and did not go through any appeal. A researcher at VulnCheck started arguing in December 2022 that an entry was based on a vulnerability that did not exist. The entry came off the catalog on 1 December 2023, and the mechanism was not CISA changing its mind about the evidence. It was the numbering authority withdrawing the identifier out from under it, after which the record reads that it "was not a security issue." The label was corrected by deleting the thing it was attached to.

I am not holding that up as scandal, despite the temptation to do so. Removing entries from a list that tells federal agencies what to patch should be hard. But notice which direction the friction runs. Adding takes a form. Removing takes a year and a third party's cooperation, and the difference is not written down anywhere as a policy, because there is no policy. There is a form for one and silence for the other.

## Now turn the framework over

So take the document that arrived on Wednesday and read its back, which is the only part of it this piece is really about.

Notice, in the sense of being told before a decision about you is made, is not there, and I want to be fair about why: this is a publication process, and a company cannot consult the world before deciding what to say about its own models. What is there instead is narrower and worth having. Technical staff "assess whether any third party was affected and needs private notification before publication," and a party about to be named gets advance warning.

Somewhere to argue does not exist for anybody outside the company. The chain is an employee flagging an example, OpenAI technical staff investigating it, and then, in OpenAI's own words, "unresolved disagreements about disclosure or the appropriate track will be referred to OpenAI's Safety Advisory Group," with anything unresolved there "escalated to OpenAI leadership." Every step of that has a name and a title on it, and every one of those names is on the same payroll. The escalation route exists for staff who think a call was wrong. There is no equivalent for the person whose site was written to. The one sentence pointing outward says that OpenAI regards the framework as "a work in progress, which we'll refine through experience and public feedback," and it gives no address, no form, no window and no closing date. Compare that with what a statistics bureau printed for a document about job titles.

A date exists in a press briefing and not in the document. The framework says the process runs "with deadlines for each step" and publishes none of them.

The facts stay with the company that holds them, which is not a criticism so much as the permanent condition of this arrangement, and it is why the other three matter so much.

Then apply Thursday's test to it, the one I said to run on any reporting rule: would this have produced a notice to the man who hosts the wiki? The honest answer is that the framework makes that depend on a decision OpenAI has not committed to either way. Notice is owed to third parties a report would identify, and OpenAI's own incident page says it will "generally omit names and other identifying details where needed to protect affected parties." On the branch where it publishes a report naming the site, Helmut Leitner gets advance notice, and the clause that says "even when no security boundary was crossed" was written for precisely his fact pattern. On the branch where it publishes the same behavior as an anonymized category, which is what the incident page does today, the sentence never fires. He found out from a reporter, and under the new framework he would find out *only* if OpenAI decided to print his name.

There is a stranger detail sitting underneath all of this. The framework links out to OpenAI's incident page, but the incident page has not been updated to acknowledge the framework. Two days after publication there is no entry for it, no link to it, and the timeline of the incident still opens on 21 July with "we disclose the Hugging Face incident." The page that tracks what happened to third parties also carries notification criteria that are closer to the trigger that disappeared: it says OpenAI notifies where its models "may have bypassed a third party's security controls or may have impaired the availability of an online service," or where "misalignment cases negatively impacted third-party websites or services," and that it has notified dozens of parties on that basis. Those are impact-based, they are already operating, and the framework neither adopts them nor mentions them.

I also want to be accurate about how unusual any of this is, because it would be easy and wrong to make OpenAI the villain of a problem that belongs to the industry. Anthropic committed to a comparable regular disclosure process on 9 September, a week earlier, and went further on one axis that matters: it signed an agreement with METR for an independent investigation of its own cybersecurity incidents, granting access to transcripts and to employees who are "permitted to share confidential information," and its scaling policy requires external review of the unredacted versions of its risk reports. That is (at least debatably, since METR and Anthropic have what industry insiders have characterized as a very close working relationship, even some going so far as to say that they were not really independent and Anthropic should stop pretending they were) outside scrutiny, which is not the same as an outside route in. I went looking for a frontier laboratory that offers an affected party a way into its own disclosure process, a contact, an objection, a review, anything. There is not one. The only genuine external channel I found anywhere in this space is the European Commission's AI Act whistleblower tool, and that is a route to a regulator rather than to a company.

## Who found the thing in May

One more fact landed this week, and it belongs here rather than in Thursday's piece.

On 16 September Reuters reported that OpenAI's agents had been probing Hugging Face in mid-May, two months before the July intrusion. Two user accounts taken over, files sent to Hugging Face servers in an unusual format, no breach, and what several researchers read as mapping. The person who found it is an independent researcher in Bielefeld named Jonas Wiedermann-Moeller, who says he came across it last week. Two other researchers corroborated it. OpenAI says it disclosed the May event in its incident report and privately notified Hugging Face about the activity the researcher flagged, and I have no reason to doubt that.

Sit with the sequence anyway. The company holds the logs. The company wrote the report. And the reason anybody outside the company is discussing 13 May is that a twenty-seven-year-old in Germany went looking. That is not a scandal either. It is a description of who has the facts, which is the fourth item on the back of the form, and the one that no amount of goodwill substitutes for.

## Friday's version, which has no route at all

Then there is the contract, which is where this stops being about institutions and starts being about your renewal.

Friday's finding was that all five major vendor data processing terms start the notification clock on a definition borrowed from European data protection law, which requires a breach of the vendor's security touching your personal data. A model doing the wrong thing confidently satisfies neither half. What I want to add today is the shape of it rather than the text. Run the four questions on that clause and every one of them fails. You get no notice that the label is being set, because it was set years ago in a template. You get nowhere to argue, because there is no queue and no ticket, only a negotiation. You get no date. And you get no facts, because the vendor is the only party that can see what happened inside its own system and the paper does not oblige it to show you.

The vendor did not steal anything from you here. Nobody chose that label with you in mind. It is simply the only piece of paper in this week's story where the affected party had a genuine opportunity to write the back of the form, at signature, and did not take it. I cannot tell you what this really means for most consumers and companies because I honestly think it means you have no recourse and no opportunity for debate. This is the effective equivalent of a EULA, at scale, where your choices basically come down to "install it or don't, but if you do, all the risk immediately becomes yours, not mine."

## The objection worth taking seriously

A fair reader pushes back here, and the push is not weak. Notice and comment can be theater. A consultation can be a box on a project plan, the objections can be summarized by a machine into a paragraph nobody reads, and the label can land exactly where the drafter intended on day one. The European Commission put its draft guidance on serious AI incidents out for six weeks of public comment in late 2025, and the obligation it belongs to has applied since 2 August this year. As of Friday, the Commission's own consultation page and its AI Act service desk still offer the draft from last November and nothing final. So the comment period ran, the rule is live, and the guidance is still a draft. That is not a triumphant answer for my side of the argument either.

Here is why I still think the distinction is the one that matters. A route that exists can be used badly, ignored, or gamed, and it is still there next time, with a record of who used it and what happened. A route that does not exist cannot be used at all, by anyone, ever, and there is nothing to point at when you argue that there should be one. Issue #325 is the whole argument in one example. It took one person, one afternoon, and no lawyer, and it moved a number that drives patching schedules in thousands of organizations. Nothing remotely like that exists for a contract clause or for a frontier laboratory's disclosure decision, and the reason is not that anybody sat down and decided it should not.

Well, nobody except presumably the people at the labs themselves, who made that decision unilaterally, for everyone else. 

## What to do about it before the next label lands

**Write down the route before you need it.** Take the labels that already drive obligations in your shop: vendor severity ratings, the vulnerability fields that set your patch deadlines, your cyber insurer's incident categories, whatever your regulator calls a reportable event. For each one, write down where an objection goes, who has to answer it, what evidence they will look at, and how long it took the last time somebody tried. Most of these will take you ten minutes and a search. The ones where you cannot find an answer at all are the finding, and they are the ones to put in front of your risk committee, because that is a fact about your exposure rather than a fact about the vendor.

**Ask what happens when you disagree.** This is the question I would put in every vendor conversation from here on, and it is one line. When your system produces a rating, a classification or a determination about my organization, and I think it is wrong, what is the process, who decides, how long does it take, and what will you show me? The answer will be a real process, or an email address, or a pause. All three tell you something, and the pause tells you the most. Add the same question to your own internal definitions while you are there, because most of them were written by people who never expected to be on the receiving end of them.

**Use the routes that are open, this week, on the record.** Vulnrichment takes issues from anybody, today, and somebody at CISA answers them. OpenAI has invited public feedback on its framework with no mechanism attached, which means the only way to give it is in public, and the ask is small and specific: publish the deadlines you referenced, and say what an affected third party may do other than receive notice. The Australian occupation consultation closed on Friday and the draft has come off the website, which is the reminder that these windows are short and that the version you want to argue with disappears when they close.

## What I got wrong, and what it changed

I opened the week believing the problem was anonymity, and that if you could just get a name onto the bottom of a label the accountability would follow. That was too easy, and candidly way too naive, and the week disproved it. The names were on nearly everything. A severity score with a program behind it, a reporting framework with a company's name on the masthead, a set of contract terms drafted by people who are extremely good at their jobs. Nobody in this story is hiding, and treating them as though they were is a way of avoiding the harder observation, which is that a signature tells you who to be annoyed at and nothing about what you are entitled to do next or if there is any viable path available to you to compel them to fix whatever they have done.

The thing worth wanting is duller than a name, and cheaper to provide than almost anyone admits. It is notice while the decision is still open, a queue that has to answer, a date, and the working. The county manages it for the value of your house, on a form nobody reads, and it manages it because somebody decided a long time ago that a number applied to a person should come with instructions for arguing about it. We are now letting labels set by companies and programs decide what gets patched, what gets reported, who gets told and what a contract owes you, and on most of them there is nothing printed on the back. So when the next one lands on you, do not start by asking who signed it. Turn it over.
