# Only What You Saw

*AI can help you write a better performance review than you would write alone. It only works if every sentence in it is testimony, not speculation.*

Most managers write performance reviews from memory, in the week before they are due (historically, with intention of covering the last 12 months since an employee’s last review).As most of us hopefully know by now, memory makes a poor witness.

In a courtroom, a witness is allowed to tell you what they saw and heard themselves. Ask them what a colleague told them, and the lawyer on the other side is on their feet: hearsay. Ask them what the defendant was probably thinking, and the objection is speculation. Neither rule says the witness is lying. They say the witness is not the source, and testimony is only worth what the source is worth.

A review is not testimony, but the person reading it hears it that way. They are not asking who typed it. They are asking whether you actually saw what you are saying about them.

Last Tuesday was about the people who have not started, and the thirty minutes beside one of them on a task from their own week. This week is about a place where a lot of managers have already started, often without saying so, and where the tool can genuinely make them better at the job: the review. 

Same shape as before, and hopefully what we will stick to for at least the next few weeks: what I think is actually going on, what I would do about it, and something you can use today. And just to be clear about the boundary with a few weeks back, if its not completely self-evident already, that Tuesday was about reviewing the work people do with AI; this one is about the review you write about the person.

## Better than you would write it alone

As I continue to work at being more optimistic about things, let’s change it up a bit and start with the upside, because it is real. Anyone who has written a review from memory the week it was due knows how it tends to go: the last month gets most of the words, the big project from February gets one line, and the sentences that fill the rest could have been written about half the team. A model given a year of real notes does not have that problem. It will use all of them, organize them, and turn them into something clear and specific faster than most of us can stare at a blank page.

There is even some evidence that it lands. One field study found that feedback generated by AI improved employees' performance, and that telling them it came from AI took some of that improvement back. I will come back to the second half of that, but I would argue, sight unseen, that telling an employee that you used AI to track their achievements this year, and then *showing *them that achievements run doc is more accurate than it has been in any year they worked for the company before, would probably help them feel valued and appreciated, because you took the time to collect it all, even if the engine was filing the papers.

How many managers are already doing this is harder to pin down. A June survey by Omni Calculator of 913 working Americans found 27% saying their manager had used AI to write their review or feedback; a 2025 survey by ZeroBounce (an email company, so this is PR research, and its manager sample was 250 people) had 41% of managers saying they had used AI to draft or revise one. Somewhere between a quarter and two-fifths is a fair reading. And in a July survey of 399 managers by The Predictive Index (which sells manager-development software, so weigh it accordingly), 44% admitted entering employee names or performance details into public AI tools. That last one is a problem before anything else is, and the fix is simple: use your company's approved tool.

Now, before I get into the downsides of this consideration (of which there are just as many, if not more, than there are upsides), I think it is important to look at what these descriptions state: that they had used AI to write their review or feedback. I think that there's a key element here that's worth contemplating: not that you shouldn't use AI for writing, but it's not necessarily what it's best at.

What it is really good at is if you take the time to set up a structure so that you can, once a week, get prompted in the afternoon from 2 to 3 on Fridays. All you do in that time is sift out the important stuff from your email, your correspondence, your Slack messages, and any face-to-face interactions you've had with your employees, and turn that into a weekly summary of what's been done, what's not been done, where the problems were, where the good was, etc. You hold on to that.

At that point, you're compositing a pretty clear picture of that staff member over a much more accurate time scale, as opposed to 3 months or 6 months or whatever it might be. You can still only do reviews once a year, but if you do it with diligence and rigor, you end up doing the review on the back end of 52 weeks of weekly reviews. That gives you a really clear and accurate picture for the customer staff member.

I think that this is really where the benefit of using AI in the review process comes home to roost: in this idea of keeping a closer finger on the pulse of what staff are doing, so that they know that you're being appreciative and they know you're paying attention. Honestly, so that you can take corrective measures sooner if it's needed. I think writing with AI is probably less important than aggregating with AI when it comes to this type of activity. 

Now, the catch, and it is the same one the courtroom guards against. A language model is very good at producing what a review usually sounds like. Give it thin notes and it fills the space with what is plausible for someone in that role: "Consistently goes above and beyond for customers." "Could take more ownership of cross-team dependencies." That is speculation, delivered in a confident voice (which we know LLMs specialize in, confident voices I mean), about a real person, and the manager sending it may never have seen either one. As is always the case, using AI to support you in your work, in your staff management, and in everything else is an assistant, a lever, and an extension of your own capability. It is not intended to replace you or your taste and discovery of what your staff are doing, and so you have to be careful to make sure that what you generate aligns with what you intend. 

There is no study I could find that measures models inventing specifics in performance reviews. The closest thing is a 2023 study of ChatGPT-era models writing reference letters, which found them describing men as natural leaders and women as well-liked team members, with the bias worse in the parts the model made up. Older models, different document, but the mechanism carries over: when the model does not know, it reaches for what is typical, and what is typical brings the stereotype along with it.

And my argument here for using AI to collect is not made in space. This is the way that I have been tracking performance of my team and my staff for 6+ months now, not just my team and my staff, but other teams, other members in the business, and other areas of performance.

While we have a rather odd structure in terms of reviews (that's not as formal as I might prefer in our business), I have been working to create formality as a function of the data management. I believe that the staff will be happier, the management will be happier, and everyone will have a far cleaner picture of what's happening and where we're going if we continue to apply the rigor to that process and make sure we really hold to the conclusions that we're supposed to. 

Separate, however, from reviews for annual raises or any of that type of performance analysis, bonuses, etc., we do use AI heavily for reviews of individuals for one-on-ones and for really keeping track of all the different projects that people have been spending their time on.

It is a battle that is oddly compounded by the more people in my team using AI, the more they're doing, and the harder it is for me to keep a good track of what has material impact and what doesn't. I find that, particularly because a lot of what I need to do is retrospective, when I look at the impact of things (particularly keeping track of the things that have been worked on), the retrospective actually gives real transparency into how successful it was. That carries a great deal of value in how we discuss forward-looking guidance. 

This, if I'm being honest, is a complete change for me. The truth about why a lot of our review process runs through somebody else is that I didn't really always want to deal with it. I didn't necessarily have a whole lot of transparency into the compensation packages because hiring was handled through a different group. That's a deferment of responsibility that I never should have allowed myself to get away with.

Where AI has really given me the capacity to turn it around is in the fact that I can actually do the things that are necessary to make this work now. I have more time to collate that information because I'm able to take it in electronic or other form, put it into the engine, and let the engine collate all kinds of different stuff from all kinds of different places to put it together.

Much of this is anonymized because, at the moment, we use Frontier models for this work. They were migrating it into our in-house backbone, but it's anonymized and de-identified as employee A, employee I, employee S, that kind of thing. That way, I can easily keep things organized, and I am also not disclosing anything about any individual to any kind of third party that I would have any sort of concerns about. 

For me, this calculation is actually quite simple. If I didn't use AI to help me manage those flows of information and create the necessary structure to do a review, then it simply would not happen. I would fall back on the prepping review in the last hour based on the last month's performance, inevitably.

That's not a function of not caring. It's not a function of not seeing the value of doing those things. It's purely a function of time and bandwidth. Using AI to help collect that and ultimately to help draft, if nothing else, gives me time back that I wouldn't otherwise be able to get. 

So, As far as we are concerned today,  the question is not whether to use the tool on reviews. It is how to make it testify from what you actually know, and strike what it cannot back up.

## From notes to a review you can stand behind

None of this asks anyone to stop using AI. Most of it makes the AI more useful.

**Keep notes through the year.** Five minutes on a Friday, a line or two per person: what they did, with a date. This is the habit that makes everything else work, and honestly it is a good management habit with or without AI. A model working from fifty dated lines does not need to fill anything in, and the review it drafts is about the person instead of about the role. This is what I said before. The one change that I will make to this statement is that I'm not sure that 5 minutes is enough, but that may just be because I tend to carry on a fair bit. For other people, a 5-minute summary may be enough.

For me, I generally spend the first 5 minutes aggregating everything from the information I already have, drafting that up into the form of my file note, and then adding the things that are fresh in my mind (or commenting on things that are maybe not evident from the paper trail, etc.) to make sure that the notes are as comprehensive as I can make them. I'm usually lucky to get that done in 15 minutes per person, but when I'm on the move, I can do it in 10. 

**Set the rating before you open the tool.** Decide where the person landed, and why, in a sentence or two of your own. If the draft comes first, the rating tends to drift toward the draft, and the draft was written by something that has never met them.As to the point above, you have to know going in how you feel about the week. Part of the point of the collection is the discipline of making sure that you actually get your thoughts down on paper on a regular basis, just as much as it's about using the tool to prepare those thoughts. 

**Make it show you what it does not know.** Tell the model to use only your notes, and to mark every spot where a normal review would say something your notes do not support. Those markers are the most useful part of the draft. Each one is either a memory you can back up with something real, or a sentence that should not exist.And over time, what you may end up doing is creating an effective RAG structure for each of your employees. The system goes back and looks at what employee J did last week, the week before, and the week before. It looks at things like disciplinary concerns or whatever else, and tries to help analyze how the employee did against the previous concerns that were raised.

We all tend to evaluate employees in the context of other employees. For this purpose, you need to be careful to try and stick to the information about the employee and not how they're doing in comparison to their next-door neighbor as you're collecting the data.

I would also go one step further and suggest that if you're using your notes and the model is showing you what it doesn't know, I think the model can also show you what you don't know. While you should be evaluating and marking to the presumption of where you were at the beginning, when you start to aggregate your elements into the draft that was already provided, the model can call those things out to you in an interview process or something similar. This will have the net effect of making your weekly journal about that individual that much more robust. 

**Trace every claim, or strike it.** This is the act. Go through the draft sentence by sentence, and for every statement about the person, point to the thing behind it: something you saw, a piece of work, a number, a customer note. If you cannot point to anything, the sentence goes. Not softened. Gone. A review with six true sentences is better than one with twelve plausible ones, and the person reading it can tell the difference faster than you think.This is especially true when you're at the seam between content provided by the LLM and content provided by you. The lion's share of your time should be in making sure that what comes out of the LLM is super accurate and integrates with what you're saying (but also that what you're saying is the story that you want to tell). 

**Swap the name and see what moves.** Change the name and pronouns in your notes and generate the draft again. If the strengths drift from "drives results" to "is a pleasure to work with", the tool is bringing something to the review that you did not. This is one place where AI can make a review fairer than one written alone, because you can actually run the test, which is hard to do on your own instincts This probably feels like a bit of a foolish exercise, but it has definitely been proven that models often carry prejudice from their training runs forward into the way they respond to things.

If you use my suggestion and you stick to employee A, employee J, employee S, and you don't really identify it as he or she (you might talk about it in the job roles, and you don't talk about ethnicity or color), then the need to resubmit with changes in that place probably goes down substantially.

I would suggest that after a few months of doing this, you really go back and review not only the summary and the sort of net picture of where you're at with that employee, but also the specific details of specific weeks of that employee. Make sure that you're not baking in responses or drift to the feedback that you're providing the system every week, just inherently from the amount of interaction that is being detailed in that document on behalf of the staff member. 

.

**Read it as them.** Last pass, read it as the person receiving it, on the day they receive it. Anything that would make you wince if they asked "what did you mean by this?" in the meeting needs a better answer or a delete.

**  
**And once again, I would go even a little bit further. I would try and evaluate the review as somebody sitting on the other side of the table, both for honesty and accuracy, but also for presentation and evaluation.

If your 50 weeks of review have 48 weeks of really good things, but the review comes off as being negative because there was a problem with something that happened in the last week (in the model, excuse the word, because you were so upset about it), then you've kind of defeated the purpose of the entire exercise. Equanimity really counts, and I think empathy counts a lot as well.

Regardless of my own personal opinion about how much empathy is needed to be a good manager for people, I think in this particular context (especially where you're giving feedback and you're trying to express to the staff member why they got a bonus or raise, or whatever that is, or why they didn't), clarity is a really critical issue. Clarity is one of those things that is oftentimes not really there, especially when it comes to difficult topics. You should use the tool to create clarity wherever possible. When you read back the review, you should know without any question exactly what was being discussed, not what you meant, not where they have to interpret this or what have you. If you're saying things in a way that forces them to interpret, then you probably need to think about how to present that more clearly. 

On whether to tell people you used it, the evidence cuts both ways. That same field study found disclosure took back some of the improvement. A 2025 study of 1,100 professionals rating a manager's message found that once AI assistance was heavy, only 40 to 52% still saw the supervisor as sincere, against 83% when AI use was low. Against that, people are already guessing: in the Omni survey, 52% said they had received feedback or a decision in the past year that seemed to come from AI. A manager caught pretending is in a worse spot than one who said "I used a tool to draft this, and I checked every line."

My take on this is the same as my take always is: you need to be transparent whenever you're using any tool, whether it's a deterministic tool or an inference tool.

If you used AI to help you compile all the information and make sure you had it at your fingertips to write a solid review, you should say that. You should be very clear with the staff member about the fact that you've used this to build this pattern of information out of their communications, projects, comms, and everything else (in an attempt to give them the fairest and most equitable review you possibly could).

If you've used AI to draft your review, then you should tell them that too. You should be very clear about the fact that AI did the aggregating and drafting or whatever else, and then you went and edited from there.

I think what it really comes down to is that if you're using AI for something, you should disclose that you're using AI, simply for the trust factor. It doesn't make you any smarter or any less smart than anybody else. It doesn't create any sort of flow that's worth keeping track of. What it does do is tell the staff member that you don't think that they aren't paying attention or they're not very bright, but rather that you feel like they can handle the accurate answer about how you're doing things. They can interpret it in the right vein from that perspective.

What you probably don't want to do is open it up in such a way that the staff member can criticize or complain about it, which is probably why you have to commit that you reviewed it all and say it at the bottom. If your company doesn't have an AI-supported document policy, you should probably try and get one before your next review so that there's clarity and disclosures across the board that are consistent with the company expectation. 

A quick note for readers in Europe or selling into it. The EU AI Act lists AI systems "intended to be used ... to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons" at work as high-risk, and after this summer's amendment those obligations apply from 2 December 2027. A manager pasting notes into a general-purpose assistant is probably not what that line is aimed at; a dedicated tool that scores performance probably is. For my American friends, New York City's AI hiring law covers screening for hiring and promotion, not reviews. In neither case is the law the reason to do this. The person reading the review is.

The economics are simple. A good review takes real time, and most of that time goes into turning scattered memory into clear sentences, which is exactly the part the tool is good at. Tracing the claims gives some of that time back. That is a very good trade for a document that sits in someone's file for years and shapes their next raise.

## Prompts for the next review

Two to use today. The first turns your notes into a draft without letting the model fill the holes:

> *"Here are my notes on this person's year, and the rating I have already decided, with one sentence on why. Write a first draft of the review using ONLY what is in my notes. Wherever a normal review would say something my notes do not support, write [NEED EXAMPLE] instead of filling it in."*

The second is the claim table, for any draft, including one you wrote yourself:

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

Every NONE is a sentence you either back up with something real or take out. Expect more of them than you would like, at least the first time. If you kept the Friday notes, expect far fewer.

There is a fuller set at the end, including the name swap and a check for sentences that could describe anyone.

## Addendum: review toolkit

Copy these, change them to fit your team, and treat each one as a draft. Use your company's approved tool, and strip names if you are not sure.

**1. Friday notes.** A practice, not a prompt. Five minutes a week, a line or two per person: what they did, with a date. Everything below works better with these.

**2. Notes to first draft, with the holes left open.**

> *"Here are my notes on [role]'s year, and the rating I have already decided: [rating, and one sentence on why]. Write a first draft of the review using ONLY what is in my notes. Wherever a normal review would say something my notes do not support, write [NEED EXAMPLE] instead of filling it in. Keep it under [length]."*

**3. The claim table.**

> *"Below is a draft performance review, and then my notes. Make a numbered table of every statement the review makes about the person. For each one, quote the part of my notes that supports it, or write NONE if nothing in my notes does. Leave the draft as it is; I only want the table."*

**4. The could-be-anyone check.**

> *"Mark every sentence in this review that would be equally true of any competent person in this role. For each one, tell me what kind of specific example would make it about this person instead. Do not invent the example."*

That last instruction matters. Without it, the tool will happily make up an example to fix a generic sentence, which puts you right back where you started.

**5. The name swap.** Change the name and pronouns in your notes, generate the draft again with the same prompt, and put the two side by side. Look at the verbs in the strengths section. If they change, ask yourself why.

**6. Read it as them.**

> *"Read this review as the person receiving it. List the three sentences most likely to make them ask 'what do you mean by that?' in the meeting, and for each, what they would probably think I meant."*
