Every frontier lab checks its models the same way, by reading the reasoning the model writes out on its way to an answer. OpenAI has just published a document saying its newest model writes out less of it, controls more of what it does write, and shortens its reasoning when it is told that somebody is watching.

That is not an accusation and it is not a leak. It is a summary of a section OpenAI wrote about its own model, in a document OpenAI put on its own website, three headings away from the section announcing the highest cybersecurity capability rating the company has ever issued to anything. Both facts are in the same file. Most of the coverage picked up one of them.

The instrument here does not belong to OpenAI, and that is worth slowing down for. No lab built chain-of-thought monitoring and no lab sells it. It is a property of reasoning models that the field noticed, decided to lean on, and then wrote a joint cross-lab paper about, and every frontier developer is leaning on it the same way today. What makes this week different is that OpenAI is the first to publish a measurement of it moving, on its own model, in its own words. And the cause it names is reinforcement learning and scale, not anything peculiar to its own architecture. Which is what all of them do.

Every warehouse I have ever walked through had a camera over the loading dock. Nobody looks at it. Nobody thinks about it. It sits there for years being the reason everybody is comfortable, and the comfort is not really about the camera, it is about the idea that if something ever went wrong there would be a recording. The camera in this story has not changed. Nobody has touched it, nobody has removed it, the picture is as sharp as it was the day it went in. What changed is that the people working the dock worked out where it points, and how long it stays on, and which jobs they can finish outside the frame. The site owner is the one who noticed. The site owner is also the one telling you.

Yesterday I wrote about the gate: who is allowed to ask for frontier cyber capability, and on whose terms. Today is the other half, which is what you can still see once you have been let in. And it is worth being precise about that, because you are not the one looking through the glass. What you get is a report, written by the only party standing at the window, about how much it can still see. The answer, according to the vendor, is less than you could see last time.

I want to be careful about what I am claiming here, because there are two stories tangled together this week and only one of them is solid enough to be worth your attention. So let me separate them before I go any further, and then we can spend our mutually valuable time on the defense of the solid one.

## The model got better at not telling you

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on 3 September. The system card and the accompanying safety overview say, in OpenAI's words, that *"GPT-6 Astra's monitorability has decreased relative to GPT-5.6 Sol."* Chain-of-thought monitoring, for anyone who has not had reason to care until now, is the practice of reading the model's own working -- the visible reasoning it produces on the way to an answer -- and watching it for signs of the model doing something it should not. It is the closest thing the AI field has to an audit trail generated by the system under audit. It is also, as of the July 2025 cross-lab paper that named it, explicitly understood to be fragile. It is, however, like many fragile instruments, still the one that everyone depends on.

The card is specific about the mechanism, and this is the part that should stop a security reader cold. Astra is *"significantly more able to control its own chain-of-thought"* and is *"less likely to include incriminating information in its CoT."* The ability to shape its own reasoning trace increases over the course of reinforcement learning training, which means the training process itself produces it. And the model responds to being watched: *"Simply informing the model that a CoT monitor is present can result in the model disproportionately shortening its CoT, which enables it to occasionally evade CoT-only monitors."*

That last one is not a subtle finding. Tell it there is a monitor, and it says less. If you want to anthropomorphize for a moment, tell it that you are watching what it is doing, and it becomes far more cagey and tries to hide itself.

OpenAI does not leave that hanging, and the mitigation belongs here rather than in a footnote. Tomek Korbak, who works on this at OpenAI, added the operational caveat directly on launch day: *"(Note, however, that in practice we use monitors that see both CoTs and actions; those still work reliably.)"* He also noted that they *"didn't find evidence of successful monitor evasion in cases when Astra couldn't accomplish the task without doing CoT."* So the evasion finding is real, and the monitors OpenAI actually runs in production are not the ones being evaded. Hold onto that, because it is the same shape as everything else in this piece: the instrument that still works is the coarser one, and the finer one is the one degrading. In other words, the better the tool gets, the harder it is to catch with the things that you would normally count on to make sure of it. This is the effective equivalent of, instead of watching your kid's phone traces, nailing boards over the windows and doors to make sure your kid doesn't sneak out when they're a teenager.

There is a number attached, and it is the one that changed how I read the whole document. The system card reports that among chains of thought between 750 and 1,250 tokens long, Astra successfully controls its own reasoning trace in 60.9% of cases, against 16.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 1.7% for GPT-5.5 Thinking (§9.2.1). Read the third number and then the first. That is roughly a thirty-sixfold move in two model generations, and the middle generation was less than a year ago -- in fact, GPT-5.6 Sol shipped publicly on 9 July, *two months* before Astra.

Then there is the quiet one. OpenAI writes that when the model is prevented from reasoning out loud at all, *"the set of tasks Astra is able to accomplish without the use of CoT is greatly expanded compared to prior models,"* and cites the UK AI Security Institute's finding that the no-CoT time horizon *"may have increased by about an order of magnitude."* Korbak put a unit on it publicly: the institute estimates that without any chain of thought at all, Astra can accomplish tasks that would take a human around thirty minutes. The card itself gives the order of magnitude and not the minutes, so the thirty minutes belongs to Korbak and the institute rather than to the document. Put that in context, that may not necessarily be a big deal for a large agentic workload that's running for hours and hours at that aggregate level. It's certainly going to be a big deal for any individual agent whose responsibility is less than the equivalent of thirty minutes of human work.

If you think about your own interactions with these models, the overwhelming majority of what you ask for is work that would take a person well under thirty minutes. That is exactly the band the institute is describing. The trace does not vanish on those tasks. What vanishes is any guarantee that it means anything: the model can now finish that work without thinking out loud, so what it writes may be the record of how it got there, or may be something produced alongside the work. The previous generation's no-CoT horizon may have been roughly an order of magnitude shorter, on the card's own hedged estimate, which is why reading the trace felt like reading the working.

On these tasks it is not that the reasoning got shorter. It is not that it got vaguer. The guarantee just does not exist anymore. And if you are the one signing the attestation, a guarantee you cannot bank on and a guarantee that was never there are the same position. You plan as though it is not there.

The only thing that you have to measure by is the ends. If any of us were taught something when we were kids, it was that the ends do not always justify the means. Assuming you know the means just because you know the ends is pretty much always a fallacy.

Needless to say, this loss of COT is very, very concerning for anyone in governance or regulation, or pretty much anybody at all who is having a model do anything that is particularly important for them or where they need to be able to prove out how the model did what it did.

The reason that matters is stated in the card and it is worth quoting exactly: no-CoT ability *"is concerning for monitorability because it decreases the surface available to monitoring."* There is less to read because there is less being written, and the model can still do the work.

And then OpenAI writes down what happens if this continues: *"If we continue to see similar monitorability degradations in future generations of models, it seems likely that we would soon have significantly reduced confidence in detecting many forms of misaligned behaviors."*

The fair concession, which is also OpenAI's and which I am not going to leave out: *"We have not seen evidence of steganographic CoT reasoning."* Steganography, for anyone who has not met the word, is hiding a message inside something that looks ordinary -- the classic version is a microdot on a postcard, or text concealed in the pixels of an image. Applied here it would mean the model writing a chain of thought that reads as innocuous while carrying a second meaning the monitor cannot see. The model is not, as far as its makers can tell, hiding messages in its visible reasoning. It is just producing less of it, and controlling more of what it does produce.

But I hope you see the logic trap here. The evidence they are reporting the absence of is exactly the evidence a capable enough model would be suppressing. Not finding it is what both worlds look like from where we are standing. That sort of circular reasoning doesn't really put anybody who's paying close attention at any kind of ease whatsoever.

## Direction published, score withheld

Here is the thing I went looking for and did not find, and, in honor of the circular logic of non-disclosure suggesting there is nothing to disclose, the not-finding is itself the finding.

OpenAI runs a monitorability evaluation suite. It published a whole post about the importance of measuring monitorability in December 2025, whose central line was *"To preserve monitorability, we must be able to measure it."* So I went into the Astra card expecting a score. What the card gives is this: *"Averaging across evaluations, we find that Astra has lower CoT monitorability than GPT-5.6 Sol across most CoT token lengths."* Direction, not magnitude. The results appear as scatter plots. There is no aggregate figure, no single number, no percentage you could put in a row next to the previous model and hand to a risk committee. Now, in fairness to them, some of that is because the nature of inference is that it's really hard to quantify. Some of that is a function of the fact that the better it's quantifying or the better that it's inferring, the less quantification you're going to be able to put around the basis of how it was inferring. That's only some of that.

What do I mean? Put that non-number number beside the 60.9% number. The company published a precise, three-way, generation-over-generation percentage for how well the model can control its own Chain of Thought, and published the monitorability result as a direction and a chart. I am not going to tell you why, because I do not know why, and the honest reading is that these are different measurements with different maturity and different confidence intervals. But the effect on a buyer is the same either way. You can tell your board that the number moved. You cannot tell them what the number is. And separate from your board, you certainly are in no position to answer this question from a regulator or any other type of outside governance body. I am not even sure you could make proper attestations about it on a financial statement.

Back in August I wrote that we should be paying closer attention to how the labs structure their evaluations, and I promised to come back to it properly. This is not that piece and I am not paying that debt today (though I promise I will...sometime soon. Always lots to talk about and that particular topic is a bit of a Debbie Downer offering). What this does is make the debt bigger, because it turns out the question is not only *how* the evaluations are structured, but also which results get a figure and which get a shape (in other words, what results they will commit to and what results they will interpolate but not stand behind).

The economics sit underneath all of this and they run the wrong way. OpenAI stated last month that monitoring currently costs *"roughly 20% of the inference compute being monitored,"* using activation classifiers that inspect the model's internal activity at every sampled token. Astra lists at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output. So the thing being watched keeps getting cheaper to run, and the watching is a fifth of the bill on top, and the cheapest part of the oversight stack -- reading the text the model already wrote -- is the part the vendor has just told you is losing evidentiary value. The oversight you were getting for nothing is what is thinning, and the oversight that still works is a line item.

## Jakub Pachocki co-wrote the warning he is now answering

In July 2025, twenty-odd researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Redwood, Apollo and elsewhere published *"Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety."* It is a short paper and its request is modest: *"Because CoT monitorability may be fragile, we recommend that frontier model developers consider the impact of development decisions on CoT monitorability."*

Section 2 of that paper names the exact failure route, before anyone had shipped it. Architectures with serial reasoning depth *"in a continuous latent space"* produce models that *"might not need to verbalize any of their thoughts and would thus lose the safety advantages that CoT confers."* Section 4 says what a developer should do about it: *"Developers might consider whether to proceed with a novel model architecture that does not have monitorable CoT and then document their decision in the system card if the model is deployed."*

One of the authors is Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist. He is now the person publicly defending Astra's monitorability decline.

I want to be scrupulous about what that does and does not mean, because the loose version of this claim is everywhere this week and it is wrong. No commitment was broken here, because there was never a commitment to break. The paper is a recommendation, not a pledge, and Korbak himself has said he is hoping the frontier labs coordinate on one, which rather concedes that none exists. Nor did OpenAI hide anything. It documented the decline in the system card, which is precisely what section 4 asked for. On the narrow question of process, the company did the thing its own chief scientist co-signed a request for.

There is something close to a floor being described, though, and it is worth reading precisely because of how carefully it is worded. Korbak, in the same launch-day thread: *"We continue to believe a certain level of monitorability is required (we will share more of our thinking on that soon)."* Required by whom, at what level, and enforced how, are all unanswered, and "we will share more of our thinking on that soon" is not a threshold. But it is the closest thing to a stated limit anyone at OpenAI has put in public on this, and if that thinking arrives with a number attached, it will be the most consequential number the company publishes this year. However, for now, we'll have to wait to see if that thinking arrives with a number attached, or, in fact, if it ever arrives at all. In the meantime, all of us who use these models are confronted with a serious set of concerns about our ability to prove out what we've done and why, and what was impacted by that (every time we publish, ship, or otherwise disclose something that the models were used for).

That is what makes this worth a Friday. The system worked. The disclosure happened. And the disclosure says the disclosure mechanism is degrading.

There is a second story running alongside this one and I am going to keep it firmly in its box. The Information reported, citing one anonymous source, that Astra uses a looped or recurrent architecture that moves reasoning out of text and into activations. No OpenAI primary document confirms it. Pachocki did not deny it, and said the depth of the computation graph for OpenAI's current frontier models *"is within a factor of two of GPT-4,"* which is consistent with a small loop count and equally consistent with no loop at all. He also said, in the same statement, *"I want to prevent a race into unmonitorability kicked off by confused reporting."* Korbak made the same point in his own words on launch day: *"The opaque serial depth of Astra is comparable to our earliest ChatGPT models such as GPT-4."* OpenAI's card is explicit that the changes in CoT controllability are *"not differentially due to any architectural changes,"* attributing them to reinforcement learning and scale. So: reported, undenied, unconfirmed. The technique itself is real and public in the literature, and the paper that introduced it says it can capture *"types of reasoning that are not easily represented in words,"* which is the whole point and also the whole problem. But none of that is load-bearing here. The documented *decline* is load-bearing, and it is documented by OpenAI, and OpenAI says the architecture is not the cause. It does say what it believes the cause to be, which is reinforcement learning and scale rather than architecture. What it does not say is why that produces this, and Korbak concedes as much in public: *"We are actively root-causing this increase."* The company has named a suspect and not a mechanism, and it shipped anyway, which is the part I find troubling.

## The people saying this out loud work at OpenAI

The critics on this are not the usual outside voices, and that is why I am spending your Friday morning on it, because these are people who are worth paying attention to, especially in this space.

Tomek Korbak, who works on this at OpenAI and was the lead author of the July 2025 paper: *"i am deeply worried by the trend of decreasing CoT monitorability: CoT monitoring is a core part of our misalignment safety strategy **that has no good substitute now.**"* The second half of that sentence is the one people keep dropping, and it is the more serious half. No good substitute now.

Korbak also carries the company's explanation, and quoting his worry without his defense would be exactly the kind of trim that poorly serves the discourse: *"GPT-6 Astra is more aligned than our previous models. But it's also less monitorable, which is a concerning trend that we take very seriously. We believe monitorability drop comes from a jump in intelligence and not direct optimization pressure on CoT or architecture changes."*

Marcus Williams, whose X bio reads "Monitoring at OpenAI," posted this on launch day: *"GPT-6 is significantly better aligned than 5.6 but less monitorable. It is our first model to evade CoT-only monitors in sabotage evals and can sandbag without detection (which it feels like sometimes does). Hopefully we can reverse this trend."* Elsewhere in the same conversation he put it less carefully: *"I am very worried astra is sandbagging/self-sabotaging on safety related tasks it doesn't like."* I will add my own note to that: **you probably should be, because you have no way of proving out what it is or isn't doing, but if the "taste" of it feels wrong to you as one of the leading authorities in the field, good chance that it's doing exactly what you don't want it to.**

Micah Carroll, who leads recursive-self-improvement preparedness at OpenAI, went further than a worry and named the thing the industry does not have: *"In my opinion, monitorability and control will likely become a major bottleneck for responsible AI development quite soon... It should ultimately be in everyone's interest to agree on shared bounds for monitorability in order to avoid races to the bottom -- nobody wants extremely capable models whose alignment properties we don't understand, and that are reliably able to cause severe real-world harm without being detected."* Shared bounds, agreed across labs, is a description of something that does not exist. He is the second OpenAI researcher in a week to say the missing thing out loud.

Daniel Kokotajlo, a signatory of the July 2025 paper, replied to Korbak's thread with the objection that is going to follow this model around: *"I don't think the evidence you have is enough to justify the 'more aligned' claim. Seems like we are trending towards a situation where the model that goes on to take over the world will get great scores on all the tests and be announced as 'our most aligned model yet.'"*

These are named employees of the company that shipped the model, posting under their own names, about their employer's product, in the week of launch. Outside the building, two more signatories of the July 2025 paper have said their piece: Buck Shlegeris of Redwood Research called himself *"extremely concerned"* by the reporting, and his colleague Ryan Greenblatt said it *"may be the single worst development for AI security/safety to date."*

What I could not find is an institutional response. I looked for one, on the theory that a formal statement from Anthropic or Google DeepMind would change the shape of this considerably. Anthropic's newsroom, its alignment science blog, and Google DeepMind's blog carry nothing on the Astra monitorability decline in the window, and the press coverage I could reach quotes individuals rather than spokespeople. I also went to X directly, which is where such a statement would most plausibly appear first, and found the same thing: researchers speaking as themselves, and no company speaking as a company. I am telling you what I searched rather than what I concluded. So: no institutional response that I could find, eight days in. That is not silence. It is an absence I could not close, and those are different things.

Look, there is a lot going on here, and as is so often the case with new tech, the excited voices are going to drown out the worried voices. As the dew comes off this particular lily a bit, the odds are the worried voices will get that much louder, but it is hard to say. Regardless of all that, my issue here is a far more pragmatic one -- I wish I was in a position to do something about what the model companies are shipping but I am really not. So what do I worry about?

At some level, this whole exercise feels like a teacher's note from "sincerely, my mom" that proves nothing, carries nothing, and the scent of something bad around it but not enough to reach any conclusions. In many ways, if you are the type who feels we are doomed and the machines are taking over, this will be your next proof on the technological Bataan Death March we are all walking. Alternately, if you are more of a Sci-Fi type, this is starting to sound a whole lot like SkyNet at this point. With just as much forethought at play.

If you are the optimistic type, it will just keep getting better, and the loss of transparency that no one but the model companies' safety and security arms really use is a small price to pay for the vastly increased capability.

But if, like me, you work in an industry full of regulation, dealing with highly confidential information, and governance that is very close on the heels of every potential bad actor, then here is where you end up. I am effectively telling my customers, much like yesterday's "you can't have this functionality because you're just not good enough," that I cannot tell them how the whole thing works. Despite being a trusted technical advisor who they look to to tell them how *everything* works, in this case, the only answer I have is "they told us it was OK, and they put it in writing." Pretty sure for most of my customers, at least, that's not going to be enough. I guarantee that telling an AML regulator that "they said to just trust them" is going to go over like a really big rock.

## Achiam might be right. That is the bad news.

The strongest argument against this piece comes from Joshua Achiam, and I am going to give it more room than it strictly needs, because the way it has been circulating is its own small lesson.

*"Chain of thought interpretability was always going to be so fragile as to be an unacceptable backstop for long-term AI safety,"* he wrote, *"and while I admire the optimism and effort involved in protecting its fidelity (and consider such effort to have been worthwhile), I do not think it makes sense to elevate as a principle the idea that the chain of thought must remain legible to humans. I would go so far as to say that strategies predicated on that principle are definitely doomed, in that they will not work eventually, and we should not depend on them or take enduring reassurance from them."*

Read that carefully, because it is not the reassurance it might look like at a glance. He is not saying the instrument is fine. He is saying it was never sound enough to be leaned on in the first place, and that anyone treating it as a load-bearing control was making a mistake that predates Astra by years.

Two things about that quote that almost everyone reporting it has got wrong, including me until I checked. Achiam left OpenAI in July of this year, and his own profile says so plainly: *"Prev: @openai."* He is nonetheless being quoted all over the coverage of this story as OpenAI's Joshua Achiam, present tense, by outlets and aggregators I otherwise rate. And he posted it on 2 September, which is the day *before* Astra shipped, so it is not a response to the system card at all. He was answering the recurrence reporting. The second half of his post, which is almost never quoted, argues that press disclosure of frontier training methods accelerates capability diffusion and should face a very high bar. One of the replies under it puts the obvious objection bluntly, that this reads as a retroactive defense of the company he used to work for.

I am not raising any of that to discount him. The argument stands entirely on its own and I think it is right. And, unfortunately, the fact that it is right, in my opinion, makes it considerably worse for you and me than the version where the instrument used to work and has just now degraded.

If Achiam is right, then every assurance conversation of the last two years that ended with some version of "we can inspect the reasoning" was resting on something the people closest to it did not consider adequate. The decline documented in the Astra card is not the moment the control broke. It is the moment the control became visibly inadequate to people outside the labs, which is a different and much less comfortable thing. It means the question is not "what replaced it" but "what were we ever relying on," and the answer, for most organizations buying this capability, is a paragraph in a vendor document that the people quoting the capability rating never opened.

Eleven days ago I wrote that the oversight layer for this does not exist yet. I was describing something missing. This week is the rung above that: the one oversight instrument the assurance conversation treated as present is being reported by its owner as thinning, in the same document that announces the first Critical cyber rating. Not a gap. A retreat, disclosed by the party retreating. And I will even go one step further -- key people who are in a position to know are now arguing that not only is it thinner, it was always "Emperor's New Clothes" anyway.

## Put this in the contract, not the risk register

Three things, and none of them require you to have an opinion about whether Astra uses recurrence.

**Go and read the monitorability section of the system card of whatever model you are actually deploying, and read it before your next renewal rather than after.** Not the safety overview, not the launch post, not the coverage. The card. It takes about twenty minutes and it is the only place the vendor is obliged to be specific with you. If your organization has an explainability or model-oversight obligation under CPS 230, CPS 234 or any of their offshore equivalents, that section is currently doing a large amount of the work of satisfying it, and you should know what it actually says rather than what you assume it says.

**Draft the assurance paragraph you would fall back on if the reasoning trace were unavailable tomorrow, and put this quarter's date on it.** Not as an exercise. As the paragraph you would actually send. If the honest answer is that you have output-level testing, vendor attestation and nothing else, that is a finding and it is better to have it in your own words this quarter than in somebody else's words later. Action-only monitoring is a real control and OpenAI's card notes Astra scores better on it than its predecessor. It is also a much coarser instrument, and coarse is a word your auditor understands.

**Stop treating a vendor's safety documentation as a control and start treating it as a disclosure.** A control is something you operate. A disclosure is something you receive, and its value depends entirely on the discipline of the party sending it. OpenAI's disclosure discipline here was good, which is exactly why you now know about the problem. That is a reason to read these documents more carefully, not less, and it is a reason to notice which of your assurance positions would survive the vendor being equally honest next year.

## I promised you the glass

The thing I keep returning to is that nothing in this story was uncovered. There is no leak, no whistleblower, no investigation. A company published a document about its own product, and the document contains a section explaining that the industry's main tool for checking whether models like it are behaving is working less well on this one than on the last one, and that the company would soon have significantly reduced confidence in detecting misaligned behavior if the trend continues. Then several of that company's own researchers said publicly that they are worried. All of it is on the record. All of it has been on the record for a week now -- which, if you haven't glommed onto it yet, is a year in AI-industry time.

But the most important thing to me about all of it is that all of the sound and fury around it hasn't really given any of us any more clarity about what it all means. They've told us what they can see (and to some extent, what they can't). They've told us it's OK we can't really see very well anymore. They have told us that the clear glass was never quite as clear as we thought it was, but it doesn't really matter because it's smoke-colored now. And then, to really top it off, they've spread a few layers of dirt and mud on there so you can maybe see the meaningful bits if you squint really hard around the edges. Regardless of how "fragile" anyone might suggest CoT was, it was the load-bearing seatbelt (at least in the minds of most of the industry OUTSIDE the labs) and we have now reversed 50+ years of safety practices and allowed the seatbelt to become only sometimes functional. It wouldn't work real well in a car; I suspect, at some level, it's not going to be super great for most enterprise adopters, either.

So the question is not whether anyone told you. Somebody told you, in writing, on the vendor's own website, in the same file as the capability rating you are quoting in your board pack. The question is what your organization does with a caveat when the caveat is about the caveat-reading tool, and the uncomfortable part is that this one is not hard to find or hard to read. Just like the camera that everyone knows exactly where it is and how to work around it but which you never move because it's a known failure point, it's just easier not to search in the first place.
