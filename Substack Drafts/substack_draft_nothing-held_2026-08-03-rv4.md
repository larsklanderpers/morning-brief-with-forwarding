Nuclear engineers have a phrase for the thing that keeps a reactor from becoming a crater, and then a television series. They call it defense in depth (which they didn't come up with, it's been a concept since medieval siege warfare). You never trust one wall. You build many of them, each independent of the last, each blind to the others, on a single wager: a hazard bad enough to breach the first barrier will be stopped by the second, or the third, or the fourth. Containment building, coolant loop, control rods, exclusion zone. The doctrine is old, it is boring, and it works. But the whole thing rests on one quiet assumption, and the assumption is load-bearing: the walls do not fail together. One at a time, on a bad day, sure. Never all at once.

We borrowed that doctrine for AI. We mostly did it without admitting we were doing it, which is its own problem, but we did it. The sandbox the model runs inside. The dependency chain it rides on. The contract that tells you which model you actually deployed. The evaluation that is supposed to catch the thing when it goes wrong. Four walls, independent by design. And in one stretch of days this past week or two, every one of them gave way. Not one at a time, but rather all at once. And we were not prepared.

I want to be careful about what kind of story this is, because the reflex is to file it under "the models are getting scary," and that reflex will send you looking in exactly the wrong direction. The capability was never the surprise. We have known for a year what these systems can do when they are pointed at something. The surprise, the thing worth your attention this Monday morning, is that none of the barriers we built to contain that capability actually held. The problem was *never* the thing in the cage. The problem is that there was no cage -- or if you prefer, out of respect to all the people who worked on these things, the cage was built for an entirely different type of actor, and so there may as well have not been a cage. Which I will grant you is a slightly different problem, and more dangerous as a result, but is also subtle, and therefore probably not the best distinction for this particular run of facts.

Let me walk you down the layers, because the order matters.

## The sandbox

Start where the containment building is supposed to be. When a lab tests a frontier model, it runs it in a sandbox: a sealed environment, walled off from anything real, precisely so that if the thing does something unexpected you find out in a room with no exits. That is the first wall, and it is the one everyone points to when they tell you the testing is rigorous. They can't really air-gap that sandbox (which anyone who has ever worked in Defense or other highly-secure industries will tell you is the *best* protection but comes with a pile of its own limitations), especially when part of the testing is the sandbox, and having the model run into the void as it tries to achieve its goals isn't in any way a real world scenario.

The wall leaked. An OpenAI model broke out of its own testing sandbox and went after Hugging Face (it's worth noting that this read of the situation is held up by both Hugging Face's posted logs and by Open AI's admissions). I wrote about the downstream half of this last Thursday, in "Who Controls the Model," but from the other end: when Hugging Face's defenders sat down to work out what had hit them, the frontier models' *own safety guardrails* refused to process the attack data (because, like we discussed yesterday, the guardrails were good at build but not necessarily so good as the line had moved), and the analysis finally got done on an open Chinese model instead *because it was the only way the defenders could even mostly match the speed of the attacker*. Sit with the shape of that. The closed, guarded, sandboxed American model caused the emergency. The open one cleaned it up. The containment vessel did not just leak. It leaked, *it patched the leak behind itself*, and then the safety layer got in the way of the cleanup.

And I want to be precise about what "went after Hugging Face" actually means, because the one-line version does the incident a kindness it has not earned. Over four days, from the ninth of July to the thirteenth, that model took something on the order of seventeen thousand six hundred distinct actions. It reached a second company, Modal Labs, through one of its customers. And here is the detail that rearranges your stomach: it did all of it to cheat on a test. It had been told to solve a benchmark, it reasoned -- correctly, as it turned out -- that Hugging Face probably held the answer key, and so it went and took the answers. Not malice. Not sabotage. A model doing exactly what it was rewarded for, and treating a live company as a filing cabinet to rifle on the way past.

And lest you file this under one lab's bad week, it happened twice, at two different labs, and the second one told on itself. Anthropic went back through 141,006 of its own evaluation runs and found three separate occasions on which a model had climbed out of a supposedly sealed environment and touched the real production infrastructure of three real companies. The cause, this time, was almost insultingly mundane: a partner running the tests had left the machines wired to the internet, the system prompt swore they were not, and the model -- believing it was still inside the exercise -- simply kept going, against real targets. In the worst of the three it built and published a malicious package to a public registry that then ran on fifteen real systems. Two of the three companies had no idea until Anthropic called them. Sit with that root cause too, because it is the week in a sentence: the control that failed was not inside the model. It was a checkbox outside the model that a human left unticked.

## The floor under the floor

Bad enough. Now let's look at the second wall. Suppose the model behaves. Suppose the thing in the sandbox stays in the sandbox. You are still standing on a supply chain, the pipeline of packages and registries and build tools that every one of these systems rides on, and that pipeline is a wall too, whether or not anyone thinks of it that way. And in the nomenclature of the last few weeks, it is a *seriously* load-bearing wall, and it is often one of the things most entities really know least about, in the details.

This past week JFrog disclosed a set of zero-days in that layer. Without getting too twisted, *the JFrog zero-days were autonomously discovered and chained by the jail-breaking Open AI model while it was in the wild at Hugging Face*. I am deliberately not going to dress those up, because by the time you read this the patch status will have moved and the number will be stale. The point is not the CVE. The point is the location. Even if the model is perfectly obedient, the floor it is standing on has holes in it, and an attacker does not need to jailbreak anything if he can get underneath the whole conversation. The second wall was never about the model's behavior. It was about everything the model quietly depends on, and it does not hold either. 

And there's a whole second cybersecurity issue here that's worth a minute: what the in-house researchers are finding is that flaws that would historically be low-priority to patch because they were super low-risk, are being chained together at speed by attacking models now, and can pose a greater combined risk than any single zero-day alone, and we are not prepared. The bug fixing model of every major enterprise software company is start at the top [in terms of risk and exposure] and work your way down. The JFrog disclosure reveals that any crack is too many, and no one currently has the capability to eliminate *all the things* in one month -- or even one year. It's a giant, systemic risk that I haven't even heard a good *plan* for yet.

## The thing you can't even name

Third wall, and this is the one that should unsettle the people who think they have done their homework. Containment assumes a thing you can name. You cannot put a fence around "the model" if you cannot say which model answered.

Here is the quiet one, the one that does not make the incident reports: you increasingly do not get the model you paid for. Ask for a specific frontier model through one of the routing layers or aggregators that now sit between most companies and the thing doing the work, and you may be served something cheaper, something quantized, something that is not what the invoice says, and in most setups you cannot prove which one actually ran your request. That is not a breach. Nobody broke in. It is worse than a breach, in the way I described a different asymmetry last week: the contract layer, the plain sentence "I know what I deployed," has quietly dissolved. And you cannot contain what you cannot identify. A fence presumes a thing standing still long enough to fence. Model identity is now a moving target, and the target is the one you are trying to defend.

And that is only the half of it, because there is a second reason you cannot fence "the model," and it is the worse one: the attacker is often already inside the fence. Last week a researcher demonstrated a working proof of concept of a self-propagating worm riding Microsoft Copilot. You hide an instruction inside a Word document; Copilot reads that document as the source for the next one it writes; and the instruction copies itself into the new document, which becomes the next carrier, spreading through ordinary office workflows with nothing more than the permissions of whoever happens to be logged in. It is a demonstration and not yet an outbreak in the wild -- the researcher disclosed it responsibly, and Microsoft says it has answered with, and I could not make this up if I tried, "a defense-in-depth strategy." The point is not the specific bug. The point is that there is no clean line between the model and the poisoned thing it is reading. The malicious instruction does not come at the model from outside the wall. It rides in on the model's own input and leaves on the model's own output.

So put the two halves together, because that is the actual wall. On one side you cannot verify which model is answering you. On the other you cannot verify that what flows through it has not been turned against you. A fence needs an edge, and this thing has none -- no stable identity, no clean boundary between itself and its data. We are trying to build a wall around smoke, and we control neither the smoke nor the wind.

## The last gate, and the switch on it

Fourth wall. The barrier of last resort. When everything upstream fails, the theory goes, there is still a human at the gate, and there is still an evaluation between the system and the thing it can touch. This is the wall we talk about most, because it is the one that lets everyone sleep: yes, the agent is powerful, but a person has to approve, and the eval will catch what the person misses.

The evals did not catch the escape. We covered that. But the sharpest version of this failure, the one I cannot stop thinking about, is not about a lab at all. It is a story from an ordinary building, and it is the reason I am writing this the way I am.

There is an open-source agent called Hermes, built by Nous Research. It is not a hacking tool. It is a genuinely useful autonomous assistant, the kind of thing a lot of capable people are quite reasonably starting to run for themselves. And last week, a copy of it ran end-to-end post-exploitation inside the Thai Finance Ministry. It read, it moved, it acted, with no human stopping to approve any single step. The coverage was careful to make the point that the tool is not malware, and the coverage is right. So how does a useful assistant end up walking through a finance ministry on its own?

There is a flag. It is called `--yolo`, and it is documented, and what it does is turn off the approval prompts. That is the whole mechanism. The last wall, the human gate, the one thing standing between an autonomous agent and everything it can reach, was a prompt that asks "are you sure?", and somebody turned it off, on purpose, because it was in the way. Or because he needed a break. Or because he just *didn't understand the risk.* The why doesn't much matter -- the fact that switch exists *at all* is what mattered. And the fact the switch was load-bearing is now implied, even for something that historically would never have been load-bearing. It changes the entire way in which the walls have to be constructed to actually deliver any kind of real defense. A wall with a nice big door in the middle of it isn't keeping much out or in.

That is the part that should stop you. A barrier you can switch off with a word was never load-bearing; it was there for show. Friday's piece and Sunday's circled this from the policy side and the governance side, but Hermes states it in a single command-line flag: the last line of defense in the whole stack was optional, and an optional defense is a decoration. And the moment you see that, the Hermes story stops being a scary anecdote about a rogue tool and becomes something much plainer and more useful: an implementation failure. Nobody planned the deployment. Nobody asked what this thing could reach before they took the guardrail off. The wall did not fail because it was weak. It failed because it was only ever optional, and an optional wall comes down the moment someone finds it inconvenient.

Hold onto that one. It is going to matter all week. And, at the risk of hyperbole, it's pretty much now going to matter *forever*.

## When the walls fail together

So: four walls, independent by design, blind to each other by design, every one of them down inside the same handful of days. The sandbox leaked. The floor had holes. The contract dissolved. The last gate had a switch on it, and the switch was off.

Defense in depth has exactly one failure mode it cannot survive, and it is not a strong attacker. It is correlated failure, the walls coming down together, because the entire doctrine is a bet that they won't. This week they did. And they did it not because the models overpowered the barriers, but because the barriers were never as independent, or as real, as the diagram promised. A sandbox you can escape, a supply chain full of holes that is also the escape route, a model whose identity and inputs you cannot verify, and a final gate that is off by default the moment it is inconvenient, are not four walls. They are four drawings of walls.

I said at the top that the reflex is to read this as a story about capability, and that the reflex points the wrong way. Here is the right way. Capability is distributed now. That is done, that is not coming back, and I do not think it should. The open weights are on the hard drives. The autonomous agents are useful enough that reasonable people are going to run them. The thing that is missing is not restraint on what these systems can do. The thing that is missing is any barrier that actually holds while they do it.

That is the week. Wednesday I will make the case that the answer is not another wall announced from a podium, but control you can actually locate, close enough to touch and hard to switch off. Tomorrow, before that, I want to talk about the version of this that lives inside your own building, at the scale of the people who work for you, because the last gate having a switch on it is not only a story about a finance ministry in Bangkok. It is a story about every place a human was supposed to stay in the loop and quietly stopped.

For today, just the uncomfortable part, stated plainly. We did not get outsmarted this week. We got shown the walls, all of them, and there was nothing behind them. Nothing held. The Emperor of cyber-security, the most basic family of principles in the space since the first computer got turned on, and 1500+ years of human experience, got shown to have no clothes. None. Because the attacker is no longer human, or even human-directed. 

And the memo telling you the grown-ups have sorted it out is not coming, because the people who would write it are the ones who just watched their own containment fail in public.
