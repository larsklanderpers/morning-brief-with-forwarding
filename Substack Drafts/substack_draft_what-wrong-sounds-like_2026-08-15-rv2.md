It has been a heavy week on this page. Five days of instruments that did not do what the people relying on them assumed.

So here is the part that is actually good news, and it has been sitting underneath all five days without me saying it out loud.

Anybody who has worked in one place long enough knows which noise is normal. The compressor that always groans on startup and means nothing. The report that runs eleven minutes instead of four, which is fine on a Monday and is not fine on a Thursday. The invoice that is a little too round. Nobody taught you this and it is not written anywhere. You accumulated it, and you probably could not produce a list of it if I asked, but you would notice in a second if it were off.

That is the scarcest thing in the room right now, and this week is five consecutive proofs of it.

## What actually went wrong on Monday through Friday

Go back through it, because the pattern only shows up laid side by side.

The control that checked identity was designed by somebody who did not know that an agent reads text an attacker wrote. The organization that could not name which tasks moved had never sat with the tasks. The register scoped to what you operate was drafted by somebody who had not noticed that suppliers now run models inside services. The workplace surveillance statute pointed employer-to-employee because nobody drafting it imagined the surveillance walking in as a guest. And the permission prompt was parsing strings because it was built by people solving a string problem rather than a judgment problem.

Every one of those was a competent person building a sensible thing. Not one of them failed for lack of technical skill. They failed because whoever scoped the control had never stood in the room it was supposed to protect, and so they aimed it at the thing that was easy to describe instead of the thing that actually goes wrong.

Which is a problem, and it is also a job description.

## One signpost, because I used a word yesterday in a different sense

Yesterday's piece was about capability -- the model's. A classifier that catches more than a tired human does.

Today's is about capability too, and it is entirely yours, and the two are not in competition. The machine got better at doing. It did not get better at knowing which of your Thursdays is the wrong kind of Thursday. That gap is not closing on any timeline I can see, and it is the only part of this whole story where the scarcity runs in your favor. And I am not the only one who sees this; the industry voices out there that are thought leading on this particular topic refer to it by the simple idea of "taste." And as in so many aspects of life, taste is often in short supply.

## The question changed while everybody was arguing

Here is the shift worth noticing, because it happened quietly and it is one of many reasons the displacement argument has gone a bit flat.

For about two years the question was *will this happen to me*, and what will I do when it does. For those making the investment in the future now, the question has now changed more to *how fast can I shape this in my function.* And the reason the doom conversation is losing oxygen is not that the concern was wrong. It is that the people who absorbed it fastest stopped arguing and started building, and they are no longer in the comments.

The research, limited as it is, points the same way, though I want to be careful about how hard I lean on it. The finding I keep seeing is that what predicts who does well with these tools is domain knowledge rather than profession -- that a good accountant beats a mediocre engineer at getting useful work out of an agent pointed at accounting. That matches everything I have watched first-hand. It is also the kind of claim that gets repeated more often than it gets sourced, so take it as a solid indicator rather than a settled result. Anecdotally, I will say that, from what I have seen, AI is like almost anything else: the more you invest into making it work for you, the more benefit you will get from it. 

## The thing companies are not telling you

Now the number that made me want to write this as the Saturday piece rather than a Wednesday one.

BCG surveyed nearly twelve thousand workers across fourteen markets this year. Among people who use these tools regularly in frontline roles, **forty-two percent say they save at least a full working day every week**. And **sixty-six percent report getting limited guidance or none at all** about what to do with the time.

Sit with the shape of that. The capability arrived. The hours are real, at least as reported. And two thirds of the people holding those hours were handed them with no instruction, which means the time is being quietly reabsorbed into more of the same work, and nobody is deciding anything.

Two honest caveats, because this is a survey and I have spent a week complaining about numbers people did not read carefully. Those figures describe regular users in frontline roles, not the whole workforce. And BCG's headline finding elsewhere in the same study -- that forty-seven percent say they now spend more time directing these tools than doing the work themselves -- is self-reported perception rather than measurement. *Say* is doing real work in that sentence. But the underlying truth remains: despite all the warnings of a job-pocalypse, what we have seen, so far, across the majority of working groups is instead job *transformation.* The hiring story bears this out; from the same surveys and others, the companies most heavily invested in AI are actually hiring, not firing. The difference, in my opinion, is executive management that sees AI as an efficiency tool vs. a capability tool.

But the gap is the story regardless of the decimal places. Nobody is going to tell you what to do with the day. That is not a conspiracy, it is just that your employer has not worked it out either. That means, should you choose to take it, you have a unique opportunity to drive the transition in the direction that most benefits you and your path.

## The seat that is actually forming

Two things surfaced this week that point at the same shape from different directions, and I want to describe them carefully because neither is a job market yet.

The first is what one commentator has taken to calling the handoff problem: deciding what to delegate, and verifying what came back. Not operating the agent. The judgment on either side of it. That is the Editor and the Risk Steward from the archetype work, restated as a *skill* rather than a title, which is a considerably more useful framing because you can practice a skill on Monday without asking anyone's permission.

The second is a role sighting inside enterprise AI operations, currently going by the Cost-per-Accepted-Task Analyst: somebody measuring the business value of what agents actually produce rather than the tokens they consume. It is the same seat that the AI FinOps category has been circling -- the principle that every request has to create more value than it costs. Two different corners of the market naming the same job is the evidence pattern worth watching.

**I want to be straight about the status of both.** These come from practitioner and aggregator sources with no posting counts behind them. I could not find live listings. So this is an emerging role, not a market, and anyone telling you there is a hiring wave here is ahead of the evidence. What makes it interesting is not the volume. It is that the shape being described in both cases is *domain judgment applied to machine output*, which is the thing this whole piece is about. And if you listen to the space, it is one of the things that almost everyone you listen to, regardless of the specific shape of how to do the work, is that the abstract position is no longer "the AI overlords are coming for us" and more "the most important differentiator in the future will be the character of your complex decision making skill", which while a lot more words, is a far more optimistic and achievable goal for everyone.

We're fighting this battle right now. We're searching for QA testers, and we need people who can not only run the battery of items detailed in the CI/CD pipeline that go with the job but also differentiate the customer experience, the actual "quality" of the output, and other soft measurements that have proven to be critical for product success. One thing has become clear along the way: in a time when the creation of the product itself is cheap(er), the way the product makes you feel, the smoothness of the delivery, the way it integrates with your vision of your business and how you work and *how you want to work* is becoming the key differentiator. Testing for that is markedly more challenging than testing for the checklist; and regardless of where the AI fits in the mix (which will be a forever growing animal), it turns out after all of everything that the critical skill is the soft one, even in engineering: people who will be able to decide what's "best", and meet the visions of others in that determination, will have massive value now. Developing that judgment, at scale, about things if your domain area and about all the intersections it comes into contact with, is one of the best things you can to position yourself for the next decade.

## The part I am not going to pretend about

Thursday of last week I made this argument's honest caveat and I am not going to re-run it, but I am also not going to quietly drop it now that it is inconvenient.

Everything above is a reassurance to people who already have somewhere to stand. If you have fifteen years in a domain, the message is genuinely good: you are most of the way there and the missing piece is a disposition rather than a degree. If you are twenty-four and trying to accumulate the knowledge in the first place, "your domain expertise is the scarce asset" is not advice, it is a description of something you do not have yet.

I do not have a clean answer to that and I distrust anybody who says they do. What I would say is that the accumulation got faster, not slower -- you can now stand next to a system that will explain any part of a domain to you at three in the morning, which is not nothing, and it is a great deal more than I had. Further, I stick to my statement above: the development of decision making capacity requires nothing more the regular, concerted effort. The domain knowledge gives you the shortcut; but developing that selection skill, and then being able to show it in an interview, in day 1 on the job, in day 90 on the job the domain knowledge beginning to integrate -- that will be the difference for you, and for the discerning employer.

## What I would actually do with a Saturday

**Write down three things you would catch that a new hire would not.** Not a process document. Three specific, unglamorous things -- the number that is wrong when it is too round, the sequence that means somebody skipped a step, the customer whose ordinary request is never ordinary. This takes fifteen minutes and almost nobody has ever done it.

**Then hand one of them to an agent and watch it fail.** Give it the task, do not give it the tell, and see what it produces. It will be confident and it will be wrong in the exact place your fifteen years live. That is the most instructive half hour available to you this weekend, and it costs nothing.

**Then write the tell down and give it that too.** What you have just done is the entire job. Not prompting, and not orchestration. You located a piece of judgment that existed only in your head, made it explicit, and installed it. That is the skill the whole market is fumbling towards a title for, and you can practice it on a Saturday in your own function without a budget, a vendor or anybody's approval.

If you don't have that experience -- like the above -- take a different tack. I heard someone suggest this in a podcast this week, and it really resonated with me. Set a timer on your phone. One to go off every hour or two. When the timer goes off, screenshot what you are doing on your phone, or take a picture of what you are doing on the computer. Or if its a more complex task, take a video. Put the screenshot or the video into Claude or Codex or whatever tool you like, and ask it to make suggestions about how you could do that better. Try the suggestions. They won't all week -- guaranteed -- but if you are willing and open, they will take you down new paths that *could* lead to something really valuable. And regardless, developing the capacity to separate good opportunities from bad is all part of the argument above -- and no one ever really learns about this until they make some bad decisions. So don't sweat the details -- the effort will lead to the results. I promise.

## What this week was actually about

Five instruments failed this week, and the temptation is to read that as a story about bad tools or careless institutions. I do not think it is either.

They failed because the knowledge required to aim them correctly was sitting in somebody's head, in a building, unwritten, and nobody asked. The people being described in all these emerging job titles are not being replaced by anything. They are the template. And the version of this that starts on Monday does not begin with a tool or a platform or a course. It begins with somebody writing down what they already know, and taking action on it, which is the one part of this entire enterprise that has never needed permission.
