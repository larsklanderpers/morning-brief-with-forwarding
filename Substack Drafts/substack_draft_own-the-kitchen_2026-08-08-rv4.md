You do not need a restaurant to make dinner. You need a stove, a couple of pans, and the willingness to start.

For about two years now, using AI has mostly meant eating out every single night. You place an order with a vendor, they cook it somewhere you never see, and they hand back whatever they have decided to plate. It is convenient and often very good (though sometimes it's fit only for the trash, like all cooking), and I am not going to tell you to stop. But be clear about the deal: they pick the ingredients, they read every order you send in, and they can change the menu tomorrow without asking you. More importantly, at least to me, there is an essential difference between cooking yourself and letting someone cook for you: I am not good at cooking. And the only way you get better at something is to do it. Repeatedly. 

All week, then, I have been making the colder version of the argument for why you might want some control you can actually locate. Today, because it is a Saturday and the week got heavy (as they all seem to at the moment), I want to do the fun part: show you how you start owning the kitchen, and cooking gourmet meals for yourself. This weekend. For roughly nothing.

And I will tell you up front where this lands, because it is a Saturday and if you are here spending time with me then you deserve to know it ends somewhere good: you almost certainly already own a rung on this ladder and do not know it, the first real step costs about an hour and no money, and the whole thing is a lot more approachable than the people selling you fear on either side would like you to believe.

## It is a ladder, not a switch

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes, in both directions. One camp says sovereignty means running a frontier model on your own metal, and then does the math on what that actually costs and concludes the whole idea is a fantasy for the paranoid. The other camp says it is trivial, download a thing and you are free. They are both wrong in the same way: they think it is a switch. It is most definitely NOT a switch: rather, it is a ladder. And if you are a novice, and have no direct experience, guess what: it's a step-stool. It's that easy. And it doesn't come out half-gummy when you try it, like your first attempt at macaroni and cheese.

Regardless, the ladder, since I am mixing and matching the metaphors fast and furious. The rungs go from "the subscription you already pay for" at the bottom to "a real box humming in the corner" at the top, and the honest news is that the value is not at the top. The cost of *curiosity* -- finding out what you can and cannot do yourself -- has fallen to essentially zero. The cost of *tooling* -- actually building the thing once you know -- has not. So you climb on purpose, cheapest rung first, and you only reach for the next one when the last one has genuinely run out of room. Nobody needs to open a restaurant to learn to cook. In fact, because of the cost of opening a restaurant, most people would most definitely learn how to cook, first. Even though the cost of curiosity is very, very low, it does draw down your most precious resource, time. So learn the progressive steps, and move up the rungs, to get the best value back on your investment.

Let me walk you up the rungs.

## Rung zero: the kitchen you are already renting

Start by noticing that you don't even have to walk to the ladder and muster your courage to climb it. In fact, you are already on the ladder. If you pay twenty dollars a month for ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro, that is rung zero, and it is completely fine. Do not let anyone shame you off it. For a great many people and a great many tasks, the sane, prudent, leverage-what-you-have answer is to stay right here and get good at it.

What rung zero does not give you is any say over the kitchen. You get the vendor's model, routed the vendor's way, on the vendor's terms, and if they quietly serve you a lighter version of the dish on a busy night, you mostly cannot tell. That is the thing the rest of the ladder buys back, a little at a time. But until you are comfortable enough to know that the model isn't actually giving you what you need, and/or until the price starts to grow, stick with rung zero. And generally, probably, stick with a second tier model instead of a first -- Opus 5/4.8 or Sonnet 5 instead of Fable 5; ChatGPT 5.6 Terra instead of Sol; and so forth. You likely don't need the super-intelligence right away; I live in this stuff and I still only use Sol/Fable very occasionally, because it's just overkill for the vast majority of work.

## Rung one: a menu you can actually read

The first real step up is an aggregator -- OpenRouter is the one most people land on, at present, though by the time you read this they might be owned by Stripe which could change that calculus a bit -- and it is the highest-leverage hour you will spend on any of this. Instead of one vendor's kitchen, you get a single doorway to most of the models on the market, and, crucially, you get to *specify the order*. You can pin the exact model you want, so you know precisely what is cooking. You can turn on Zero Data Retention (ZDR), which means the provider is contractually not keeping your data around after it answers. You can control not only what you see and how you see it, but (for me at least) more importantly you can control *how and what they see of you and yours.* Crucially to this conversation -- Fable 5 at present does not support ZDR at all: it, and the other frontier "covered" models, carry a mandatory thirty-day retention window so Anthropic can watch for serious misuse -- jailbreak farming, state-sponsored espionage, extortion campaigns.[^2] Reasonable enough as safety goes, but if keeping your data at home is your whole reason for being here, that rule *alone* might convince you to steer clear of it.

This is the direct fix for the thing I complained about on Monday -- the quiet substitution, the "you did not get the model you paid for." Here you get to name the model and hold them to it. It costs pennies to a few dollars a month for normal personal use, because you pay per use instead of a flat subscription, and it takes about an hour to wire up. But, again, here is where the cost starts to accrue: it takes an hour. And if it goes wrong, it might take two, or three. So have confidence established from Rung zero, and have a *reason* for the router adoption (not just LK says this is the next step). Every step along the way here, you need to keep your eye on where the return is *for you* because you are not me or anyone else and this is very much not a one-size fits all kind of paradigm. Be ready for the investment; make it only when it will pay off for you in some way. 

One honest caveat, because, as always, I try to give you the real version and not the brochure. ZDR means the provider does not *keep* your data. It does not mean they never *see* it -- they still have to receive your order to cook it. So rung one buys you visibility and control over which kitchen and what happens to the leftovers. It does **not** yet mean the food never leaves your house. That is a real distinction, and it is exactly where the next rung comes in.

## Rung three: your own stove (yes, you can skip a number)

I am skipping past rung two -- letting someone host an open model for you, a fine middle option, especially for enterprises, but a bit weedy for this conversation -- because the rung that surprises people is this one: running a model on a computer you already own.

If you have a Mac from the last few years, you have a more capable kitchen than you think, because Apple's chips share memory in a way that lets them punch well above a normal laptop. A sixteen-gigabyte MacBook -- an ordinary one, the kind a lot of people already have on the desk -- will comfortably run a small but genuinely useful model like Qwen 9B at reading speed or better, entirely offline, using a free app like Ollama or LM Studio. A machine with a bit more memory will run flagship-class coding help on its own; Simon Willison, who benchmarks this stuff carefully, clocked a 27-billion-parameter model at a perfectly usable clip on a 24-gigabyte Mac. You can find similar PCs on Amazon that will get you there for a thousand USD or so. But the real kicker here, is if you have it *already*, then you get the next level of stuff for free on the hardware side and so it becomes effectively a *reduction* in cost from what you have now [because once it's up you can kill your subscription(s) or at least reduce them].

The point is not the numbers. The point is that at this rung, the food never leaves the house. No per-token cost, no vendor reading the order, no menu that changes overnight. A friend of mine runs exactly this kind of setup on a MacBook to power a private little assistant for his golf coaching -- drills, notes, questions -- and it costs him nothing per month and answers only to him. That is the whole idea in one homely example: not a supercomputer, a bounded, private, always-on kitchen that happens to already be sitting in your bag.

And the timing has never been better, because the ingredients just got a lot easier to buy. This month OpenAI -- the company that basically invented the phrase "closed frontier model" -- released two open-weight models it calls gpt-oss, under a genuinely permissive license, and they run in the same free local apps as everything else. The firm most associated with keeping the kitchen locked just handed a lot of people the recipe. For anyone who wanted the familiar brand but on their own hardware, that was the last excuse gone. And if you question whether there are other viable alternatives, a quick browse to [Hugging Face's model hub](https://huggingface.co/models) shows you lots that you can route to, that you can choose online, and that you can download into your own container at home. Maybe not quite frontier capacity, but as noted above, more than enough unless you are trying to solve complex space navigation equations.

## Rung four: the serious box, when you have earned it

Above the laptop sits the real hardware -- a workstation with a proper graphics card, the kind of thing that will run bigger models and drive genuinely autonomous agents on-premises. It is a thousand to a few thousand dollars (I watched a YouTube recently of a guy running four pieces of inference in his house for different purposes, and the rigs collectively cost him about $20K USD) and some ongoing fuss, and it is a wonderful rung. It is also one you should climb only when the rungs below have run out of room, because this is where the cost of tooling stops being your afternoon and starts being your credit card. Be prudent. Leverage what is already there first. The leverage is so disproportionate with these tools, you should only climb to this level if you determine you really need it (or you just like having really cool toys).

And to keep me honest about the ceiling: no, you are not running the true frontier at home. The very biggest open models -- the kind that made headlines for matching the closed labs -- are datacenter animals, well over a terabyte, and putting one "on your laptop" is a fantasy no matter what the thread promised you.[^1] But that is the restaurant, and you do not need to own a restaurant to cook a good dinner. Owning the kitchen was never about running the biggest possible thing. It was about being able to make *something* yourself, on your own terms, in a space you control.

## Where to actually go learn this

If reading about it is one thing and you would rather just do it with your hands -- which is the entire spirit of a Saturday -- there is a genuinely good, genuinely free place to start, and I want to point you at it by name.

It is called the AI Adventure, from AIDB and Superintelligent, put together by Nufar Gaspar. It is self-paced, it is free, and it is built as a set of hands-on "destinations" -- little projects, each of which has you actually build a working thing rather than watch a video about building one. New ones are dropping weekly through August. A few of them map almost eerily onto this exact week: there is one where you stand up a tiny AI-run business, which is Thursday's "build your own ladder" made literal; one where you vibe-code your first little app, which is the entry rung you make for yourself; and one where you put several models head to head and write yourself a routing card, which is Monday's "did you actually get the model you paid for" turned into a Saturday project.

Two honest notes to keep in mind: it is a true freemium: the adventure itself is free and complete, but there is a paid cohort they will offer you, and you should know that going in and take it or leave it on its merits. And the content is licensed for your own learning, so I am pointing you at their door, not reprinting their recipes here. Go, credit Nufar (from all her content I have listened to over the past year, she deserves it), have fun with it.

## The one sharp knife in the drawer

I owe you a single note of caution, and then we go back to the good part.

At the sovereign top of this ladder, the tool a lot of people will eventually reach for is a local, open-source agent -- one that does not just chat but actually goes and does things, reads your mail, runs your errands, takes instructions and acts on them. The best-known of these is an agent from Nous Research, and it is a genuinely useful piece of software. It is also, and I am not being dramatic here, the exact same tool an attacker ran unattended inside a national finance ministry a couple of weeks ago, which I wrote about on Monday.

Nothing was wrong with the tool. What the attacker did was flip one setting -- a documented flag, `--yolo`, which turns off the little moments where the agent stops and asks a human before it does something irreversible -- and then let it loose to work the network alone. And that, right there, is the whole point of the serious version of this argument, rendered as one line in a config file. The approval prompt is the guardrail. The flag that turns it off is how you give the kitchen away. So by all means climb toward running your own agents -- but treat that particular rung as the sharp knife in the drawer: enormously useful, worth owning, and not the first thing you hand a beginner. Keep the prompts on. Cook with the guard up. Make a casserole a few times before you try a souffle.

## So, go cook

That is the ladder. Rung zero is the subscription you already have and there is no shame in it. Rung one is an hour or so and a few dollars and it hands you back a menu you can read. Rung three is very possibly already sitting in your bag or your home office, and equates to cooking privately for free. And the top rungs are there when you have earned them, sharp knives and all, but capable of delivering a full dinner party with ease.

You do not have to climb the whole thing. You do not have to climb it today. But the cost of finding out what you can do in your own kitchen has fallen so low, and the ingredients have never been easier to get, and there is a free, friendly, hands-on place to start that is open right now. Don't waste the opportunity that presents, even if you stop on the zero rung because you determine it's enough.

This weekend, now, today, before the week gets heavy again tomorrow, go make something small and entirely yours. Worst case, you learn where your own walls are. Best case, you never quite look at eating out the same way again.
