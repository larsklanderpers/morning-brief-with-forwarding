If you have ever watched a good handler work a dog, you have seen the whole argument I want to make today, so let me start there before I ruin it with abstraction.

The handler does not do the dog's job. That would be absurd; the dog is faster, has a better nose, and can go places the handler cannot. But the handler does not let the dog run loose either. The handler stays in it: reading the field, giving the next instruction, correcting the small mistakes before they become big ones, precisely because the dog is capable enough to get into real trouble on its own. The skill is not strength and it is not speed. The skill is staying engaged with something that could do the work without you, and refusing to check out. My family got a dog a few years ago now, at the urging of one of my sons, and I was thinking about how hard it was to raise and train an indoor dog (just for regular use) and was doing a fair bit of reading and study at the time. An insight then, which I am paraphrasing here, really stuck with me over time: "When people don't learn how to work with the dog, chaos happens. When they do, the dog and the person are happier, everything goes more smoothly, and a solid pairing can accomplish far more than either could alone."

That is the entire question of AI at work in 2026, and almost nobody is framing it correctly. We keep talking about tools. Who has access, who got the license, who is on the good model and who is stuck on the free tier. And access matters, a lot, and I don't want to wave that by. But that's a far bigger political and socio-economic question than I am willing to take on today. 

Putting base access aside, once you have access, the tools are nearly everywhere now, and the gap between the people getting enormous value out of them and the people getting almost none has stopped tracking access at all. It tracks something else. You will hear AI-pilled talking heads say "the difference is context." And I think that's true -- I just think it cuts both ways. Context for the AI can only fully be established by the human involved. So success, it seems, then tracks on whether the person stayed in the loop, or handed over the leash and walked away. Now, not to get into the weeds too deeply, but this is the basic concept behind loop engineering, graph engineering, and other industry specific phrases -- the leverage is what gives you the power, but the lever still needs someone to pull on it, in the direction it needs to go, before the physics work on your side. And there is a pun in that name worth pulling out on purpose: loop engineering is really the art of getting the human *out* of the loop, out of typing each prompt, which only works while a person is still deciding where the loop points and what "done" means. You are not in the loop anymore. You are the one who drew it.

Yesterday I left you on a switch. A person inside a government finance ministry turned off the one approval prompt standing between an autonomous agent and everything it could reach, because the prompt was slowing him down. That is the loud version of handing over the leash: one human, one flag, one very bad afternoon. It is the effective equivalent of taking your dog off the leash on a busy street because you just don't have the energy to deal with her today. When she has never been off the leash before and doesn't have comfort around what's ok and what's not. It's chaos in a bundle, and the bundle part is the important part because you don't see the chaos right away, it leaks out of the edges as you go.

The quiet version, on the other hand, needs no command-line switch, and I will guarantee you, sight unseen, that if your company has done any level of AI adoption at all, it is running silently *somewhere* in your building right now. It is every capable person who has stopped reading what the machine hands back, stopped correcting it, stopped being the judgment in the loop -- not because they flipped anything, but because nobody built their job so that staying awake was the point. Monday was about the walls that did not hold. Today is about the one wall that was always supposed to be a person, and what it takes to keep them standing there.

## Two kinds of user

David Brooks wrote a piece in The Atlantic earlier this summer that has been rattling around in my head ever since, and I am going to lean on it, because he named the thing more cleanly than I have managed to. His argument is that in the age of AI, what will separate people *and their success with the new tools* is "not how smart they are but their relationship to mental effort." Not raw intelligence. Not even technical skill. Their relationship to the actual effort of thinking. 

Brooks sees the population splitting into what he calls, a little alarmingly, "extreme cognitive polarization." On one side, the people who "enjoy thinking hard," who reach for AI the way you would reach for a brilliant librarian, to go further into a problem than they could alone. On the other, the people who reach for it to stop thinking, to hand the whole task across and take back a finished answer they never interrogate. 

For all of us, Google search has performed this function for some years now. And you can tell exactly the seam he is discussing from looking at who you know who uses search. Who goes beyond page 1 of the responses? Who drills through into Wikipedia? Who disappears down a rabbit hole for 15 minutes because they decided that wanted to understand something about an idle question? And who reads the AI summary at the top. 

In both examples -- AI as a lever for changing outcomes, search as a means for accessing the world's knowledge from your pocket -- it's the same tool. Opposite outcomes. And the line between the two groups is not who is cleverer. It is who stayed engaged. And as any Tik Toker or social media influencer will tell you, engagement is *everything* in our modern world. 

Here is the sentence of his I would tape to the wall, because it is the one that turns a cultural observation into a management problem: the people who thrive, Brooks says, "want to find ways to use AI to increase their agency, rather than diminish it." Increase their agency. Not their output, not their speed. Their agency, their grip on the work, their standing as the person who still owns the judgment.

Now hold that next to something I keep coming back to. Those two-to-five humans supervising a factory of agents are the control layer; the whole system is held up by their judgment. Brooks is describing, at the level of the individual mind, exactly the thing I was describing at the level of the org chart. The judgment is the asset. And you can deploy AI in a way that sharpens it, or in a way that quietly retires it, and that is a choice somebody makes, in the deployment of every AI system.

## The skill is management, and it was always management

The good news, and there is some (there is always *some*, you just might have to look really, really hard for it), is that the skill this requires is not exotic. Ethan Mollick has been making this case all year, and his line for it is that management is the new superpower. Not prompt wizardry. Not a secret phrase that unlocks the model. The ability to explain what you want, give useful feedback, and judge whether the work is any good. And then refine that. And refine it again. And redirect when you get it wrong. And recognize that getting it wrong will happen, and that that is ok, and being infallible is not a benefit, it's a cost regime that comes due incrementally.

"If you can explain what you need, give effective feedback, and design ways of evaluating work," Mollick writes, "you are going to be able to work with agents." The skills we spent years dismissing as soft, he says, "turned out to be the hard ones." Working with an agent is less like operating software and more like managing a person, which means the people who already know how to manage, who know what good looks like and can say so clearly, already hold the scarce skill. It is management 101, pointed at a very fast, very literal new hire who never gets tired and never pushes back.

I would put one sharp edge on Mollick's point, because it changes who owns this problem. If the core skill is management, then "tooling people up" is not an IT rollout. It is not a procurement exercise that ends when everyone has a login. It is a management and, frankly, an HR question, because what you are actually distributing is not software. You are distributing a new way of working that either keeps your people in the loop or lulls them out of it, and no license key decides which.

## Agency is not a nicety. It is the output.

I want to close the loop with the person who runs the machine at real scale, because it is easy to hear all this as a soft plea to be more thoughtful, and it is not soft at all. Elizabeth Stone, who is the Chief Product and Technology Officer at Netflix, was on Lenny's Podcast a couple of weeks ago, and she said the quiet part with a clarity I appreciated. "It can be that an agent wrote the code," she said, "but it doesn't make people not have the responsibility."

That is the whole thing in one sentence. The agent can do the work. The accountability does not transfer with it. And the reason that matters is not moral, it is operational: the moment you deploy these tools so that a person no longer feels they own the outcome, you do not get a happier, freer worker. You get a checked-out one, and you get worse work, because the judgment that was supposed to catch the mistakes has left the building along with the sense of responsibility. Strip the agency out and you pay for it twice, in the quality of the work and in the person who used to guarantee it. Keep the agency in, and the same tool that could have sedated your best people instead makes them faster without making them absent.

None of this is expensive, which is the part that should bother you if your organization is getting it wrong. It costs nothing to deploy these tools in a way that keeps people accountable for what comes out. It costs nothing to tell a team "the agent drafts, you own it" instead of "the agent handles it now." The disengaged version and the engaged version carry the same license fee. One of them just quietly writes off the most valuable thing you employ.

## So, tomorrow

The tools on every desk in your building are, more or less, identical to the tools on your competitor's desks. That is not where the difference lives anymore. The difference is whether the person at the desk is still awake, still holding the leash, still the one who owns the answer, or whether somebody deployed the tool in a way that let them drift off with the work still running.

And that, unlike almost everything I write about, is not a decision made in Washington or a lab or a boardroom you will never sit in. It is made by whoever designs how the work actually gets done, which on a good day is you. Brooks thinks the coming divide is a matter of temperament, of who happens to love mental effort and who does not. I think that is too fatalistic. Temperament is real, but engagement is also something you can build the conditions for, or destroy the conditions for, in how you hand the tools out. In fact, having three kids who all grew up in the social media era, I would argue that engagement not only can offset temperament, it can drive the discipline it requires to maintain it. All three of my kids love Youtube, which I used to think was just because it was easy entertainment, but I have since learned it is because they can go deep on just about anything, simply by pursuing the engagement where it takes them. Most social media makes knowledge and experience more shallow, and AI tools can do this too: but for those that are engaged, and committed, and want to make it more, social media like Youtube empowers that choice. I can't say enough -- agency will be what makes the difference, in adoption, in usage, in employee satisfaction, and in enterprise performance. Building the walls to enable the engagement in ways that don't put you and yours at risk is the challenge, and it's a big one. But it starts today.

Wednesday I will get back to the harder, colder version of the week's question, where control lives when it is not routing through a vendor you do not control. But the version that is actually in your hands is this one, and it is worth saying before the week gets heavy again: *you cannot outsource your way to an advantage everyone else can buy off the same shelf*. What you can do is keep your people in the loop on purpose, and let the ones who love the work do more of it. That is not a soft benefit. On a long enough timeline, it is the only one left.
