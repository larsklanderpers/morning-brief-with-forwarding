New South Wales now requires every one of its agencies to register every AI system it uses, before it uses it. It is the most serious piece of AI governance any Australian government has actually shipped.

It also contains one sentence, about forty words long, that keeps a fast-growing category of government AI off that register. I wrote about that sentence a month ago and did not publish it. In the meantime the policy went through a revision, the version number moved, the file was republished, and the sentence came back word for word identical. That is the part I want your Monday for, because a clause that survives an edit is not an oversight. Somebody looked at it and left it.

Think about what happens when you move house. You leave a forwarding address, and the forwarding address is a real instrument. The post office honors it, the mail goes where the card says, and nobody in the chain is doing anything wrong. The whole arrangement rests on one assumption that nobody checks, which is that somebody is still at the other end. If the address on the card is an office that got renamed, or a suite that was subdivided, or a company that was folded into another one two years ago, the redirect still works perfectly. The mail arrives exactly where you told it to go. It just does not arrive anywhere useful, and you will not find out until something in it mattered.

That is the shape of the thing I want to show you, and it is not really a New South Wales story. Some version of this sentence is sitting in the AI inventory your own organization is standing up right now.

## What New South Wales actually built

Give them the credit first, because they have earned more of it than they are going to get.

The NSW AI Operational Policy is issued under Circular DCS-2026-02, which is active and carries an issue date of 30 July 2026, and it replaces both the 2024 circular and the NSW AI Ethics Policy. It binds every agency in the NSW Government sector. Four hard obligations sit at the center of it. Designate an Accountable Official at senior executive level and notify NSW GovAI within three months. Register AI use cases before use. Run the assessment framework where registration tells you to. File an annual attestation by the thirty-first of October each year, with the first reporting date in 2027.

And then there are three clauses in that document I did not expect a state government to write down.

The first: agencies must ensure AI dependencies supporting critical services have tested business continuity plans that can be activated when required. That is a government refusing, in writing, to become dependent on a vendor it cannot replace.

The second: records must be managed as State records in accordance with the *State Records Act 1998*. You cannot rent that audit trail. It cannot live only inside the provider's platform, and if it goes missing that is a reportable event.

The third is the one I would print out and pin up. *Treat all inputs to and outputs of AI systems as potentially shared with the provider.* Stated flat, as policy, by a government. Everything I have been writing about inference sovereignty arriving not as infrastructure spend but as an acceptable-use clause that costs nothing and bites the week it is written.

This is not a box-ticking document. Somebody thought hard about it.

## The forty words

Here is the sentence, verbatim.

*"The requirement to register AI use cases does not apply to third-party suppliers providing services to the NSW Government where they use AI as part of those services. These arrangements are managed through procurement, contracting, and supplier assurance processes."*

Read the second half again, because the second half is the whole piece. The clause does not say supplier AI is out of scope. It says supplier AI is somebody else's filing. It is a forwarding address.

And the register is not a filing cabinet. It is the artifact the entire regime is built on. It is what the Office for AI reports against and what the annual attestation certifies. Draw the scope of the register and you have drawn the scope of everything downstream of it.

Two versions of this policy have now been published. The first carries the version line *"Final version 1: July 2026."* The one currently linked from the live policy page carries *"Final version 1.1 - 30 July 2026,"* which is the date the circular took effect, and it is served under a September filename. I have not seen a change log and I am not going to tell you what moved between the two, because I do not know and neither does anybody else outside the department. What I can tell you is that I checked the forty words in both files and they are identical, down to the punctuation.

So this is not a first-draft artifact that a second pass would have caught. It went past a second pass.

## Now follow the forwarding address

Here is where I stopped writing this piece in August and where it got interesting in September.

The clause says these arrangements are managed through procurement, contracting and supplier assurance. Fine. So I went and read procurement.

**The AI procurement essentials guidance is the document that actually tells a NSW buying team how to handle a supplier delivering an outcome with AI inside it.** It names that exact procurement type: *"Platform, service or outcome-based procurement involving AI: Contracting a supplier to deliver a specific outcome or deliverable, where the supplier utilises generative AI technologies as part of the service provision."* That is the excluded category, described precisely, in the place the exclusion sends it. The guidance is real and it is not empty.

It also carries a date stamp on the page that reads **updated 6 May 2026**. That is four months ago and it is before the Operational Policy existed. Under its own heading of related NSW policies and frameworks it lists the AI Ethics Policy, which is the policy the Operational Policy replaced in terms. It also says, twice, that further guidance is coming: guidance *"will be developed for responsible AI officers, and buyers,"* and it refers to *"the upcoming AI Procurement Guidance."* Four months on, still upcoming.

**Behind that sits the Procurement Policy Framework**, which is the instrument with real force here, mandatory under the *Public Works and Procurement Act 1912*. Its published update history ends on 13 December 2024. The AI entry in that update history names Circular DCS-2024-04, which is the circular that was archived and replaced this July. Twenty-one months, and the framework that carries the statutory weight still points at a document that no longer exists.

**And then the one that genuinely surprised me.** The landing page for the AI Assessment Framework, which is the mandatory assessment tool the whole regime runs on, still describes its own mandate this way: all NSW government use of AI *"must apply the NSW AI Assessment Framework (AIAF) in line with the NSW mandate under Circular DCS-2024-04 to use both the AI Ethics Policy and the AIAF."* The archived circular and the replaced policy, cited as live authority, on the page hosting the tool. I first checked that page on 6 August. I checked it again on 5 September and it says the same thing.

Four properties, one government. Exactly one of them is current, and it is the one with the exclusion in it.

## The narrower claim, which is the true one

The obvious version of this argument is wrong, and I would rather say so than have a procurement officer take the piece apart in the comments, which they would be entitled to do.

Supplier AI is not unregulated in New South Wales. It is unregistered, and the paperwork it is redirected into is dated. Those are different sentences from "nobody covers vendor AI," and the difference is the piece.

Vendor AI does get looked at. It gets looked at once, at the moment of purchase, by a track whose own governing documents were written for the previous regime, and then it never enters the central inventory or the annual attestation. The defensible claim is about visibility and the reporting line built on it, not about coverage, and I want to be held to the narrow one.

I should also say plainly that I have been here before. Earlier this year I ran two corrections in this newsletter on a different Australian instrument, APRA's CPS 230, because I wrote about what a standard required before I had read the standard properly. The argument I made after those corrections, that an instrument which never mentions AI can reach further than one written for it, is an argument I have now published twice and I am not going to run it a third time. I raise it only to be clear about which kind of claim this is. Everything above is a description of what four documents say today, with the dates they say it on, and every one of them is a page you can open yourself in less time than it took to read this section.

## What to actually do with this

Three things, and none of them is a meeting.

**Find your own forty words and read them as a routing instruction, not as an exclusion.** Every AI inventory has a scope sentence. It was written in a working group, usually by one person, usually in a single line nobody escalated. Do not ask whether the scope is broad enough. Ask where the sentence *sends* the things it leaves out, because it almost always names a destination rather than a void.

**Then go to that destination and check the date on it.** This is the step nobody takes, and it is the cheapest thing in this piece. Whatever your scope sentence redirects to, third-party risk, procurement, vendor assurance, the standard contract terms, open the actual document and look at when it was last revised and what it cites as its authority. A redirect into a process that has not been updated since before your AI policy existed is not coverage. It is a forwarding address to an office that moved.

**Then find out who owns the reconciliation, and accept that the honest answer is probably nobody.** The register has an owner. The procurement framework has an owner. The scope sentence connecting them has no owner at all, because it is one line inside a document that belongs to the first of those two. That gap is not a scandal and nobody is hiding it. It is just genuinely nobody's job, and things that are nobody's job do not get done in the year they stop being true.

## The objection, and it is a fair one

**"You are describing normal government website lag, and dressing it up as a governance finding."**

Partly, and I will give that full weight rather than half. Web properties trail policy everywhere, in every jurisdiction, and a stale citation on a landing page is not evidence that anyone is doing their job badly. The people who wrote the Operational Policy do not own the procurement pages and cannot edit them.

Here is why I still think it matters. In an ordinary case, a stale page is an inconvenience: somebody reads the wrong version, notices, and finds the right one. This is not the ordinary case, because a specific clause in the current policy performs a redirect *into* those pages, and it does so for the fastest-growing category of AI in government. The staleness stops being background lag at the moment an operative instrument routes a live obligation through it. And the tell is the annual attestation. On 31 October 2027 an Accountable Official signs that their agency has complied. Everything in the register is in scope of that signature. Everything the forty words redirected is in scope of a framework last revised in December 2024. Both of those things will be true on the same page, over the same signature.

## What this is really about

Every organization standing up an AI inventory this year is making the same scoping choice, and almost all of them are making it the same way. List the models we run. List the tools we licensed. Vendor AI arriving inside a service we bought goes to third-party risk, where it becomes a questionnaire at onboarding and then, functionally, nothing. The choice does not feel like a governance decision when it is made, because it reads as an administrative tidy-up: this belongs over there, with the people who handle that.

New South Wales did the hard thinking, wrote it all down, and then wrote forty more words deciding how much of the problem the register would ever be able to see. Those forty words were the cheapest thing in the document to draft. They were cheap because they did not decide anything, they only pointed somewhere, and pointing somewhere always feels free. It is free right up until somebody walks to the address on the card.
