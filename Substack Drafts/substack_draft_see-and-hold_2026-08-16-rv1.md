Australia is running two AI governance instruments at once, and they fail in exactly opposite directions.

One of them can put a chief executive under summons, compel the documents, and jail somebody for three months if they refuse to answer. When it reports, its findings will bind nobody. The other cannot compel anyone to do anything at all, and it is the one that actually binds -- except that it was scoped so that most of the AI in the buildings it governs falls outside it.

Six days ago the Premier of South Australia announced the first, a royal commission into artificial intelligence. I wrote about the second on Wednesday. Putting them side by side is the most useful thing I have done all week, because between them they explain every one of the five failures this page has spent the week describing.

## The reading I started with, and why it is not enough

The obvious read on the week is *direction*. Five instruments, all of them aimed with reasonable care, every one of them pointed at something adjacent to the actual problem.

That reading is true. It is also Monday's piece, and a capstone that restates Monday has not synthesized anything -- it has just said it again with more confidence and a better view.

So here is the one underneath it. Every narrow instrument this week was the cheap one.

Checking who somebody is costs less than checking what they intend, so the control checked identity. Announcing that you use AI costs less than producing a list of which tasks moved, so the announcement is what got made. A record of the systems you operate costs less than a record of the systems you rely on, and New South Wales priced that difference in actual dollars -- a high-risk internal use case buys you a privacy impact assessment, a human rights impact assessment, a cybersecurity review and a committee referral, while the same capability bought from a supplier costs one triage at procurement. Banning a device costs less than building the governance layer that would let you allow it conditionally. And an understanding written in a room and shown to nobody costs less than a rule published, argued over, and made to survive contact with people who disagree.

Five days, five instruments, and in every case the version that got built was the one that cost less to build.

That is a good argument and I believed it on Thursday. Then South Australia went and spent three million dollars to disprove it.

## The counter-example

On 10 August, Premier Peter Malinauskas announced Australia's first royal commission into artificial intelligence.

I want to be careful with the framing, because the version traveling around has already grown in the telling. It is Australia's first, not the world's -- royal commissions are a Westminster instrument and only a handful of countries have them, so "nobody has done this before" is an argument from absence and it is misleading in substance. The European Union ran a multi-year legislative process. The United States had a national security commission. Australia's own Senate select committee reported in 2024. Plenty of people have asked the question. South Australia is the first to ask it with this particular instrument.

Which is the interesting part, because of what the instrument is.

A royal commission under the *Royal Commissions Act 1917* is the most coercive investigative power a state government has. Failing to attend on summons is an offence. Refusing to be sworn is an offence. Refusing to produce a document is an offence. Refusing to answer is an offence, and the chairman can commit a person to jail for up to three months for it. Refusals on separate days count as separate offences, so the exposure compounds. The commission is not bound by the rules of evidence. If it wants to know what is running inside a company's systems, it can require somebody to come and say so under oath, with the alternative being a custodial sentence.

Nothing else in Australian AI governance comes within a hundred miles of that. It is, by an enormous margin, the most powerful thing anybody has pointed at this problem.

Three commissioners, recruited by international search, none of them named yet. About three million dollars. They start work in October, and the report is due on 1 July 2027. The terms of reference are four to six weeks away, which means that at the time I am writing this nobody -- including the government that called it -- knows precisely what it will examine. There is no gazetted name yet either, because the Letters Patent have not been issued.

And here is the thing. It investigates and it recommends. It does not prosecute. Its findings bind nobody.

South Australia knows this better than most, because its own 2016 royal commission into the nuclear fuel cycle produced a central recommendation that was subsequently abandoned. The state has direct experience of running its most powerful instrument to completion and then declining to act on the answer.

It is also reporting into something that does not exist yet. In July the Prime Minister said at the University of Sydney that Australia would legislate mandatory national AI standards, with a new Office of AI inside his own department to draft them, obligations on data centres for power and water consumption, and the strongest copyright protection available for Australian creators. Legislation is expected in parliament early in 2027. Note the tense on all of that: it is announced policy with a drafting body attached. Not a bill. Not an exposure draft. Not law. The legal trackers still record Australia as having no wide-reaching AI statute, and they are right to. So South Australia reports on 1 July 2027, roughly alongside a federal bill that may or may not have entered parliament, on terms of reference that will not be written for another month.

So the most expensive, most coercive, most serious instrument available -- one that can compel testimony on pain of imprisonment -- will, in the ordinary course, produce a document that everybody reads and nobody has to follow.

Cost is not the variable. I had the wrong cause.

## The two dimensions

Here is what I think the actual variable is, and I want to name it plainly because it took me the whole week to see it and it makes every day of it legible at once.

Every governance instrument has two separate properties, and we habitually talk about them as if they were one.

**The first is how far it can see.** What falls inside its scope. Which systems, which people, which conduct, which suppliers. This is the thing Wednesday's piece was about, and Thursday's from the other side, and it is the dimension this page has been circling for a month.

**The second is how hard it can hold.** What happens when it finds something. Whether it can compel, bind, fine, revoke, or merely observe and write it down.

Those two are independent. That is the entire point and it is the thing I had wrong. An instrument can have enormous reach and no grip. It can have absolute grip over a tiny patch. Naming the pair is what turns the week from five bad-luck stories into one pattern.

Run the week through it.

**Monday.** The Google agent authorization control had grip -- it made a real decision and enforced it, and when it said yes the workflow ran. Its sight extended exactly as far as *who is asking*, and no further. An injected collaborator satisfied the identity check honestly, because it really was the identity it claimed. Perfect grip, six inches of vision.

**Tuesday.** The corporate AI announcement has neither. It sees nothing, because it does not enumerate anything. It holds nothing, because it commits to nothing. That is not an instrument at all, which is why the rehire is the only evidence it ever produces. I said that week's diagnostic was whether an organization can name which tasks moved and which judgment calls stayed with a person, and the reason that diagnostic works is that naming is the minimum act of *seeing*. Without it there is nothing for a grip to close on.

**Wednesday.** The New South Wales AI Operational Policy has real grip. An accountable official, a mandatory assessment before use, a five-business-day referral for high and critical results, and an annual attestation signed by that official and approved by the agency head. Those are teeth in a government context. And the sight is bounded by a single forty-word exclusion: registration does not apply to third-party suppliers using AI as part of the services they provide. The instrument binds hard and looks at only what the agency runs itself. Grip without sight.

**Thursday.** A ban has the hardest grip available and the crudest sight. It cannot distinguish the surgeon's recording from the stranger's, which is why the proposal needed carve-outs, and the carve-outs are the admission: enforcing them requires precisely the governance layer that does not exist. A ban is what you reach for when the seeing apparatus was never built.

**Friday.** Two at once, from opposite ends. The classifier reviewing agent actions sees an enormous amount -- every tool call, all day, without fatigue -- and holds nothing beyond the session it is in. The deny rules in managed settings hold absolutely, and neither the classifier nor the user can override them, and they see only exactly what somebody wrote into them, which in most organizations is nothing at all. And in the second half: a framework completed on time, on the deadline, and withheld. Perfect grip, and a sight radius that stops at the room it was written in.

**And South Australia.** The widest sight anybody has built -- compulsory attendance, compulsory production, three months in jail for refusing, unbound by the rules of evidence -- attached to no grip whatsoever.

Six instruments. Not one of them has both.

## The pair worth holding in your head

Set South Australia next to New South Wales, because between them they cover the entire space.

South Australia can see anything it wants and hold nothing. New South Wales holds its agencies to a real, dated, signed obligation and cannot see what its suppliers are running. One of them can compel a witness into a chair and cannot compel an outcome. The other compels the outcome and cannot look past the boundary of what the agency operates itself.

Same country. Same year. Same technology. Two governments, both acting in good faith, both producing something genuinely useful, and neither of them producing an instrument that both sees the problem and does anything about it.

And notice what that does to the usual complaint. The standard line about AI regulation is that governments are moving too slowly, or not spending enough, or do not understand the technology. None of those explain this. South Australia moved fast and spent real money on the most serious instrument it has. New South Wales produced a policy with a level of operational sophistication I did not expect -- continuity plans for AI dependencies on critical services, decision records kept as State records, a standing requirement to treat every input and output as data the supplier can read. That is a document written by people who have run production systems. Neither of them is careless and neither of them is slow.

They just each built one dimension.

## The one in this country that has both, and it is not new

There is an instrument in Australia that gets both dimensions right, and it is one I reached for on Wednesday for a different reason, so I want to pick it up deliberately rather than have it look like an accident.

The AUSTRAC obligation does not attach to a technology. It attaches to a designated service. If you provide the service, the obligation follows you, and it does not matter what you built the service out of, who owns the equipment, or whether the thing performing it was invented decades after the rules were drafted. On Wednesday I used that to make a point about scope. Today the point is the other half: **AUSTRAC can also act.** Reporting obligations, enforcement powers, penalties with real numbers on them, and a supervisory relationship that does not stop when the annual paperwork is filed.

Sight by function. Grip by statute. Both.

And it is worth being precise about why that combination happened, because it was not foresight about artificial intelligence. Nobody drafting the anti-money-laundering regime was thinking about agents. They scoped by *what is being done and who relies on it* because that is the only way to write a rule about money that survives people inventing new ways to move it. The technology-agnostic scoping was a defense against financial innovation, and it turns out to work against every other kind. **An instrument scoped by function ages differently from one scoped by technology, and that is the closest thing to a design principle this whole week produced.**

Which is a genuinely useful thing for those of us who work in and around clubs, gaming and financial services, because it means the good version is not hypothetical and it is not overseas. We are already regulated by one. Most of us complain about it, and I include myself.

## Which is where I have to be honest, because the cheap instrument is not always the worse one

If I stopped here this would be four days of the same complaint with a royal commission bolted on, and I would deserve the email.

Friday supplied the counter-argument and I am not going to bury it.

Anthropic published a study of 1,053 paid testers in which human reviewers caught a dangerous command 143 times out of 1,053 -- 13.6 per cent -- while its classifier blocked 937 of the same 1,053, or 89 per cent. Both figures are the vendor's own, and the arms of that comparison are not the same test: the humans met a single injected prompt partway through a session, in a study environment rather than their own codebases, knowing they were being evaluated, with nothing at stake if they got it wrong. The classifier was evaluated separately against the same command set. I wrote it up on Friday as a floor on human attention rather than a measurement of it, and I will say again that even if you generously triple the number for real practitioners in their own repositories with real consequences, you land somewhere around forty per cent and the conclusion does not move.

Here is why that matters today. **A classifier call costs a fraction of a cent and runs on every action, all day, without getting tired. A human approver costs a salary, and on the only evidence anybody has published was catching one dangerous command in seven.** The cheap instrument is not merely cheaper. On this evidence it is also better than the expensive one it replaced.

So the failure this week is not thrift. Nobody did anything wrong by choosing the cheaper control. Cheap controls that see widely are the best thing in the entire stack, and the deny rule -- free, absolute, overridable by nobody -- is the cheapest control in the whole arrangement and the one almost nobody has written.

The failure is narrower and more specific than being cheap. It is that when somebody chose the cheap instrument, they took the reduction in *sight* as an unavoidable part of the bargain, when it very often was not. Identity checking was cheap because identity is easy to verify. Nobody sat down and decided that intent was out of scope. It fell out of scope, and then the decision was made, and by the time anybody noticed there was a control in place and it had a green light on it and nobody wanted to reopen the question.

## What Saturday was about, and why it belongs in this argument

I spent yesterday on the constructive half of this and I do not want it to read as a separate essay.

Every one of these instruments was scoped by a competent person who had never stood in the room the thing was meant to protect. That is not an insult, it is a description of how organizations allocate work. The person who knows that suppliers now ship agents inside product updates is in the building. They were not in the meeting where the definition got written, because writing definitions is a governance job and knowing what actually goes wrong is an operations job, and those are different floors.

Which makes the scarce input not money, and not technology, and not regulatory sophistication. It is somebody with enough domain knowledge to say *that scope will not see the thing that actually breaks*, in the room, at the time the scope is being set, before it hardens.

Yesterday I called that judgment, and noted that the people furthest out in front of this have started calling it taste. Today's version is more mechanical and I think more useful: **the sight radius of any instrument is set by the domain knowledge of whoever was in the room when it was drafted.** That is the whole mechanism. It is why the workplace surveillance statute points employer-to-employee, why the inventory stops at what the agency operates, why the authorization check read the name on the badge and nothing else. Nobody made a mistake. The room just did not contain the person who would have asked the next question.

## Where I would push back on myself

The sharpest reader I have is going to fire back something like this, and it is a good objection: *you have just described the ordinary condition of all law and all controls. Instruments have always been narrower than the problems they address. The tax code does not see everything. Fire regulations do not prevent every fire. Welcome to governance.*

That is fair and I want to concede most of it. The see/hold gap is not new and I am not claiming it is a novel discovery about regulation. Every instrument humans have ever built has been narrower than the thing it was built for.

What is different is the rate at which the gap widens after the instrument is finished.

A fire regulation written in 1998 is worth slightly less every year, because buildings change slowly. A recording statute written in 1998 -- and Western Australia's is from that year, with Victoria's from 1999 and New South Wales' from 2007 -- was written for a world in which the recording device was a thing a person deliberately produced and pointed. It is not that those statutes were badly drafted. It is that the gap between what they can see and what is happening opened faster than any drafting process can close, and there is no mechanism anywhere in the arrangement that notices the gap has opened.

That is the actual claim, and it is narrower than "regulation is broken." **An instrument's sight radius is fixed on the day it is written, and the thing it is looking at is now moving faster than the revision cycle.** An agent inventory written eighteen months ago was scoped for a world where agents did not arrive inside supplier product updates. It is doing exactly what it was built to do. It is just doing it to a world that no longer exists, and it will keep reporting compliance the whole time.

The second objection is more uncomfortable and I will take it too: *this is a diagnostic, not a fix. You have told me how to see the problem and not what to do about it.* Partly guilty. What follows is the closest I can get to prescriptive, and it is deliberately small, because the honest version of this is not a program.

## What I would actually do this week

**Ask what your control can see, separately from what it can do.** Take one control you rely on -- an approval workflow, an access review, an inventory, a vendor questionnaire -- and answer two questions about it in writing rather than one. What is inside its scope? And what happens when it finds something? Almost every control document I have ever read answers the second question at length and the first in a single sentence that nobody has revisited since it was drafted. The single sentence is the one that decides everything.

**Then find its date, and ask what changed after it.** Not the review date. The date the scope was actually written. If that date is before agents started arriving inside other people's products, the instrument is scoped for a world that has moved, and it will keep passing its own audits the entire time it is failing. This takes about ten minutes and it is the highest-yield ten minutes available to most of us right now.

**Then ask the price question properly, in three parts.** What did this control cost to build? Where is its power actually pointed? And what would the version pointed at the right thing have cost? That third question is the one nobody asks, and it is the only one that produces a decision. Sometimes the answer is that the wider instrument costs ten times as much and is genuinely not worth it -- that is a legitimate answer and I would rather somebody reach it deliberately than arrive at the narrow version by default. What is not acceptable is not knowing. Wednesday's argument was that the compliance your suppliers do on your behalf is something a person selected rather than something circumstances handed you. This is the same claim generalized: **the sight radius of every control you own was chosen by somebody, and in most organizations nobody wrote down that a choice was being made.**

**And write the deny rule.** This is the one concrete thing from Friday that costs nothing. In the most widely used agentic coding tool in the world there is a category of rule that neither the classifier nor the user can override, that ships free, that is evaluated before anything else in the stack, and that almost nobody has configured. It is the only control in the arrangement that the customer holds outright rather than renting from a vendor. Grip without sight is still a great deal better than neither, and it is sitting there unclaimed.

## What this week actually said

Five instruments failed between Monday and Friday and a sixth was announced in South Australia that will not fail so much as decline to conclude, and the common property is not incompetence, cost, or speed. Each of them was built strong in one dimension and left near-blind in the other, and in every case the choice was made by someone who did not know they were making it.

The version of this that starts tomorrow is not a call for better regulation, because I do not think that is coming quickly and I would not build a plan around it. It is a much smaller and more available thing. Look at whatever you rely on to catch the problem, and establish -- in writing, with a date on it -- how far it can see and how hard it can hold. If it turns out to be strong in one and empty in the other, you have not found a failure. You have found the ordinary condition of every control in your building, and the only meaningful difference between organizations now is whether somebody bothered to look.

Next week I want to take that somewhere more constructive, because there is a version of this that is not about instruments other people wrote. The argument I have been circling for three weeks is that governance is something you build rather than something you receive, and that a guardrail you cannot point at in a system diagram is not a guardrail at all. This week was the case for why that matters. Next week is the part where we build it.
