In Augusta Township, Michigan, roughly nine in ten voters who turned out in August overturned their own board's rezoning for a billion-dollar data center. Then residents filed to recall all seven members of the board.

That is what AI politics looks like in 2026 in the places where people actually get to vote on it. It does not look very much like the argument on television.

Last Tuesday, in the middle of a piece about Washington, I wrote that the question of whether data centers and AI are the root cause of inflation, "whether that is fair and accurate or not, is a topic for another day." This is the other day. And I am going to take it a bit wider than inflation, because once you start pulling on the data center thread, it turns out to be attached to the midterms, to the power bill, to the county zoning board, and, somewhat unexpectedly, to a camera on a pole at the end of your street.

This is a Tuesday piece, so it is lighter on the machinery and heavier on opinion than the weekday ones. The short version is this: AI is argued globally and voted on locally, and those are now two completely different arguments with two completely different scoreboards. Most of the coverage is watching the wrong one.

## The tower behind the school

If you are old enough to remember the late nineties, you remember the cell tower fights.

The national conversation at the time was about the wireless revolution: spectrum auctions, the death of the landline, the phone in every pocket. Spectrum auctions did not fill many public meetings. What people turned up for was the tower, the specific one, going in behind the elementary school, or on top of the church steeple, or on the ridge you could see from your back deck. The global machine was argued in Washington and in boardrooms. The piece of it that needed a hundred feet of land and a concrete pad was argued in a county building, by people who lived within sight of it.

For towers, Congress actually wrote that split down. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 says that nothing in the law "shall limit or affect the authority of a State or local government" over the placement and construction of wireless facilities, which left the tower decision exactly where it had always been, with the town. And then, in the same paragraph, it took one argument off the table entirely: no state or local government may regulate a tower "on the basis of the environmental effects of radio frequency emissions" so long as it meets the federal rules. The town decides where. Washington decided what you are allowed to argue about when the town decides where.

Hold onto that, because the ratepayer bill that just stalled in the Senate would not have drawn that line for data centers either; it tells state utility commissions what to consider, and says nothing to a county about zoning. So every argument is on the table at the county meeting: the power bill, the water, the noise, the tax abatement, the farmland, the promises about jobs. And the county meeting is where the decisions are actually landing.

## What Washington managed last week

To be fair to Washington, it tried.

Last Wednesday the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3. That is not a typo; in the current Congress, 417 people agreeing on anything is roughly as rare as a quiet week in AI. The bill applies to loads above 100 megawatts and requires state utility commissions to *consider* standards that would make those large loads pay their full incremental cost to the grid. Consider, not adopt. Part of the reason it passed so easily, I suspect, is how little it binds.

On Thursday, Senator Jon Husted of Ohio asked the Senate to pass it by unanimous consent, which is the fast lane: if nobody objects, it goes. Senator Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, the top Democrat on the Energy Committee, objected, saying the bill "falls short of what we need to do to respond," and that instead of "voluntary pledges or suggestions to states" (his characterization of the House bill, to be clear, not mine), "Congress needs to pass real legislation with real teeth." He then asked for unanimous consent on his own bill, the GRID Savings Act, which would require large loads to pay for their grid connections outright. Senator Bernie Moreno of Ohio objected to that one. And then Moreno, with what I can only assume was a straight face, summed it up: "The term do-nothing Congress exists for a reason. We absolutely accomplished that right now."

The bill is not dead. It can still move through the Senate the slow way, and Husted said he would be back. But on the day, the two parties each blocked the other's version in the same exchange, and nothing passed.

One more fact, and I am going to set it beside the others and leave it there. In August, Axios reported an internal memo from the Senate Republicans' own campaign committee about Ohio. It said: "More than any other thing in this race, data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck." It also said, "This has become a sleeper issue for the entire election cycle." Make of the sequence what you will.

## Where it is actually being decided

Now go down a level, to the bodies that meet on a weeknight, publish an agenda, and vote by name.

In Montgomery County, Maryland, the council voted 11 to 0 in July for an 18-month moratorium on data center development. In Prince William County, Virginia, which is about as close to the heart of data center country as it gets, the board voted 8 to 0 in July to deny a project on roughly 2,000 acres. In Monterey Park, California, voters banned data centers outright in June with over 86 percent of the vote, and the mayor was admirably blunt about why they went to the ballot rather than leaving it to the council: "The next council could have come back and reversed it. So this ballot measure is more permanent."

And people are losing their seats over it, in both parties. In Box Elder County, Utah, two Republican commissioners lost their primaries in June over a data center campus, and one of them, Lee Perry, did not dress it up: "Do I think that the data center vote cost me the election? Yes I do." In Calvert County, Maryland, three Republican incumbents lost to challengers who backed a pause. In neighboring Frederick County, it was the Democratic council president who lost. In Festus, Missouri, four incumbents went out, and here is the detail I find most interesting: they included both the members who voted for the project *and* one who voted against it. That looks like anger about how the question got asked, and who got told when, more than anger about the answer. Data Center Knowledge counts eighteen incumbents turned out over data center decisions between August 2025 and August 2026, with its own honest caveat that the evidence is stronger in some cases than others.

The polling moved with it, and it moved fast. A Heatmap Pro survey in August 2025 had registered voters split almost evenly on a data center near their home, 43 in support and 42 opposed. The same question this August came back 75 percent opposed, with strong support down from 13 percent to 4. Gallup, in March, had 71 percent opposing one locally, including 63 percent of Republicans. This is not a blue issue or a red issue. It is a my-county issue.

Even the governors have noticed. Greg Abbott in Texas wants data centers to "bring their own power, reuse their own water and do it in a way that reduces the cost." Tina Kotek in Oregon says her state "needs to stop being a cheap date." Kelly Ayotte in New Hampshire: "It does not make sense at all to site a data center in New Hampshire." A Republican, a Democrat and a Republican, in three different registers, landing in roughly the same place.

## The honest version of the power bill argument

Here is where I have to slow down a little, because the local revolt is running on a claim that is real but only partly true, and a piece that just cheers the revolt on would be doing you a disservice.

The claim is that data centers are raising your electricity bill. The strongest public version of it has already been fact-checked down: in June, PolitiFact rated Senator Elizabeth Warren's statement that bills near large data centers had risen "as much as 267%" Mostly False, because the figure described wholesale prices at specific grid nodes, not residential bills, and wholesale is only somewhere between 30 and 50 percent of what you actually pay.

The more careful numbers are still not nothing. In PJM, the grid operator that covers much of the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, the independent market monitor puts data centers at about 9 percent of total wholesale costs for the first seven months of this year, around $5.1 billion of $56.7 billion, and credits data center load with $29.4 billion in capacity revenue increases across four auctions. And then, just to keep everyone honest, PJM's most recent auction, in July, cleared 2.5 percent *lower* than the year before. Some utilities are filing testimony that big new loads will lower residential bills, spreading fixed costs across more demand. Those are projections, not promises, and you should read them with the same skepticism you would read the scary numbers.

Virginia, which has studied this more than anyone, gives you both halves in a single report. Its legislative watchdog found data centers supply 31 percent of Loudoun County's local tax revenue, about $733 million, and support around 74,000 jobs statewide, and it also projected that unconstrained growth adds about $23 a month to a typical residential bill by 2030. Both of those are true at the same time. On the other side of the ledger, the tax breaks are large too: Good Jobs First puts Georgia's forgone revenue at $2.5 billion in fiscal 2026 alone, and fourteen states publish no annual figure for what their data center breaks cost at all. Water, for what it is worth, is the weakest strand of the case on the statewide numbers, even though it is one of the loudest at the microphone.

So the fair version is something like this: data centers are one real driver of rising costs among several, the tax base they bring is real where it has been measured, the tax breaks they get are real and often unmeasured, and the person at the county meeting is being asked to weigh all of that with none of it on a single page. That is exactly why process anger, the Festus kind, keeps showing up. People do not trust a deal they were not shown.

## What the candidates are actually running on

The midterms are 42 days out, and AI, as such, is not on the ballot. Pew asked voters in July what they most want congressional candidates to talk about, and the economy topped the list at 29 percent; AI did not show up as a volunteered top issue at all. The same survey found 51 percent of Americans agree with *neither* party on AI policy, which is either a sign of a vacuum or a sign of an opportunity, depending on which campaign you work for.

What is on the ballot is the physical footprint of AI, and the money has noticed. AdImpact, via NPR, counts more than $45 million in ads touching data centers, split roughly evenly between the parties, with $12.8 million in the Georgia governor's race alone. In Georgia, two seats on the Public Service Commission, the body that actually sets utility rates, are up on 3 November with the majority at stake, and candidates of both parties are running on making data centers pay.

The two big AI-money networks, Leading the Future on the pro-industry side and Public First Action on the safety side, both spent heavily in the same New York Democratic primary in June, and the candidate neither of them backed won it. Micah Lasher's victory line was aimed at both: "I won't be taking my cues from either of you when it comes to protecting our kids, our jobs, our environment." In Michigan's Senate race, the Republican, Mike Rogers, says "I believe we must slow down development of AI," and the Democrat, Abdul El-Sayed, says "We need to all hit pause." (Rogers came out for a statewide data center moratorium in August, days after disclosures showed between $1.7 million and $2.6 million in AI-exposed holdings, per the Michigan Advance.)

And one more shift worth knowing about: the partisan valence of AI worry has flipped in three years. In 2023, 59 percent of Republicans told Pew they were more concerned than excited about AI, against 46 percent of Democrats. This year it is the other way round: Democrats 56, Republicans 49.

## The camera on the pole

This is the strand I did not expect to be writing about, and it is the one I find most interesting.

Flock Safety sells automated license plate readers, the cameras on poles at intersections and neighborhood entrances that log every car that passes. By its own count it serves more than 5,000 agencies, and it raised $500 million at a valuation of roughly $8.3 billion at the end of August. Every one of those cameras goes up the same way a data center does: a city council or a county board votes on a contract, usually on a consent agenda, usually without much of an audience.

The audiences have arrived. In Pflugerville, Texas, a public records request showed that 459 outside agencies had run about 1.6 million searches against the city's 28 cameras over six months, against 5,078 searches by the town's own police. The council ended the contract unanimously in August. The town was paying about $87,500 a year so that other people's police departments could search its residents' cars. In Knox County, Tennessee, under a Republican mayor, commissioners passed a unanimous emergency ordinance in August, and the trigger was not privacy at all: it was a $2.6 million maintenance ask that had never come before them. In Texas the whole state network is funded by a one-dollar levy on every auto insurance policy, which means that, in the most literal sense, nobody ever voted for the cameras.

And look at who is doing the objecting. Greg Abbott: "Flock cameras are being reduced dramatically. They're going to be required to be used responsibly if they're used at all." Ron DeSantis: "I'm all about having law enforcement have tools. But I don't want to have this become a surveillance state." Senator Josh Hawley opened a Judiciary subcommittee probe in August: "Americans do not surrender their privacy rights when they drive to work, drop their kids off at school, or go to church." The House bill to cut off federal grants to cities running the cameras, the Flock-Off Act, was introduced by Thomas Massie with Lauren Boebert, Paul Gosar, Chip Roy, Victoria Spartz and, on the other side of the aisle, Ro Khanna. The Fourth Amendment lawsuits are being run by the Institute for Justice and the Wisconsin Institute for Law & Liberty, a libertarian shop and a conservative one, while the ACLU does the records work. The sharpest slogan in the whole fight belongs to a gun-rights group, the National Association for Gun Rights: "No warrant. No suspicion. No vote."

That last line is the whole piece in five words, really: the objection is as much about the vote that never happened as about the camera.

The company has moved, too. At the end of July, Flock told a reporter its default retention was 30 days. By mid-August its own trust page said seven, "unless local law says otherwise," and it added tools letting a community block searches for specified purposes, including immigration enforcement. That is what a vendor does when the county meetings start going badly.

## Where the coalition thins out

I would love to tell you that this is a tidy left-right alliance sweeping the country. It is not, and the piece is more honest with the exceptions in.

Cleveland, under a Democratic mayor, voted 9 to 6 in July to renew its cameras, despite records showing around 2,000 immigration-related searches in 2025. Oklahoma City renewed 5 to 3 in August with nobody from the public speaking in favor. Carrollton, Texas, expanded its network this month, and its police chief called it "the best tool we have to reduce crime." (Whether it actually reduces crime is a live fight between two interested institutions, with studies on both sides, and I am not going to pretend it is settled in either direction.) Denver cancelled a ten-year, $22 million Flock contract in February and replaced it with a system from Axon, which the ACLU also opposed, so ending a Flock contract is not the same thing as ending plate reading. And the President likes the cameras: "I sort of like them because of that, because of law enforcement."

Most telling of all, in the Reuters/Ipsos survey from August, Republican voters were the group most in favor of the cameras, while Democrats and independents opposed them. Nationally, support has dropped to 38 percent in two separate polls. So the Republican governors, senators and legal shops leading this fight are running ahead of their own voters, not behind them. That is a more interesting sentence than "strange bedfellows," and it is the same pattern as the data centers: the argument at the top of the ticket and the argument at the county building have come apart.

## Before 3 November

The one thing I would do differently after reading all of this: **before 3 November, find out who on your own ballot actually votes on the substation, the rezoning and the camera contract.** It is not the name at the top.

**Look up the down-ballot seats first.** County commission, board of supervisors, city council, township board, and in some states the utility commission itself. Those are the people who decided Montgomery County's moratorium, Prince William County's denial and Knox County's cameras. Find out which of those seats are up this year, who is running for them, and what each candidate has said about the specific project nearest you. If they have said nothing, that is also an answer, and you are allowed to ask them to say where they stand before the vote rather than after it.

**Read the next zoning agenda instead of the next Senate vote.** The frontier fight will be on the news every night between now and November, and it will not change a thing about your street. The planning commission agenda will. Look for a rezoning of a few hundred acres, a large-load interconnection, a tax abatement, or a data center under whatever name the applicant chose for it. The agenda is posted ahead of the meeting, it is public, and one informed person at the microphone is harder to ignore than you might think.

**Put the camera question to your city clerk in writing.** Does your town have a license plate reader contract, with whom, when does it renew, what is the retention period, and how many searches did outside agencies run against it last year? Pflugerville found its answer through a records request. And while you are at it, ask who *else* runs cameras inside city limits, because when Asheville terminated its contract it covered 11 city cameras, and 86 more, run by other entities, kept running.

Washington will still be arguing about the frontier in November, and it will very likely still be arguing about it the November after that. The substation, the rezoning and the camera contract will all have been decided long before it finishes.

The only open question is whether you turned up for it.
