On Saturday morning the chief executive of the most valuable AI lab in the world asked, in writing, to be slowed down. By Saturday evening the chief executives of the second and third had agreed with him.

I do not think the sky is falling, and I am not going to spend your Tuesday arguing that it is. What I want to spend it on is the shape of the argument that broke into the open this week, because it is not the shape most of the coverage is giving you. The fight in Washington over frontier AI is not left against right. It is not even government against industry. It is a fight between people who want an institution and people who want a runway, and that line runs straight through both parties, straight through the industry, and, as of this weekend, straight through the White House.

Bear with me on the analogy, because it is going to carry the whole piece. In motorsport, nobody on the grid disputes that the point is to beat the other cars. What they fight about, endlessly, is scrutineering: whether there is a person with a badge in your garage before the race, what that person is allowed to measure, and whether the team gets to fill in its own form. Every team wants the badge in the other garages. No team, historically, has asked for one in its own. That is the story of this week. The fastest team on the grid just asked for the scrutineer, and half the paddock has decided that means the fastest team is cheating.

## What Dario Amodei actually wrote

The essay is called *We Must Pace the Frontier*, it is on his personal site under his own name, and it is about 3,850 words long. You should read it rather than the coverage of it, because the coverage has been doing what coverage does. The bolded thesis is this: *"We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain."* Two paragraphs later comes the hedge that most of the headlines dropped: pacing *"does not mean halting model training or technical progress, but ensuring companies take adequate time to align and safeguard their models, and for third party evaluators to confirm this."*

Now name the interest, because it is a large one. Anthropic is expected to market an IPO in October. A rule that applies only to frontier labs is a fixed cost that Anthropic can carry and a startup cannot. The essay asks for a narrow antitrust waiver so that the labs can talk to each other about safety, which in any other industry would be called something less flattering. Matt Stoller, who watches concentration for a living, read it as a demand *"that the government stop applying laws restraining big business."* The accelerationist wing read it worse. Jason Calacanis: *"That's all this is about: stopping open source."* Martin Casado at a16z, whose fund has a large open-weights book: *"It refuses to be caged."* Chamath Palihapitiya said it was a case for concentrating *"enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic."* Every one of those men has money on the other side of the sentence, and every one of them may still be partly right.

Here is the sentence I would ask you to sit with anyway, because it is the operative one and it cuts against the man who wrote it: *"The most effective method of pacing is via regulation that targets all US frontier AI companies, as that covers even those who are unwilling to cooperate voluntarily."* That is the company that benefits most from the voluntary status quo stating, over its founder's name, that voluntary is second-best. And it did not stop at the sentence. The essay commits Anthropic, unilaterally and now, to embedding third-party evaluators with employee-like access and *"the right to publish key findings… without editorial control by Anthropic… we can't redact findings just because they are unfavorable."* Two and a half hours later Sam Altman: *"I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier… Committing to having independent evaluators with employee-like access is a great idea, and we will do the same."* Elon Musk, three words: *"Dario is right."*

Whatever you think of the motive, a norm moved on Saturday. On Friday no frontier lab had committed to letting an outsider with a badge publish what it found without the lab's permission. On Sunday the three largest had.

The accelerationist answer arrived late on Saturday night, and it is worth reading closely, because it does not say what the paddock assumed it would. David Sacks, the former White House AI czar and now co-chair of the President's science council, replied to Amodei and Altman on X: *"People may be surprised by my response: go ahead. You guys are the frontier."* And then the sentences that tell you where the line actually runs: *"But stop pretending you need anyone else's permission. Stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel. Stop pretending you need a regulatory approval process that supersedes product liability."* He did not dispute that the cars should slow down. He conceded it, in writing, and added that *"the market already punishes models that behave in unpredictable or unauthorized ways."* What he disputed was the badge. Slow yourselves, crash on your own account, and let the courts settle it afterwards. He also got off the best line of the weekend, and fairness to the man requires printing it: pacing the frontier *"would also create breathing room for a more intelligent conversation about regulation than Bernie Sanders' 'shut it all down.'"* Note what that sentence concedes. He is not against a conversation about regulation. He is against one that ends with a person in the garage.

One more thing the essay does not do, and it matters for what follows. It does not mention states, or preemption, or Congress, or a bill, or an executive order, or a single named politician. The political mapping was outsourced to Anthropic's head of public policy, Sarah Heck, fourteen minutes later on X, and her version asks for *"the power to block"* unsafe models before release, thanks Thune, Cruz and Klobuchar by name, and commends state lawmakers *"for moving with such urgency."* Hold on to that. The lab that wants to be regulated is not asking for the states to be cleared out of the way.

## The senator who wants more regulation, and the one who wants less, are the same senator

Ted Cruz is the chairman of the committee any federal AI bill has to pass through, and this week he said two things that do not fit in the same room.

On Politico's *The Conversation*, published Thursday, he said, and I am quoting him precisely: *"We need substantially more regulation when it comes to AI."* On ABC's *The View* on Wednesday he said that for AI implicating biological or nuclear risk, *"it would require government supervision and approval before they can be implemented."* Government supervision and approval, before release, from the man who a year ago tried to attach a ten-year moratorium on state AI laws to a budget bill. He also told the same audiences that *"if there are going to be killer robots, I would rather they be American killer robots, rather than Chinese killer robots,"* that slowing down is an *"impossible endeavor,"* and that if Washington shut the industry down *"China would catch up in six months and soar ahead."*

The bill he is negotiating with Thune and Klobuchar has not been introduced. As I write this it has no number, no title and no public text. `[MONDAY-NIGHT RE-CHECK: the Senate returned Monday 14 September; if the bill was introduced, replace this sentence with its number and one line on what the text says, and delete the next paragraph's hedges accordingly.]` What exists is a set of descriptions from people who say they have seen a draft, and those descriptions do not agree with each other or with the chairman. Transformer, a newsletter whose primary funder is Coefficient Giving, the philanthropy formerly known as Open Philanthropy and a committed party to this debate, reported from one source that the bill *"lets AI companies grade their own homework"* and creates a voluntary regime. Politico's four sources and Reuters describe something with more spine: a legal duty of care, a duty to tell Commerce when a company discovers a dangerous capability in its own model, and a Commerce injunction in federal court as the backstop. Read those together and you get a self-triggered disclosure with a court-reviewable stick behind it, which is neither "voluntary" nor "approval." Nobody who has seen the text describes anything that looks like pre-release government approval. The chairman described it on national television anyway.

I do not think Cruz is lying. I think he is standing on the fault line. The part of him that has read the same briefings Amodei has read wants a scrutineer. The part of him that has spent a decade running against Washington wants the yellow flags out of the way. He said both out loud in a single week, and the bill is stuck between the two halves of its own sponsor.

The Democratic side is not tidier. Maria Cantwell, the committee's ranking member, welcomed the urgency and then drew her line: *"the answer is not a weak federal standard that becomes a backdoor for wiping out stronger state protections. Meaningful legislation would require the most powerful AI models undergo testing by scientists and experts at our national laboratories."* John Fetterman, the same week: *"I reject the political pandering and hyperbole over data centers or AI doomsdaying."* Josh Hawley, of all people, made the cleanest institutionalist case in the building, *"calling on Congress not just to say no to a bunch of state laws, but to put down a federal standard. That's a very different thing."* Josh Gottheimer, co-chair of the House AI commission, read Saturday's essays and was unmoved: *"If Dario and Elon are truly worried, they can pump the brakes at their own labs, today. We can't rely on self-policing and third party audits."*

Line those up by party and you learn nothing. Line them up by whether the speaker wants somebody with a badge in the garage, and every one of them sorts cleanly.

## The improvised instrument, and the people who reached for it

Here is the fact that made me want to write this piece at all.

On the fourth of August, fifteen Republican state attorneys general, led by Pennsylvania's Dave Sunday, wrote to OpenAI demanding that it *"immediately cease the testing activities that led to the breach unless and until OpenAI can demonstrate those activities can be conducted safely and responsibly."* Sunday's own line: *"innovation cannot come at the expense of public safety."* Six days later, thirty-two House Democrats wrote to the same company demanding the same logs about the same incident. Same event, same demand, opposite parties, six days apart. Show us what happened, and stop until you can.

Neither group reached for a federal AI statute, because there is not one. Twenty-nine states enacted AI legislation this year. The number of federal AI statutes enacted is zero. The only instrument that has actually compelled a document out of a frontier lab in 2026 is a state consumer-protection subpoena, the Alabama attorney general's, issued in August under a deceptive-trade-practices act written for used-car lots. Last November thirty-six state attorneys general, Idaho and Utah and Tennessee alongside California and New York, told Congress in one letter that *"broad federal preemption would undermine states' ability to respond quickly and effectively."* Seventeen Republican governors asked the Senate majority leader to strip the moratorium. Ron DeSantis called it *"bad policy and even worse politics."*

And the federal government's own instrument disclaims itself in its own text. Executive Order 14409, signed on the second of June, sets up a frontier-model review framework and then, in section 3(c), says this: *"Nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models, including frontier models."* The access framework is *"voluntary."* The one part with teeth, the benchmarking that decides which models are covered at all, is classified. The government took the problem seriously enough to write an order about it, and then wrote into the order that it could not compel anything. That is a choice, made in daylight, and it is the choice Anthropic's policy chief asked on Saturday to have reversed.

The most forceful thing the federal executive has done about AI governance this year is join Elon Musk's lawsuit against Colorado, whose AI Act, the most comprehensive state statute in the country, has had its enforcement suspended since April by joint motion. No court has ruled it unconstitutional. It is simply not being enforced, at the request of the company that sued and the government that intervened.

## The split runs through the White House

I would have written the above a week ago as a story about Congress and the states. What changed is that the same line now visibly runs through the executive branch, and the President stepped on it in an airport.

Bloomberg reported on Saturday, from unnamed officials and I will label it as such, that Treasury Secretary Bessent drafted a proposal for a FINRA-style independent AI regulator, with the National Cyber Director on his side, and that David Sacks, now co-chair of the President's science council, called it *"a horrible idea"* and *"a total fig leaf."* The same reporting says early drafts of the June executive order would have compelled the labs to share their models, and that Sacks phoned the President in May to kill the restrictive version. Sacks said the quiet part on Fox on Thursday, on the record: *"The truth is this was a well-orchestrated media op. to scare the public about AI in order to implement their preferred approach to AI, which is a government takeover."* Two days later, in the Saturday-night post, he sharpened it rather than walked it back: go ahead and slow down, but *"demanding your preferred regulatory framework as the price of that will look like blackmail of the public and the political system,"* and if the labs will not pace themselves without a rule attached, *"we'll know this was just another bid for regulatory capture,"* or, his other option, *"an election-season psyop."* Bessent's own line, reported in the same Bloomberg piece: *"There is no day after tomorrow if China wins at this."*

Then the President, at DFW on Thursday, asked by a reporter whether he had any concerns about AI leading to human extinction. I am going to give you the exchange rather than the paraphrase, because the paraphrase has been doing the rounds and it is not what he said. *"No, I don't have any. I have concerns that if we don't win AI, we're going to be put in a very bad position. We are leading China right now by a pretty good period, I would say a year, which is, you know, considered a lot."* On Laura Ingraham's show the next night he went further: *"We have guardrails. The biggest guardrails are having people that are just as smart… China doesn't have guardrails by the way. You know the guardrails? President Xi is the guardrail… whoever wins AI wins."*

Read the two men on his own staff and then read him. Bessent wants the institution because of China. Sacks wants the runway because of China. The President's answer is that the smart people are the guardrail, and the guardrail is a lead measured in months. And Amodei, in the essay that started all this, does not dispute a word of the China frame. *"I agree with Secretary Bessent that a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world,"* he writes, and then argues that the lead is exactly what buys the time to pace. Senator Cornyn's entire reply to the essay was two words: *"Will China?"* Sacks closed his own reply on the same note: *"China is very unlikely to join a global agreement, as you know, and that has to be taken into account as well."*

That is the tell. Nobody in any camp is defending the status quo, and the only sentence every camp signs is the one about China. The disagreement is over what the lead is for. The accelerationists think it is the prize. The institutionalists think it is the budget.

## Where I sit

I will be plain about my own position, because a piece like this without one is just a seating chart.

I think the extinction drumbeat is mostly noise, and I have said so in this newsletter more than once. That is not the same as thinking the institutionalists are wrong. `[YOUR TAKE -- the insurance paragraph. Drafted from his own sentence; rewrite freely.]` Something being extraordinarily unlikely has never been a reason not to protect against it as well as we are able. Nobody I know who runs infrastructure believes their data center is going to burn down this year, and every one of them has a fire suppression system, an insurance policy and a tested failover, and would be fired for not having them. We do not price controls on whether we expect the event. We price them on what it costs if we are wrong and whether the control is affordable. A person with a badge in the building, allowed to publish what they find, is about the cheapest control in this whole debate, and the argument against it this week has mostly been that the people asking for it have impure motives. They probably do. The fire marshal's motives are not why the sprinklers work.

`[YOUR TAKE -- the CTO's seat. Sketch only, not fabricated: the vendor-side view. Every frontier lab you buy from just changed its public posture in one weekend; your board will ask what that means for the contract, and the honest answer is that until there is a badge in the building, the only assurance you have is the vendor's own paper. He has lived the version of this where the regulator shows up every two years in gaming and the assurance is real because somebody outside the company holds the clipboard.]`

For my Australian readers, this is not a foreign dysfunction you get to watch from a safe distance. Australia ran this exact experiment first. In September 2024 the Department of Industry consulted on a standalone AI Act with binding guardrails for high-risk settings. In December 2025 the National AI Plan dropped them for a formula that reads *"regulate as necessary but as little as possible."* That is section 3(c) of the American order, arrived at independently, on the other side of the world, six months earlier. You are not behind the United States on this. You are eighteen months further into the same decision, and American firms are now citing your political stability as a reason to build data centers there. Being the jurisdiction that chose not to build the instrument is, for the moment, a selling point.

## What to do with this on a Tuesday afternoon

Nothing in this piece requires you to have an opinion about a Senate bill that does not exist. It does require two things, and a third that is yours.

**Ask your frontier vendors which side of Saturday's line they are on, in writing, at the next review.** Three companies committed this weekend to embedded third-party evaluators with employee-like access and the right to publish without the company's editorial control. That is a contractual fact about your supplier now, or it is not. Ask whether the commitment applies to the models you are licensed to use, who the evaluator is, and where the findings will be published. A vendor that signed on Saturday and cannot answer on Tuesday has told you something useful, and so has a vendor that never signed.

**Assume for the next twelve months that your AI regulator is a state attorney general, not a federal agency.** Every instrument that has actually bitten a frontier lab this year is a state consumer-protection power, and thirty-six attorneys general have said in writing that they intend to keep using it. If you are an American reader, the deceptive-trade-practices statute in your own state is the operative AI law, and it applies to what you tell your customers about your AI as much as it applies to OpenAI. If you are an Australian reader, the ACCC has the same shape of power, and the National AI Plan just made it the only one you have.

## Where I think I am most likely wrong

The strongest case against everything above is that the improvised instrument is winning.

Defense-side counsel, who have no reason to flatter attorneys general, describe state AG power as broad, flexible and fast: civil investigative demands, subpoenas, compelled testimony, all under statutes deliberately written loose so that legislatures do not have to anticipate every new harm. The AGs moved in weeks. Congress has moved in fourteen months and produced one committee vote, twenty-nine to nothing, on a bill that then sat for two and a half months. On raw responsiveness, the thing nobody designed has beaten the thing everybody is arguing about, and Kat Cammack's objection is not rhetorical either: a startup cannot build a compliance function for fifty states and survive.

The second case against me is Sacks's, and it goes at the control rather than the politics. His position is that liability after the fact is the regime, and that an approval process before release adds nothing a courtroom cannot supply. Name his book too: a venture portfolio with plenty of the companies he says a frontier-only rule would leave to be policed by evaluators the frontier chose. The commercial interests run both ways in this fight, and that is the point of the seating chart. But the shape of his objection is fair, and it is the one I would make myself: a badge is worth exactly what the badge-holder's independence is worth, and if the answer to "who checks" turns out to be "people the labs picked," that is self-certification with a lanyard.

Here is why I still think the institutionalists have the better of it. Consumer-protection law compels documents after the fact. It cannot set a standard before deployment, cannot put a person in the building during an incident, cannot require a report on a clock, and produces fifty answers to one question. It is forensic, not preventive. This country built compelled-access regimes for aviation and for chemical plants, with investigators who can walk onto the site and a statute that says they may. It has not built one for this, and the only people asking for one this week were a Treasury Secretary, a Republican committee chairman on daytime television, and the three men who stand to be regulated by it. Liability after the fact is what you have when nobody has built the other thing yet. It is not a design. It is the default.

## The line everyone signs

`[MONDAY-NIGHT RE-CHECK: Casar's deadline for OpenAI's full answers was this morning. If OpenAI answered, say so in one sentence and keep the paragraph; if it did not, keep the paragraph as written.]` By the time you read this, a congressman's deadline for OpenAI to explain what happened inside its own systems will have passed. Whatever OpenAI did or did not send, nothing follows from it, because there is no consequence attached to that letter in either branch of government. That is the whole institutionalist argument in one sentence, and it does not need the sky to be falling to be true.

The odds being long has never been the reason we skip the insurance. It is usually the reason we can afford it.
