## 🆕 New Roles & Archetypes Watch

**The Conductor archetype — live specimen:** Ryan Carson's 15-agent solo operation is the clearest real-world instantiation of the Conductor role yet documented in this thread. He doesn't code; he sets direction, maintains quality gates, manages the customer layer, and makes the calls that require skin in the game. The agents do the rest.

**The Auditor role (named):** Medium piece "The Age of Agentic AI: What Engineering Jobs Actually Look Like in 2026 (second half)" formalises "Auditor" as a mid-tier engineering role — reads agent-generated code with skepticism, treats agents "like a brilliant but reckless intern," enforces strict boundaries and demands rigorous unit tests.
→ Maps to: **Editor** (quality gate) + **Risk Steward** (boundary enforcement).

**The Orchestrator role (named):** Same piece defines "Orchestrator" as the new senior engineer — rarely touches implementation details; translates business requirements into explicit, bounded contexts that agents cannot misunderstand.
→ Maps directly to: **Orchestrator** in the NLW/Cherny taxonomy.

**Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) — resurging:** Wikipedia has a standalone entry; FinOps X 2026 conference treated it as an established role. Combines software development with domain understanding and direct end-user collaboration. As agents commoditise implementation, the FDE's deployment-and-domain side gains relative value.
→ Maps to: **Grower** + **Evangelist** (bridges product and customer context).

**AI FinOps as emerging function:** State of FinOps 2026 finds 98% of FinOps practices now managing AI inference spend — up from near-zero two years ago. "AI FinOps" is crystallising as a discipline with its own orchestrator-agent-consultant-agent architecture. The practitioner who understands both unit economics and model routing is the new **Risk Steward**.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The community conversation has crossed a threshold: the argument about *whether* AI reshapes work is over, and the argument about *how to run it* has begun. Ryan Carson's 15-agent solo operation isn't a future scenario — it's a current company with paying customers. DHH is naming the vocabulary ("agentic engineering") that separates serious practitioners from bystanders. Mollick is reframing the tool-selection question as a hiring question. The architecture-of-work model isn't a taxonomy anymore; it's a job description. The reader who can articulate which archetype they occupy, and which ones they want to add to their repertoire, is already ahead of most of their peers. The window to make that self-assessment isn't closing, but it's no longer standing wide open.
