## ⚖️ The Debate (kept honest, kept brief)

The week's biggest structural story: both Altman and Amodei have walked back their jobs-apocalypse warnings. Altman: "I'm delighted to be wrong… I thought there would have been more impact on entry-level white-collar jobs by now" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/05/26/sam-altman-dario-amodei-walking-back-ai-jobs-apocalypse-prophecies-ipo/)). Amodei now argues automation may *expand* work: "If you automate 90% of the job, then everyone does the 10%." The evidence base: Yale Budget Lab finds no meaningful unemployment change for AI-exposed workers since 2022 ([Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/ai-probably-not-yet-reason-labor-market-weakening)). Honest caveats: both companies have IPO filings pending (skeptics note the incentive), a UN panel this month flagged agentic AI as a rising white-collar pressure, and displacement is real in junior dev, paralegal, and entry-admin roles even as aggregate numbers hold. Conaway's apprenticeship-gap point is the version of the pessimist case worth engaging.

## 📌 Today's Signal

The apocalypse retracted and nobody panicked — that's the story. The two loudest doom voices in the industry publicly downgraded their own predictions in the same season that a $200K job title materialized from nothing, a six-hour agent-building course for non-coders pulled 64K views, and Mollick quietly renamed the core skill of the era "assigning work to agents." The community conversation has moved from *whether* jobs survive to *how work decomposes* — Mollick's fight over what "front-end" even means is archetype-negotiation happening in public. For the reader trying to adapt: the leverage is in the seams. Pick your function, learn to assign work to agents (it's management, not magic), and watch the token bill — because Orchestrator, Builder, and Risk Steward aren't future jobs, they're this quarter's job postings wearing new names.
