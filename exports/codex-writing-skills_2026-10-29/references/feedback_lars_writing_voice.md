**Opener style:** Two short declarative sentences. No "As if..." or subordinate clause openers. State the fact, then escalate. Example: "The sheer volume of threats has spiked horrifically over the last 6 months. The threat arc is increasing constantly, sometimes with exponential jumps."

**Why:** He trimmed a long hedging opener to two punchy declarations across multiple revision rounds.

**Analogy-first:** Reaches for a concrete analogy before stating the abstract argument. Analogies are colloquial and slightly irreverent ("Jimmy in the mailroom", "modern-day Obi-Wan"). Pop culture references are welcome when precise.

**Problem-before-fix:** US East Coast instinct -- names the threat clearly and fully before prescribing solutions. Do not restructure this; it's intentional and correct for his audience.

**Jargon handling:** Explains technical terms inline with parentheticals rather than separate explanatory sentences. Example: "context explosion (creating lots of junk that the AI has to interpret...)"

**Hollywood vs. grind contrast:** Recurring instinct to contrast the "cinematic" framing of threats with the reality of diligent, repeatable defensive work. A signature observation -- but a conditional callback, not a slot (see signature-devices rule below).

**Sentence rhythm:** Short punchy sentences mixed with longer structured ones. Comfortable with semicolons. Uses normal dashes (-) and double dashes (--) for emphasis. Never use em-dashes (--) -- he explicitly rejected them. Do not convert his dashes.

**Section openings:** Leave light -- Lars will add his framing. Don't front-load section openers with my own argument; leave a natural drop-in point.

**How to apply:** Draft Substack articles with a light, open intro structure and punchy declarative section openers. Leave `<!-- [YOUR TAKE]: -->` prompts only at spots where his personal experience or opinion would sharpen the piece. Calibrate for CISO/decision-maker audience, not purely technical practitioners.

**Additional voice markers (from Privacy Act draft collaboration, Jun 25 2026):**

**Closes -- CORRECTED (2026-07-24; supersedes the 2026-07-19 "default" rule):** The reader's-calculus/news-cycle close is a rationed signature callback, NOT the default weekday closer. His words: the beat is real, "it just doesn't always land in the piece and it doesn't always align to the topic, so it should be very much conditional as a signature device, not a requirement." Once or twice a week at most, only when it aligns with the topic, worded fresh each time -- "the calculus idea is right, the deluge of content is right on, but saying it a different way, consistently, is also very much right on." Close shape: two short paragraphs, resolved line first; the last sentence lands one of two ways -- "a meaningful conclusion that people go 'yeah I get it'" or, when genuinely open-ended, "an open question that indicates they need to take action because no one else is going to hand them easy answers to this stuff." "What I'm going to do" forward-guidance closes rank a distant third ("these are less about me and more about the space"), licensed mainly when the piece carries a personal/professional anecdote, and even then sparingly. **How to apply:** derive the close from what the story is about; before deploying the calculus close, check the last two scheduled/published pieces; keep an escalating-tool metaphor concrete when one is genuinely running, never force one.

**Signature devices are conditional callbacks (2026-07-24, general rule from Lars):** ALL signature devices -- "Read that again.", the escalating-tool metaphor (hammer → bandsaw), Hollywood-vs-grind, the reader's-calculus close -- are callbacks rationed across the week, deployed only when the piece calls for them, never slots to fill. Device repetition across consecutive pieces is slop (the editor panel's Seat 5 now checks the last two pieces for it). His framing: "we need to make sure that we're not just repeating a formula where we're actually delivering to what the story is about." A draft loses nothing for lacking a device; it loses for forcing one. **Enforcement caveat (2026-07-25):** this rule only bites if the charter copy actually running the panel has it -- see [[charter-drift-across-three-copies]]. A stale installed skill drove the calculus close onto four straight weekdays after this correction was made.

**Appositive-gloss-then-consequence (2026-07-24):** In exposition he names an abstraction, stacks its disqualifying traits in a `--` appositive right after the noun, then breaks the consequence into its OWN following sentence. Do NOT collapse it back to one sentence when tightening -- the break is the point.

**Excuse-Expired sign-off calibration (2026-07-25 piece, approved 2026-07-21):**
- **He'll take a proposed skeptic-preempting concession+turn** -- when a piece leans on one number/claim a sharp reader could dismiss (here: a view count = a "movement"), a short two-beat "No, X isn't Y. But [upside reframe]." works and he'll green-light it. He approved "No, a view isn't a finished course. But nobody accidentally sits down for six hours." Keep it brief and end on the upside -- this IS the Saturday "one beat of doubt, then pivot up" rule applied to the skeptic, not just to fear. Offer such a line rather than leaving the soft spot open.
- **Economics honesty, both sides:** when a free/cheap on-ramp is part of the pitch, name the free option AND its real limit, then the honest paid path. He wanted readers told the free tier exists but only gets you a taste (hit-the-wall / wait-for-reset), with the real path being ~$20/mo Claude Pro (~a couple hundred a year). Get the actual price right -- he'd been on Max and mis-stated it as $10; don't let a guessed price ship. Extends the capability+cost marker: understating cost reads as dishonest to him.
- **View/stat numbers:** use HIS live-verified figure even if it's lower than an earlier number -- he'd rather ship a real 87K he checked this morning than an unverified higher one. When a number looks off (e.g. body vs. header mismatch) flag it, but his live read wins over a stale/typo'd figure. Keep the note-to-self to refresh the count on publish morning.

**Length: acknowledge it, don't cut the argument (2026-07-25, "Who Controls the Model" sign-off):** When a piece runs long because the argument needs the room, his move is to TELL THE READER and ask their patience -- not to sand the argument to hit a word count. The 30 Jul piece ran 2,810w against a ~2,000-2,330 house norm; the panel flagged and trimmed twice; his call was "nah, let's go with it," plus an aside he'd add after the opening, roughly: *"now bear with me, because like Frank Herbert, I am about to wax on for a bit. This piece is somewhat longer than most, but I think the message is important enough that it's worth it. I hope you stick it out."* Three mechanics worth copying: it is wry self-deprecation aimed inward (the footer register, promoted into the body); it hangs the aside on the piece's own frame so it doubles as a callback rather than sitting outside the piece; and it ends on an invitation, not a justification. **How to apply:** flag length so he can decide, never treat the house norm as a hard gate, and offer the acknowledgment-aside as a third option alongside cut-or-ship. Placement is after the opening/frame, before the argument gets going.

**"Tooling People Up" sign-off calibration (2026-08-04 piece, signed off 2026-08-01) -- MINDSET/FLAVOR (same standing caution: dispositions to recognize, not templates to reinsert):**
- **Upbeat close is welcome on the lighter/constructive tributary weekday (not only Saturday Diversions).** He liked ending *up* -- "we made it upbeat too, which is a nice change for a Tuesday." The deliberately-lighter human-scale beat (the Co-Existence / Shape-of-Work tributary that gives readers a breather mid-week) is licensed to land on agency + action instead of problem->prescribe->doom. Most of the week earns its bleakness; recognize when a piece is the exhale and let it land up. Don't force cheer onto a heavy piece.
- **One landing, at the very end (competing-landings fix).** He struck a mini-drum that ended a body paragraph ("But it starts today.") and moved that action beat into the FINAL sentence instead ("...the only one left. Luckily, it's one you can start today."). Two "endings" a few lines apart blunt each other; consolidate to a single close, and let the last line carry the forward/action note. General craft rule, not a phrasing.
- **Personal-LIFE anecdotes as ground, not only professional cost stories.** He opened the dog metaphor with his own family dog ("my family got a dog a few years ago, at the urging of one of my sons") and closed on his three kids and YouTube. Broadens the "leave room for a first-person story" rule: warm lived anecdote (family, hobbies, raising kids) is as valid as a cost story. Leave the slot at the emotional beats; his real life is the payload -- never fabricate it.
- **Cross-piece economy -- don't double a beat that lives better elsewhere in the week.** He cut the "building the walls / do it safely" sentence because "that's covered in the capstone." He'll strike a good line when its point is carried by another piece in the same arc. When a beat is already handled elsewhere that week, offer the cut rather than repeat it.
- **Everyday, no-jargon analogy for an abstract split.** To illustrate Brooks's "two kinds of user," he reached for Google search ("who goes past page 1 / who drills into Wikipedia / who reads the AI summary at the top") -- an ordinary behavior everyone recognizes. He likes a mundane, universally-legible analogy to make an abstract division concrete; offer one when a section is getting theoretical.
- **Faint metaphor-blend is a real (small) miss he'll fix.** Part of why he cut "building the walls" was that it pulled Monday's containment-*walls* metaphor into Tuesday as *enablement* -- same word, different meaning, across consecutive pieces. Watch for a controlling metaphor from one day leaking into the next with a shifted sense; flag it.
- **He'll adopt a proposed wordplay that resolves a real tension.** He took the loop double-meaning line ("loop engineering is getting the human *out* of the loop... You are not in the loop anymore. You are the one who drew it.") because it turned a skeptic tension into a pun that sharpened the point. Offer wordplay when it does argumentative work, not for decoration.
