# Research Note — the vendor AUP sweep: who the policy is written about
For: `pitch_policy-exempts-its-author_2026-09-04.md` (proposed Tue 15 Sep) · Depth: deep, four primaries fetched in full · Date: 2026-09-05

## Bottom line

**The lazy version of this piece is wrong, and the true version is much better.** The lazy version
says AI vendors impose disclosure duties on customers and disclose nothing themselves. That is false:
Microsoft, Anthropic and OpenAI all disclose, in detail, that AI models do the detecting and in some
cases the deciding. **What is true is that none of them disclose it in the document that carries the
obligation.** The duty is contractual. The disclosure is editorial — a Learn page, a blog post, a help
centre article — none of it incorporated into anything the customer signs, all of it editable or
deletable without notice. **The rule and the confession live in different buildings, and only one of
them is binding.** That is the exact register shift Illingworth found in UK universities, running in a
commercial supply chain, and it is Lars's material because he is on both sides of it.

## What's solid — the four documents, fetched in full

### 1. Microsoft — Code of Conduct for Microsoft AI Services, v4.0, last updated 1 May 2026

The most important specimen, because it is his core vendor and because it is **contractual** — it hangs
off the Product Terms, not off a documentation site.

- **Duty on the customer is explicit and itemised.** Responsible AI requirement #3, verbatim:
  *"Disclose when the output, decisions, or actions are generated by AI, including the synthetic
  nature of generated voices, images, and/or videos, such that users are not likely to be deceived or
  duped … or that any AI-generated decision or action is done by a human."* Requirement #4 adds
  durable visible watermarking and re-signed AI Content Credentials for video before external
  distribution. The Speech/Voice terms add a further prominent-disclosure duty to external users.
- **Disclosure by Microsoft about Microsoft: none in this document.** The only enforcement sentence,
  verbatim: *"Customers deemed by Microsoft to have violated the Code of Conduct or the Microsoft
  Product Terms may lose access to the Online Service, at Microsoft's sole discretion."*
  **"Deemed by Microsoft."** The document does not say what does the deeming.
- ★ **And Microsoft does say, elsewhere.** Azure AI Foundry / Azure OpenAI data-privacy documentation,
  verbatim: *"the system employs algorithms and heuristics to detect indicators of potential abuse.
  When these indicators are detected, a sample of customer's prompts and completions may be selected
  for review. Review is conducted by automated means including by AI models such as LLMs by default,
  with additional reviews by human reviewers as necessary."* Human reviewers are named as *"authorized
  Microsoft employees"* using Secure Access Workstations and just-in-time approval, EEA-resident for
  EEA deployments.
  **That is a real Appendix A. It is on a documentation page, not in the code of conduct, and no
  customer is required to read it.**
- ★★ **The line worth the whole sweep, for a regulated reader.** Customers approved for *modified
  abuse monitoring* get, verbatim: *"the data storage and human review process described above is not
  performed. However, automated review may still be conducted, leveraging algorithms including AI
  models that review prompts and completions at the time provided or generated."* **The opt-out
  removes the human, not the machine.**

### 2. Anthropic — Usage Policy, effective 15 September 2025

- **Duty on the customer, verbatim (High-Risk Use Cases):** *"If model outputs are presented directly
  to individuals or consumers, you must disclose to them that you are using AI to help produce your
  advice, decisions, or recommendations. This disclosure must be provided at a minimum at the
  beginning of each session."* Plus: all consumer-facing chatbots *"must disclose to users that they
  are interacting with AI rather than a human."* High-Risk list includes legal, healthcare, insurance,
  finance, employment and housing, academic testing, and published journalistic content.
- **Partial self-disclosure, in the policy itself, verbatim:** *"Anthropic's Safeguards Team will
  implement detection and monitoring to enforce our Usage Policy … If we learn that you have violated
  our Usage Policy, we may throttle, suspend, or terminate your access."* It says monitoring happens.
  It does not say a model does it.
- ★ **The model does it, and Anthropic says so in a blog post** (12 Aug 2025), verbatim: *"Our systems
  for detection and enforcement are powered by a set of prompted or specially fine-tuned Claude models
  called 'classifiers'"* and *"We use a combination of automated systems and human review to detect
  harm and enforce our Usage Policy."* Whether a human sees a case before termination is not stated.
- **The cleanest single instance of the asymmetry in the sweep:** a policy that requires the customer
  to announce AI involvement at the start of every session in a high-risk decision, and announces its
  own only in a blog.

### 3. OpenAI — Usage Policies, effective 29 October 2025

- **No general duty on the customer to disclose AI use.** The nearest thing is a prohibition on
  *"automation of high-stakes decisions in sensitive areas without human review"* — a human-review
  duty, not a disclosure duty.
- **Self-description without content, verbatim:** *"We monitor and enforce policies with privacy
  safeguards in place and clear review processes."* And: *"You can appeal if you think we have made a
  mistake enforcing policy."* The review processes are called clear and are not described.
- **Described in a help centre article instead,** verbatim: *"We use automated tools, such as an
  internal version of our /moderations API, to detect content (prompts, completions, uploads) that may
  be harmful"* and *"We use a combination of automated systems and a trained team of experts to review
  these reports."* The second sentence is about **reported** content; for proactive warnings and
  blocks the article does not say whether a human is in the loop first.

### 4. Google — Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy, last modified 17 December 2024

- **Duty on the customer is oblique and heavily qualified:** prohibits *"Misrepresenting the provenance
  of generated content by claiming it was created solely by a human, in order to deceive."* Deception
  is the trigger, so silence is compliant.
- **Nothing at all about Google's own enforcement** — no detection method, no automation, no human
  review, no appeal. The shortest policy in the sweep and the least self-describing.
- ⚠️ I did not chase Google's separate enforcement documentation the way I chased Microsoft's,
  Anthropic's and OpenAI's. **Treat Google's row as "not found in the policy," not as "does not
  exist."** Fix before print if Google is named.

## The pattern, stated precisely

| Vendor | Disclosure duty on customer | Vendor's own AI use in enforcement | Where the self-disclosure lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft (v4.0, 1 May 2026) | Itemised and contractual | Disclosed in detail | Azure AI Foundry docs page |
| Anthropic (eff. 15 Sep 2025) | Explicit, per-session, contractual | Disclosed — Claude classifiers | Blog post, Aug 2025 |
| OpenAI (eff. 29 Oct 2025) | None | Disclosed — internal /moderations | Help centre article |
| Google (17 Dec 2024) | Oblique, deception-gated | Not found | — (not chased) |

**Four for four: the obligation is in the contract and the disclosure is not.** And the two vendors
with the strongest belief in AI disclosure as a principle — Microsoft and Anthropic — impose it most
comprehensively on the party downstream of them.

## What's contested / watch out

- ⚠️ **Do not print "vendors don't disclose."** They do. The claim is about *where*, and the piece
  dies if a reader can produce the Learn page. Print the location, not an absence.
- ⚠️ **Microsoft's abuse-monitoring page covers Azure OpenAI / AI Foundry, not every Microsoft AI
  service.** The Code of Conduct is broader than the page that discloses. Do not let the two documents
  be described as covering the same surface.
- ⚠️ **Contractual status:** the Code of Conduct hangs off the Product Terms; the Usage Policy and
  Usage Policies are terms of use. The Learn page, the blog post and the help article are none of
  those. That distinction is the argument and needs to be stated once, plainly, rather than assumed.
- **Dates are the weak flank.** Google's is 17 Dec 2024, Anthropic's 15 Sep 2025, OpenAI's 29 Oct 2025,
  Microsoft's 1 May 2026. **Re-check every one before print** — an AUP is exactly the kind of page that
  moves silently, and the pipeline has been burned on a two-week-old vendor doc already (Wed 2, xAI).
- **The fair concession, and it should stay in:** Microsoft's disclosure is genuinely good. It names
  the mechanism, names the reviewers, names their access controls and their jurisdiction. A piece that
  reads it as an evasion is misreading it. The complaint is architectural, not moral.

## The economics

There is no price here, and that is the point worth making. A disclosure paragraph costs an afternoon
and nothing per year, which is why its absence cannot be explained by cost and has to be explained by
who was in the room. The real cost sits on the other side: *"may lose access to the Online Service, at
Microsoft's sole discretion"* is, for an MSP, a customer's tenant going dark on a determination whose
method is documented three clicks away from the contract and whose human-review step is the part a
customer can apply to have removed. **That is the operational sentence for his audience**, and it does
not need a dollar figure to land.

## How this joins the piece

MIT wrote the rule and put its own paragraph in Appendix A of the same document. Every vendor here
wrote the rule in one document and its own paragraph in another, unbinding one. **His own policy has
neither.** That is the three-step the piece walks: the institution that did it, the vendors that did
it somewhere else, and the writer who did not do it at all.
