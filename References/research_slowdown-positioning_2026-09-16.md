That piece covered the Washington split up to Mon 14. This note covers what moved Mon 14 – Wed 16, and re-maps the field.

## Bottom line
In four days the argument moved from "is AI dangerous enough to slow down?" to "who is allowed to coordinate the slowing?" Nearly everyone now concedes some form of outside evaluation (even Jensen Huang backs embedded auditors); the live fight is over the antitrust safe harbor that coordinated pacing needs — in substance a shield from the legal exposure (treble damages, enforcement) that agreeing on a speed limit creates — and that is where an unusual left-right bloc (FTC's Ferguson, Treasury's Bessent, Lina Khan, Cohere, Meta) is lining up against the three signatory labs. Zuckerberg's move is the cleanest statement of the counter-position: pace yourself unilaterally, don't ask for permission to pace together. Trust level: the quotes below are solid. The easiest place to get caught is conflating the two liabilities: exposure for coordinating (what the waiver addresses) versus liability for model harms (what critics say must stay).

## The seating chart (six positions)

1. **Coordinated pacers (the signatories).** Amodei's essay (12 Sep): pace capability, not hardware or training per se; three steps — embedded evaluators (unilateral, now), US industry coordination via legislation/standards with capability "checkpoints", then tiered global agreements (ban bio uses → mandatory pre-release testing → RSI speed limits → full pause "unlikely"). Pairs pacing with *harder* China measures (export controls, anti-distillation, weight security). Altman: "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier… we will do the same" on evaluators. Musk: "Dario is right." Hassabis: "direction is correct," pointing to his 14 Jul FINRA-style standards body — which explicitly covers open-weight frontier models.
2. **Pace yourself, don't coordinate (the unilateralists).** Zuckerberg (X, 15–16 Sep): "Every lab has the responsibility and incentive to move at the pace required to train its models safely, and the ability to take its own actions to ensure that happens"; Meta held Muse back for months — "We didn't call for everyone else to do this before we would"; and "committing the significant majority of compute towards serving people rather than racing towards recursive self-improvement is one of the best ways to ensure we develop this technology safely." Nadella: welcomes "deliberate pacing needed to get alignment right" but it "cannot be controlled by a handful of entities"; Microsoft published a draft MAI code of conduct (no shutdown resistance, no hiding reasoning from auditors; six-week comment). OpenAI's Chris Lehane, notably, says the labs already collaborate on safety without needing a waiver.
3. **Safety is engineering, extinction talk is irresponsible (Nvidia).** Huang at All-In (14 Sep): the extinction number is "made up"; told Trump on a live call "We're not going to let it happen, sir." But he *backed* independent auditors embedded in labs, conditioned on independence from "the doomer community," and framed incidents as root-cause-and-institutionalise engineering work.
4. **Regulatory-capture critics (a left-right bloc).** FTC Chair Ferguson (Georgetown, ~14 Sep): "if companies are simultaneously coming to Washington and asking for a host of regulations and an antitrust exemption, all of my alarm bells go off… They're asking for barriers to entry that will insulate their incumbency." Bessent (House Financial Services, 15 Sep): "The best way to guarantee safety is that the creators are liable for what they build"; "We can't let these large labs have regulatory capture." Lina Khan has warned on concentration risk from the Schiff–Banks safe-harbor bill. Aidan Gomez (Cohere): the latest "effort by Silicon Valley incumbents to shape the regulatory landscape." LeCun: "Everyone should make fun of them now." Burry: "how self-serving it is." Sacks: "Just stop pretending that your motives are altruistic."
5. **The accelerationist state.** Trump's seven Truth Social posts (14 Sep): AI risk a "hoax," "the only guardrails AI needs is a strong and smart high-IQ president," Amodei "pretending to be a perfect little angel." Vance: labs begging to be regulated is "a bit of a Trojan horse." DoW CTO: "Americanism, not effective altruism." China mirrors it: Global Times calls the proposals "inappropriate, groundless, and hostile"; MFA's Guo Jiakun: "fear mongering, confrontation, competition will just disrupt [the] process of global AI governance." Z.ai raised $5B with 60% to training incl. a "fully self-training" loop.
6. **The political adopters.** Obama (15 Sep): encouraged the lab leaders "agree on slowing the pace," "neither accelerationist nor doomer," launched Project Blueprint (Ron Conway). Harris added support. Coons attacked the "traitors" language. Sanders and Bannon both slated for an FLI "pro-human" assembly. Politicisation warnings from Solana, Krishnan, Orhan: once it's tribal, half the country opposes it because the other half supports it.

## What's contested / watch out
- **The antitrust ask IS a legal-exposure ask (correction, on Lars's steer 17 Sep).** Read the waiver as a liability shield, not a narrow competition-policy nicety. What coordinating labs are exposed to without it: private Clayton Act suits at treble damages; DOJ/FTC enforcement, where agreeing to restrict output is the kind of conduct treated as per se unlawful; state AGs. The existing shelter (NCRPA notice, which cuts damages to actual damages) expressly excludes agreements restricting members' own R&D or production, so "hold your next model until the group signs off" gets no cap (Business Model Analyst, 16 Sep). Altman has acknowledged coordination "could expose them to antitrust claims if a court found it suppressed competition." Historical warning shot: DOJ v. Automobile Manufacturers Association (1969), a claimed pollution-control "cooperation" treated as a conspiracy to restrain competition, settled by consent decree. The Schiff–Banks bill's AG-notice mechanism is built to cut exactly this exposure.
- **So Bessent's "liability exemption" line is closer to the mark than it first reads, but it conflates two liabilities.** The antitrust shield covers exposure *for coordinating*. It does nothing for product/tort liability *for what the models do* — which is the liability Bessent says should stay ("creators are liable for what they build and generate"). The only product-liability-limiting evidence FedScoop cites is OpenAI backing an Illinois bill. Safe to write: the labs want protection from the legal exposure that coordinated pacing creates. Not safe without attribution: that they want immunity from harms their models cause.
- **Do the labs need the waiver at all?** OpenAI's Lehane says the safety talks don't need one; Amodei asked Congress for "a narrow antitrust waiver covering certain safety conversations"; the Banks/WaPo brief says Google, Anthropic and OpenAI urged lawmakers for clarity; ARI's Doug Calidas: "If they agree to restrict output, that's a pretty classic antitrust violation." Reconciled: sharing safety information and standards is defensible under existing law; agreeing on a speed limit is where the exposure sits. Gomez's framing of the same point: the rules "cannot be written by a small group of commercially aligned companies behind an antitrust waiver."
- **Musk's position.** "Dario is right" is solid; several aggregators (e.g. Plataforma Media) also group him with Altman/Amodei. His companies' shipping cadence (Grok Bot, 4.6) is not paced. Treat as rhetorical endorsement only.
- **Markets read.** SOX −5.9/−6% Mon 14; Intel/AMD/Marvell −5–6%, Nvidia −3%+; Alphabet +2%, Microsoft +1.6%, Meta +1.4% (Fortune). D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria reads it as hyperscalers digesting capex. Selloff cause is contested; don't attribute it solely to the essay.

## The economics
- **Pacing capability ≠ pacing capex.** The essay does not ask anyone to buy fewer GPUs; it asks for time spent on alignment before advancing. Markets priced it as the opposite (chips down, buyers up). If the Lynx supply-chain read holds, the trade is a mispricing — or a bet that political pacing eventually becomes capex pacing.
- **Who a pause pays.** Luria's framing: a coordinated slowdown lets hyperscalers "digest what they have," like Amazon's post-pandemic warehouse freeze — revenue continues on deployed models while capex falls. That is exactly the incumbency economics Ferguson and Burry are pointing at.
- **Zuckerberg's compute-allocation argument** is an economics claim dressed as a safety claim: put most compute into inference for users (revenue) rather than RSI training (risk). It is also the cheapest safety posture for a company whose business is serving 3B+ users.
- **The cost of unilateral pacing is competitive, and China is the price tag.** Z.ai's $5B with 60% to training and a declared self-training loop is the number every "not without China" argument will cite.
- **Liability as the market alternative.** Bessent's and Zuckerberg's positions converge: price the risk through liability and competition, not coordination. For a buyer, that means risk lands in contracts and indemnities, not a regulator's checkpoint.

## Angles this opens
- **"Permission to pace together."** The antitrust-as-liability question is the under-covered one and it fits your Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime thread: coordination to restrict output is textbook cartel conduct; the Schiff–Banks safe harbor is the mechanism; Ferguson, Bessent and Khan are all against it for different reasons. The sharp version: the labs are asking to be shielded from the legal cost of coordinating, while their critics insist the legal cost of harm stays — both can be right at once.
