## §3 Cybernews 63% — CONTESTED. Print the 42%.

**Primary:** Cybernews, 20 Aug 2026, by **Egle Grasys, PR Manager**; *AI Trustworthiness Ranking 2026*.

> "**63%** of the top AI companies don't **clearly** disclose whether they train their AI models on

> "This does not necessarily mean that 63% of companies use user data for AI training."

- ❌ **"Don't disclose" is not what was measured.** 63% = 42% silent + **21% judged "vague."** The
  scoring is Clear/Vague/Missing, and "Clear" requires *"both an explicit answer and, where training
  occurs, a working opt-out."* **So a company that says plainly "yes, we train on your inputs" but
  paywalls the opt-out is counted inside the 63%.**
- ❌ **"Top 500" is circular** — no external league table exists; these are the companies Cybernews
  chose. **And its own FAQ says "650+"** on the same page that says 500.
- ⚠️ **Method:** *"an automated flow… verified by a human."* No named researcher; the byline is a PR
  manager; no scrape date.
- ⚠️ **Commercial interest — three ways.** Legal entity is **"Cybernews, adtech lt, UAB"**; the
  publisher is Mediatech, *"whose investors are the founders of Nord Security"* (NordVPN, Surfshark).
  **Three of four "independent" advisory-board members sit inside that orbit** — two are Mediatech
  staff (one a Technical SEO Manager), and a third is Head of Product & Engineering at **nexos.ai**,
  the Nord founders' enterprise AI-governance venture, **which sells the remedy for the exact gap the
  ranking measures.** The ranking is monetised via a licensed badge (`sales@cybernews.com`), whose

  *(To be fair: no affiliate links inside the article or ranking pages. The exposure is structural.)*
- ❌ **The tool list is not Cybernews'.** Weeve / Paper Trail / Duck AI / Privatemode all come from

  to the analysis.** Caplan's post carries a paid sponsor and at least one affiliate link.
- **Names:** it is **"Privatemode"** (one word) and Proton's **"AI Paper Trail"**. Weeve's primary
  product is a **local-first Mac meeting notetaker**; the anonymiser is free lead-gen. Proton's tool
  is a marketing instrument — a free tool whose output is *"look how much you've exposed,"* from the
  company selling the alternative.

### ★ The nugget — the hard governance version of this story

**EU AI Act Article 53(1)(d)** requires GPAI providers to publish a *"sufficiently detailed summary"*
of training content on the Commission's mandatory template (template published 24 Jul 2025).
Obligation applicable **from 2 August 2025**. **Commission/AI Office enforcement powers switched on
2 August 2026 — three weeks before this brief.** Models on market before 2 Aug 2025 have until
**2 August 2027**. **Enforcement to date: zero. There is no central registry** — the summaries are
*"scattered across each company's website in its own format."*

⚠️ Consistent with the established position: **Digital Omnibus, Reg. (EU) 2026/1744, in force
27 July 2026, deferred Annex III HIGH-RISK obligations to 2 December 2027. It did not touch
Chapter V. Article 53 GPAI and Article 50 transparency are LIVE.** Say which is which explicitly —
the distinction is the story.

> Cybernews found 42% of consumer AI companies don't say whether they train on your data. In the EU,
> for general-purpose model providers, saying so has been mandatory for a year — on a Commission
> template — and the enforcement powers came online on 2 August 2026. There is no registry, no index,
> and zero enforcement actions. The disclosure regime exists, the deadline has passed, and nobody is
> checking.

## §8 Regulatory dates, exact — both of the brief's hooks are wrong as written

### 8.1 "The December deadline" — singular, and narrower than the brief implies

**Obligation:** new **APP 1.7, 1.8 and 1.9**, inserted by the ***Privacy and Other Legislation
Amendment Act 2024*** (Cth), **Schedule 1, Part 15** — "Automated decisions and privacy policies."
**Commencement: 10 December 2026.**

OAIC: *"From 10 December 2026, APP entities that use personal information in ADM with the potential to
affect rights or interests will be required to provide information in their privacy policies."*

**Requires three disclosures in the privacy policy:** the kinds of personal information used in ADM ·
the kinds of decisions made **solely** by a computer program · the kinds of decisions where a program
does something **substantially and directly related** to making the decision.

★★ **WHAT IT DOES NOT REQUIRE — this is the piece.** Per Allens:
> *"Unlike overseas equivalents (such as the EU's GDPR)… it does not provide a right to contest
> decisions or request information, nor does it impose an obligation on organisations to directly
> notify individuals."*

**It is a privacy-policy disclosure obligation and nothing more.** No individual explanation. No right
to human review. No right to contest. No notification at the point of decision. **No power to stop
anything.** ❌ **Any piece that lets a reader infer a GDPR Article 22-style right is wrong.**

**Human-in-the-loop is not an escape hatch:** OAIC reads "substantially" as the automated output being
*a key factor* in a human's decision — so scoring systems with a human rubber-stamp are caught.
⚠️ **Guidance status today: still not final.** Issues Paper 18 May, consultation closed 15 June, final
guidance promised "by September 2026." **Nothing published, 8 days out from the stated deadline.**

⚠️ **Two things the brief may be conflating into "amendments" (plural):**
- **The statutory tort of serious invasion of privacy commenced 10 JUNE 2025** — in force fourteen
  months. **Not a December 2026 item.**
- **The Children's Online Privacy Code:** 10 Dec 2026 is the deadline for the **OAIC to register** the
  Code, not a compliance date. MinterEllison: *"The commencement date of the Code has yet to be
  determined."*

**Safe wording:** *"the automated-decision-making transparency obligation that commences on
10 December 2026"* — singular, named, dated, scoped.

### 8.2 CPS 230 — in force since 1 July 2025, and it does not contain the word "AI"

**"This Prudential Standard commences on 1 July 2025."** Fourteen months in force — **not a
forthcoming deadline.** The service-provider transition (*"the earlier of the next renewal date… or
1 July 2026"*) has also passed.

⚠️ **Verified against the standard's own text: "artificial intelligence", "AI", "algorithm", "model"
and "automated" do not appear anywhere in it.**

❌ **So "maps directly to CPS 230" is false as written — and the honest version is stronger:**
> CPS 230 has been in force since 1 July 2025 and does not contain the word "AI". **That is not a gap
> — it is the point.** It regulates critical operations regardless of what technology runs them, so an
> AI system inside a critical operation inherits every obligation: a defined tolerance level, a tested
> capability to stay inside it, and, where a third party supplies it, a material-service-provider
> agreement with APRA access rights and a working exit. **The question CPS 230 actually forces is not
> "do you have an AI policy" but "can you keep the operation inside tolerance when the model is the
> thing that's failing, and can you exit the provider?"** The authority to halt is an inference from
> that — a well-founded one, and it should be presented as an inference.

### 8.3 ★★ THE NUGGET — three jurisdictions, three answers, one of them actually answers

- **EU AI Act, Article 14(4)(e)**, verbatim from the Commission's own service desk: *"to intervene in
  the operation of the high-risk AI system or interrupt the system through a **'stop' button** or a
  similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state."* **A literal legislated
  stop button.**
- **NIST AI RMF, MANAGE 2.4:** *"Mechanisms are in place and applied, **and responsibilities are
  assigned and understood**, to supersede, disengage, or deactivate AI systems that demonstrate
  performance or outcomes inconsistent with intended use."* ★ **Note the two halves — mechanisms AND a
  named person.** MANAGE 4.1 adds *"appeal and override, decommissioning, incident response,
  recovery, and change management."*
- **Australia:** a privacy-policy disclosure.

> **Europe wrote the stop button into statute. NIST wrote it into a voluntary framework and — the
> sharper half — required that someone be *named* to press it. Australia wrote a privacy-policy
> disclosure. Three jurisdictions, three answers to "who can stop the machine," and only one of them
> actually answers it.**

**That comparison is a piece, it needs no LinkedIn post, and every source in it is a primary text.**
⚠️ ISO/IEC 42001 is paywalled and was not read — **do not print a specific Annex A control number**;
the NIST-published crosswalk is the honest pointer.

## §11 The structural finding — and it is the one worth keeping

**Engine 5 (killed-claim regeneration) has a mirror image, and today produced the first instance of
it: KILLED-CLAIM FALSE POSITIVE.**

A verification pass concluded "no primary exists" from a search that missed the subject's own domain,
her own captions, and a podcast episode published two days earlier. That false negative was then
written into **a research note, two week plans and three drafts** — propagating exactly the way a
regenerating false claim does, through the same machinery, in the opposite direction.

**Why it matters more than an ordinary miss:** every other engine in
`feedback_brief_claim_provenance.md` describes the record becoming **more confident than the
evidence**. This one describes the record becoming **more skeptical than the evidence** — and because
skepticism reads as rigour, **nothing in the existing check catches it.** A do-not-print entry is
never re-tested; it is inherited.

**The fix, and it is small:** a kill based on **absence of evidence** ("no primary states X") is not
the same kind of finding as a kill based on **contradicting evidence** ("the primary says Y"). The
first is provisional and should carry an expiry and a re-check trigger; the second is durable.
**Mark them differently in every future note.** Absence-kills should read *"not found as of
<date> — re-check before reuse,"* never *"the do-not-print stands."*

★ **And the reusable line:** *the four-minute check has a fifth question, and it runs in both
directions — did you look on the subject's own surface, or only on the surfaces that talk about them?*
