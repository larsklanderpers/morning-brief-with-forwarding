# Research Note — NSW AI Operational Policy 2026: supersession, attestation assurance, supplier gap

For: NSW / CPS 230 contrast piece (Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime, target 10–16 or 17–23 Aug) · Depth: deep · Date: 2026-08-06

## Bottom line

Both `[VERIFY]`s resolve, and both resolve in your favour. **DCS-2024-04 is formally archived and superseded by
DCS-2026-02**, and the policy states in terms that it "replaces the NSW AI Ethics Policy" — so the AIAF landing
page citing both is genuinely stale, and so is the Ethics Policy page, which carries no supersession banner at
all. On attestation: it is **self-attestation with no enforcement mechanism**, internal audit is "strongly
recommends" rather than required, and the AI Review Committee's advice on critical-risk systems is **explicitly
advisory** with agencies retaining full decision authority. The Auditor-General has an AI governance audit
*scheduled* for 2026–27 and has not yet reported.

**One finding cuts against the simple version of your thesis and you need it before you draft.** NSW procurement
guidance *does* cover supplier-delivered AI — explicitly, as a named procurement type, under a framework that is
mandatory by statute. So the gap is not "nobody covers vendor AI." It is narrower and better: **vendor AI is
covered at the point of purchase but invisible in the central register**, which is the artefact the Office for AI
reports on and the attestation certifies. That is a *visibility* gap, not a coverage gap. Write it that way or
the first procurement person who reads it takes the piece apart.

**Also, a correction I owe you.** In the vault notes I marked the attestation extension pathway and the
internal-audit recommendation as things I had invented and could not verify. **Both are in the policy, verbatim.**
My first pass at the PDF simply didn't surface them and I over-corrected. They are quoted below and I have fixed
the notes.

## What's solid

- **DCS-2024-04 is Archived, superseded by DCS-2026-02** — status confirmed on the NSW circulars register. Issue
  date of DCS-2024-04 was 1 July 2024; it had itself superseded DCS-2022-01.
  [arp.nsw.gov.au](https://arp.nsw.gov.au/dcs-2024-04-use-of-artificial-intelligence-by-nsw-government-agencies)

- **The circular lineage is four instruments in six years** — DCS-2020-04 (AI Strategy) → DCS-2022-01 →
  DCS-2024-04 (1 Jul 2024) → DCS-2026-02 (2026). Whole-of-government AI obligation, revised roughly biennially,
  carried entirely by circular rather than statute. This is the thread's thesis with a paper trail.
  [arp.nsw.gov.au circulars register](https://arp.nsw.gov.au/dcs-2022-01-use-of-artificial-intelligence-by-nsw-government-agencies)

- **The policy replaces the Ethics Policy, in terms.** *"The policy replaces the NSW AI Ethics Policy and adopts
  Australia's AI Ethics Principles to ensure consistent national alignment."* The Digital NSW policy page repeats
  it: Australia's AI Ethics Principles *"replace the NSW AI Ethics Policy to align NSW with a nationally
  consistent ethics approach."*
  [Policy PDF](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-07/nsw-ai-operational-policy-2026-v1_2.pdf) ·
  [Digital NSW](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/artificial-intelligence/ai-strategy-and-operational-policy/nsw-ai-operational-policy)

- **Two live NSW pages are stale, and they are the load-bearing ones.** The **AIAF landing page** — the page
  hosting the mandatory assessment tool — still cites Circular DCS-2024-04 and the NSW AI Ethics Policy as its
  authority. The **AI Ethics Policy page** carries no status banner, no supersession note, and no reference to
  the Operational Policy or Australia's AI Ethics Principles; it reads as current guidance. Checked 6 Aug 2026.
  [AIAF](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/artificial-intelligence/ai-governance-assurance-and-frameworks/nsw-ai-assessment-framework) ·
  [Ethics Policy](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/artificial-intelligence/artificial-intelligence-ethics-policy)

- **Attestation is self-attestation, and the assurance around it is voluntary.** Verbatim: *"Agencies must provide
  a signed annual attestation for the previous financial year to the NSW Office for AI by 31 October each year,
  commencing with the first reporting date of 31 October 2027."* On assurance: *"The NSW Office for AI strongly
  recommends that agencies consider incorporating compliance with this policy into existing audit, risk and
  assurance programs"* — a recommendation, not a requirement. The document does not say who reviews attestations,
  and does not mention the Audit Office or any independent review. [Policy PDF]

- **There is an extension pathway.** Submitted by the Accountable Official, endorsed by the Agency Head,
  describing which requirements cannot be met and why, plus *"a time-bound remediation plan, including milestones,
  interim controls to manage risk."* *"Requests must be made in writing to the NSW Office for AI via
  oai@customerservice.nsw.gov.au prior to 30 September of every reporting year."* [Policy PDF]

- **No enforcement mechanism, no penalties.** Non-compliance runs through an internal reporting pathway to the
  agency's AI governance board, then to the Agency Head and Accountable Official, and *"The Accountable Official
  must detail any non-compliance in the agency's attestation."* No sanctions, escalation consequences or
  enforcement provisions appear in the document. [Policy PDF]

- **The expert committee's advice is advisory.** AIRC has 12 members, chaired by **Professor Ed Santow** (former
  Australian Human Rights Commissioner), and reviews systems the AIAF rates high or critical. Verbatim: *"The
  advice is intended to support safe, lawful and responsible implementation, while remaining advisory, with
  agencies retaining full decision-making authority and accountability."* No published turnaround times, no case
  counts, no published outcomes, no annual report.
  [AI Review Committee](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/artificial-intelligence/ai-governance-assurance-and-frameworks/ai-review-committee)

- **The Auditor-General is coming but has not arrived.** *Governance of NSW Government agencies' use of artificial
  intelligence and automation* is listed as an **upcoming audit for 2026–27** — announced 10 Sep 2024, page last
  updated 20 Feb 2026, no findings published.
  [Audit Office NSW](https://www.audit.nsw.gov.au/our-work/reports/governance-of-nsw-government-agencies%E2%80%99-use-of-artificial-intelligence-and-automation)

- **The supplier registration exclusion, verbatim:** *"The requirement to register AI use cases does not apply to
  third-party suppliers providing services to the NSW Government where they use AI as part of those services.
  These arrangements are managed through procurement, contracting, and supplier assurance processes."* Scope also
  excludes local government and universities, and excludes *"standard automation and analytics that follow
  predefined logic ... macros, scripts, deterministic rules-based processes, and descriptive analytics."*
  [Policy PDF]

- **Transition wording is open-ended.** *"Agencies will have a transitional period from the time the policy is
  issued"*; the Office for AI *"will issue an implementation guide."* No duration stated; the 31 Oct 2027 date
  *"provides a minimum of 12 months."* During transition agencies *"continue to apply the existing AI Assessment
  Framework Excel-based assessment process to all AI use cases."* [Policy PDF, Digital NSW]

## What's contested / watch out

- **The supplier gap is narrower than "vendor AI is unregulated" — this is the one that can break the piece.**
  NSW's *AI procurement essentials* (last updated **6 May 2026**) names as a primary procurement type:
  *"Platform, service or outcome-based procurement involving AI: Contracting a supplier to deliver a specific
  outcome or deliverable, where the supplier utilises generative AI technologies as part of service provision."*
  It expects an *"AIAF triage"* at planning and an *"AIAF self-assessment"* at sourcing, and the Procurement
  Policy Framework has statutory status under the *Public Works and Procurement Act 1912*, so *"NSW Government
  buyers must comply with its mandatory requirements in every procurement activity."*

  So supplier AI is not unregulated. **It is unregistered.** The defensible claim is about the register and the
  reporting line built on it, not about coverage. Note also that the procurement guidance predates the July 2026
  policy by two months and still describes the AIAF self-assessment world, not the register world — so the
  procurement track and the policy track are not yet reconciled with each other. *That* is the ad hoc regime in
  miniature, and it is a better fact than the one you started with.
  [AI procurement essentials](https://www.info.buy.nsw.gov.au/resources/artificial-intelligence-ai-procurement-essentials)

- **Supplier disclosure of AI use is weaker than the policy implies.** The policy says contracts must support
  *"transparency, including mechanisms for suppliers to disclose AI use and notify the agency of material changes
  over time."* The procurement guidance imposes no blanket supplier-disclosure requirement; the nearest thing is
  that buyers should *"assess the system's ability to notify users they are using AI."* User-facing notification
  is not vendor-to-agency disclosure. Do not let the two blur.

- **"30 September of every reporting year" vs a first-period extension.** The extension text reads as
  general-purpose in one place and first-cycle in another. Minor, but do not assert "one-off grace period"
  without re-reading that section. [Policy PDF]

- **"Less than 30 minutes."** The 27 Jan 2026 media release claims the redesigned framework cuts assessment time
  *"from days to less than 30 minutes"* and *"automatically identifies the right level of oversight and expert
  review."* Treat as a vendor-style claim from the issuer. It sits oddly against a 2.33 MB Excel workbook and
  against 16 questions that trigger a PIA, an HRIA and a cyber review. Useful as a fact *about the claim*, not as
  a fact about the burden.
  [NSW media release](https://www.nsw.gov.au/departments-and-agencies/customer-service/media-releases/nsw-strengthens-ai-oversight-modernised-framework)

## Couldn't verify

- **Number of registered use cases, or how many came back high/critical.** Nothing published. The AIRC page
  publishes no case counts or outcomes; the media release carries no figures; the Auditor-General hasn't reported.
  If you want a number, there isn't one — say so in the piece rather than reaching. This absence is itself the
  strongest available evidence for "the gate is mandatory and nobody can see through it."
- **The attestation template and its evidence requirements.** The policy says *"The template includes the evidence
  requirements that address an agency's compliance with this policy"* but the template is not published on any
  page I could reach. So the evidence standard exists and is not public.
- **The implementation guide.** Promised in the policy; not found published as at 6 Aug 2026.
- **Independent legal commentary on this policy.** Searched; found none. Australian firms have written on NSW WHS/AI
  and on the federal 2026 landscape, but nobody appears to have published on the Operational Policy itself. For a
  whole-of-government mandatory instrument issued a month ago, that silence is notable — and it means you would be
  first, with the corresponding obligation to be right.

## The economics

The cost story is asymmetric, and that asymmetry is the argument.

- **Cost of being in the register: real and recurring.** A high or critical rating pulls a Privacy Impact
  Assessment, a Human Rights Impact Assessment, a cybersecurity review, and an AIRC referral within five business
  days — per use case. Above **$5M** the Digital Assurance Framework adds central oversight. Then the standing
  obligations: tested business continuity plans for AI dependencies on critical services, State Records Act
  retention of AI-informed decisions, and a maintained human or non-AI channel wherever AI faces the public.
- **Cost of being outside it: a procurement checkbox.** Supplier-delivered AI attracts AIAF triage and
  self-assessment at sourcing — one-time, at purchase — and no register entry, no attestation line, no AIRC
  referral, no continuity obligation of its own.
- **So the policy prices in-house AI above bought-in AI**, and the differential is not small. A rational agency
  facing a high-risk AIAF result has an obvious move: buy the outcome rather than run the model. The policy's own
  incentive gradient pushes AI out of the register it just built — which is the same structural joke as the
  federal energy-neutrality rule pushing inference offshore in
  `Signals/Australia mandatory AI standards and Office of AI.md`. Same government, same year, same shape:
  **a control whose price signal defeats the control.** That parallel is yours already and it is the strongest
  frame available for this piece.
- No published unit costs for a PIA or HRIA in NSW government; don't invent one.

## Angles this opens

- **[[Ad Hoc Regulatory Regime]] — the sharpened thesis.** Not "federal and state disagree" but: *the
  technology-agnostic instrument catches AI; the AI-specific one registers only what the agency runs itself.*
  CPS 230 scopes by function and reliance and reaches vendor AI without naming AI. NSW scopes by the technology
  the agency operates and pushes vendor AI onto a procurement track that hasn't been reconciled with the register.
  Four circulars in six years, an advisory expert committee, self-attestation, no penalties, and the
  Auditor-General still a year out. Concede the asymmetry — prudential standard vs public-sector policy,
  service-provider register vs AI register — and argue the mechanism.
- **[[AI Sovereignty]] / the price-signal callback.** The incentive gradient pushes AI out of the register, the
  same way federal energy-neutrality pushes inference offshore. One government, two instruments, both defeating
  themselves on cost. That is a Sunday capstone shape if you want it, not just a Wednesday.
- **A forward hook worth banking.** The Auditor-General's audit is the moment the "nobody can see through the
  gate" claim gets tested. Writing the gap now, before the audit lands, means the piece has a scheduled sequel.
