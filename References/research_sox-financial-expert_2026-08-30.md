# Research Note — the SOX audit-committee-financial-expert precedent
For: `substack_draft_oversight-layer_2026-08-31` (Mon 31, "What we did last time") · Depth: quick-to-deep · Date: 2026-08-30

## Bottom line

**Three of the four sub-claims are now footed to primaries and one is FALSE AS WRITTEN.** The
comply-or-explain mechanism is real, the citation is confirmed, and the SEC said in its own adopting
release that it was not requiring anybody to have an expert. **But the draft's line "Nobody was
compelled to have one" is wrong for listed companies** -- the NYSE and Nasdaq listing standards,
approved later the same year, DO mandate financial expertise, and they are not comply-or-explain.
And the empirical claim underneath the analogy ("visible absences get filled") **cannot be causally
supported**, because the disclosure rule and the hard listing requirement landed within ten months of
each other and no clean attribution survives that. The fix is not to cut the analogy. It is to tell
the sequence in order, which produces a better paragraph and a sharper AI argument.

## What's solid

- **The instrument and the comply-or-explain text.** 17 CFR 229.407(d)(5), verbatim: the registrant
  discloses that its board has determined it either *"Has at least one audit committee financial
  expert serving on its audit committee"* or *"Does not have an audit committee financial expert
  serving on its audit committee"* -- and if it does not, it *"must explain why it does not have an
  audit committee financial expert."*
  [eCFR 17 CFR 229.407](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-229/subpart-229.400/section-229.407)

- **★ THE SEC SAID IT PLAINLY, AND THIS IS THE QUOTE TO USE.** Adopting release, verbatim:
  > **"None of these amendments requires a company to have an audit committee financial expert or a
  > code of ethics."**
  Releases **33-8177 / 34-47235**, File No. S7-40-02, adopted **24 January 2003**, effective
  **3 March 2003**. (A correcting release, 33-8177A, followed on 26 March 2003.)
  [SEC, Disclosure Required by Sections 406 and 407 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2003/03/disclosure-required-sections-406-407-sarbanes-oxley-act-2002)

- **★ THE SAFE HARBOUR, AND IT IS A GIFT TO THIS PIECE.** Also in Item 407:
  > *"The designation or identification of a person as an audit committee financial expert pursuant
  > to this Item 407 does not impose on such person any duties, obligations or liability that are
  > greater than the duties, obligations and liability imposed on such person as a member of the
  > audit committee and board of directors in the absence of such designation or identification."*
  **This pre-answers the first objection any director makes to "name your AI expert": that being
  named makes you the one who gets sued.** The 2003 rule solved that problem explicitly, twenty-three
  years ago. Nobody in the AI-governance conversation is citing it.

- **⚠️ CITATION TRAP -- the Item number MOVED.** When adopted in 2003 the requirement sat at
  **Item 401(h) of Regulation S-K** (Item 401(e) of Reg S-B for smaller issuers). It was relocated to
  **Item 407(d)(5)** by the 2006 executive compensation and related-person disclosure rules.
  **So "Item 407(d)(5)" is correct TODAY and anachronistic for 2003.** Write the mechanism, not the
  section number -- or if a number must appear, say "now codified at Item 407(d)(5)."

- **The exchanges mandate it, and this is the correction the draft needs.** NYSE and Nasdaq listing
  standards, approved by the SEC in **November 2003**, are mandatory conditions of listing, not
  disclosure: NYSE requires audit committee members to be **financially literate** and at least one
  to have **accounting or related financial management expertise**; Nasdaq requires members who can
  **read and understand fundamental financial statements** and at least one who is **financially
  sophisticated** by past experience or background.
  [SEC press release 2003-150](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2003-150.htm) ·
  [Stanford Law, Corporate Governance Standards: Overview](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Corporate-Governance-Standards-Overview.pdf)

## What's contested / watch out

- **The causal claim is confounded and should not be asserted.** "The rule made the absence visible,
  and visible absences get filled" is intuitive and it may well be true, but the disclosure rule
  (January 2003) and a hard listing mandate (November 2003) arrived ten months apart as parts of one
  legislative response. **Any rise in audit-committee financial expertise after 2003 has two
  candidate causes and the data cannot separate them.** Do not write the disclosure rule as the
  thing that worked.
- The academic literature searched on 30 Aug is almost entirely about a DIFFERENT question -- whether
  ACFE *presence* affects earnings management, accruals quality and audit outcomes -- not whether the
  disclosure rule *increased* ACFE presence. That second literature may exist; it did not surface,
  and it is not needed once the paragraph stops making the causal claim.

## Couldn't verify

- A clean before/after series on the share of US listed boards with a financial expert, attributable
  to the disclosure rule alone. Not found, and per the above it is probably not findable.
- The exact SEC release number approving the NYSE/Nasdaq governance standards. The **November 2003**
  date is corroborated across the SEC's own press release and contemporaneous client advisories from
  Katten, Kirkland and others. **Safe: "November 2003." Not safe: a specific release number.**

## The recommended fix, and it is an upgrade rather than a patch

The draft currently reads: *"Comply or explain. Nobody was compelled to have one. The rule simply
made the absence visible, and visible absences get filled, because no board wants to write down that
sentence twice."*

**Replace with the sequence told in order** -- narration, NOT concede-then-sharpen, because that shape
is reserved for Thu 3 this week:

1. The disclosure rule, January 2003, with the SEC's own "none of these amendments requires a company
   to have an audit committee financial expert" as the quote.
2. The safe harbour, because it kills the liability objection in advance.
3. Then November 2003 and the exchange mandate, stated flatly: visibility and a floor arrived in the
   same year from two different directions, and nobody can honestly credit the disclosure rule alone.
4. **The pivot that makes it stronger than the original:** for AI expertise we currently have
   *neither*. Not a floor, and not even the cheap step. The absence is visible in the filings only to
   somebody who goes and counts it, which is what the Conference Board had to do.

**That last move is why this research improves the piece rather than damaging it.** The original
paragraph borrowed authority from a tidy story. The corrected one borrows authority from an accurate
one, and lands on a harder finding: the 2003 response used two instruments and the 2026 response has
deployed zero.

## Drop-in facts (each with a source)

- "None of these amendments requires a company to have an audit committee financial expert or a code
  of ethics." -- SEC Releases 33-8177 / 34-47235, adopted 24 Jan 2003, effective 3 Mar 2003.
- A company without one "must explain why it does not have an audit committee financial expert."
  -- 17 CFR 229.407(d)(5).
- Designation "does not impose on such person any duties, obligations or liability that are greater
  than" those already carried as a director. -- 17 CFR 229.407(d)(5).
- NYSE: financially literate, at least one with accounting or related financial management expertise.
  Nasdaq: able to read and understand fundamental financial statements, at least one financially
  sophisticated. Both mandatory listing conditions, SEC-approved November 2003.

## Angles this opens

- **The safe-harbour beat is a standalone.** "Name the expert and you have named the defendant" is
  the unspoken reason boards resist designation, and US securities law answered it in 2003. That is a
  cheap, concrete, unglamorous governance move nobody in the AI conversation has proposed, and it
  connects straight to the Sun 6 capstone's argument about who can say no.
- **Two instruments, not one** is a reusable frame for the Ad Hoc Regulatory thread: the regimes that
  actually moved behaviour used disclosure AND a floor, and most current AI governance proposals are
  disclosure-only.
