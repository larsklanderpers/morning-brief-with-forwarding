# Pitch — The Policy Does Not Apply to Its Author

Raised by Lars, 4 Sep 2026, from an inbox forward. Not slotted. Not researched. Nothing below is verified.

## The spark

Sam Illingworth, *Slow AI* (Substack), 4 Sep 2026: "Your AI Policy Does Not Apply to the Person Who
Wrote It." Two claims sit under it:

1. **MIT's Aug 2026 committee report on AI in teaching and learning.** §3.1.9 recommends against
   relying on AI detectors — the escalation is unwinnable, false positives land on non-native English
   speakers and neurodivergent students, and the tools miss partial use (outline, edit) entirely.
   §2.3 turns it round: students are frustrated by *instructors'* AI use in grading, feedback and
   assignment creation, and the report recommends instructors be transparent when they use AI to
   evaluate. The report calls the two-way result "an underground river of mutual suspicion."
2. **Illingworth's own Feb 2026 sweep of all 163 UK degree-awarding institutions, published with HEPI
   in May.** 96 had a policy a student, parent or regulator could find; 67 had nothing. Of 19 read
   line by line, 8 were detection-and-discipline written in the language of learning. **Not one asked
   staff to declare their own AI use.**

He also prints a four-question template for writing a disclosure statement in ten minutes (which
tools by name · which parts of the job · what you kept by hand · what would change your mind, with a
review date) and a blank fill-in version.

## Why it is a piece and not a repost

The generalisable claim is one line: **the person setting the rule is almost never inside it.** That
is not an education story. It is the missing rung in the 7–13 Sep spine, which is *the controls
everybody relies on were put there by somebody else, on somebody else's terms* — this is the control
**you** put there, and then stood outside.

Lars's ground: directors signing off an acceptable-use policy for staff while using the tools in
board papers and performance reviews; an MSP that both issues customer-facing AI policy and receives
it from its own suppliers. Illingworth's own commercial line is the one that lands hardest for that
audience: commission work and your suppliers are guessing at your position and pricing the risk in,
so a disclosure line at the bottom of a proposal is a competitive advantage precisely because nobody
else writes one.

★ **The first-person asset nobody else has:** Lars is mid-cohort in an MIT Professional Education
programme on applied agentic AI while MIT's own committee tells instructors to declare. He can put
the report's question to his own course and print the answer, or the silence. **His call** — it names
an institution he is enrolled with.

## The beat that makes it his, not Illingworth's

Illingworth stops at "disclosure is better than detection." **It is, and a disclosure statement is
also cheap to write and unfalsifiable.** "I used Claude to pressure-test the argument" is not
auditable either; it is a claim about a process nobody watched. A piece that only endorses disclosure
is one rung above the thing it criticises. Naming that is what stops this reading as a repost, and it
is the same shape as the pipeline's live instrument thread.

   MIT report and the HEPI publication as primaries; do not carry him as the finding.
2. **Every quotation above is the newsletter's rendering.** §3.1.9, §2.3 and the fall 2025 Tech Survey

3. **"Not one of 163" is an absence claim** — one researcher, one snapshot, UK only, Feb 2026. Print it
   as what he found and when, never as a fact about the sector now. Per `feedback_killed_claim_false_positive`,
   absence claims need an expiry.
4. Do not print the detector false-positive material as a finding about detector accuracy in general;
   the report's own hedge is that the tools are "reasonably accurate" on purely AI-generated text and
   fail on the mixed case.
