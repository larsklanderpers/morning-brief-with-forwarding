# Research Note — the AI policy that exempts its author
For: `pitch_policy-exempts-its-author_2026-09-04.md` (proposed Tue 15 Sep) · Depth: deep, both primaries fetched · Date: 2026-09-05

## Bottom line

**Both primaries are real, fetched and quotable — and the newsletter that raised this got two
things wrong in ways that make the piece better, not worse.** MIT's report says everything
Illingworth says it says about detectors, and its instructor-disclosure recommendation is real but
lives in **§3.2.3**, not §2.3 (§2.3 carries the diagnosis). More importantly: **MIT's report
discloses its own AI use, in Appendix A, verbatim and specifically** — so the report is not an
example of the gap, it is the worked example of the fix, and Illingworth does not mention it.
Meanwhile the load-bearing sentence of his post — *not one of the 163 policies asked staff to
declare their own AI use* — **is nowhere in his published HEPI research**, is not in his open
dataset's analysis, and is not in the Times Higher coverage. It appears to exist only in the
newsletter. His published finding is different and better sourced, and it is the one to print.

## What's solid

### MIT — Report of MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training, 13 Aug 2026

Primary fetched: `https://sites.mit.edu/ai-use/files/2026/08/AI-Committee-Final-Report-Aug-13.pdf`
(302s to `bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.mit.edu/dist/d/2418/...`; fetch the redirect target directly).
Committee: five months, Jan–Aug 2026, weekly meetings in spring; surveys, separate graduate and
undergraduate listening sessions, peer policy review; members drawn from students, faculty across
all five schools, and Libraries / Teaching Lab staff.

- **§3.1.9 recommends against relying on AI detectors, verbatim:** *"we recommend against relying on
  AI detectors, as it risks an arms race in which students respond to automated detection by using
  increasingly powerful 'AI humanizers' to remove signals that AI detectors are cued to catch. The
  result: a lot of effort on both sides that in the end serves no one."*
- **The hedge is in the same section and must travel with the claim:** *"Many tools purport to detect
  AI use, and some are reasonably accurate when given a piece of purely AI-generated text. However,
  most real-life situations are more nuanced: If a student uses AI to complete an outline or edit a
  section, these tools are unlikely to uncover it."* And: *"No doubt the tools will improve."*
- **False positives, verbatim:** *"AI detection systems may also mistake the writing of non-native
  English speakers or neurodivergent students for text generated by AI. Even low rates of false
  positives can put students on edge and cause serious individual consequences."*
- **The policing beat, verbatim:** *"Students should not feel policed. Durable change will require
  instructors to be as clear as possible about their expectations and students to understand AI
  misuse as an unacceptable deviation from shared peer norms and community values rather than a
  violation of an arbitrary bureaucratic rule."*
- **§2.3 is the diagnosis, verbatim:** *"For their part, students fear being wrongly accused of AI
  cheating and are increasingly frustrated by instructors' use of AI in areas that demand a human
  touch, such as grading, assignment creating, and feedback."* … *"Such an underground river of
  mutual suspicion is no foundation for a healthy classroom."*
- ⚠️ **§3.2.3 is where the recommendation actually lives** — headed *Encourage instructor disclosure
  around their own AI use* — and the newsletter files it under §2.3. Verbatim: *"Just as MIT needs
  norms around students' AI use, the community also needs to agree and adhere to norms on the
  appropriate use of AI by instructors. If instructors plan to present students with content
  substantially generated by AI, or to use AI for some aspect of evaluation, grading, or feedback, we
  strongly recommend that they be transparent with their students about how and why AI is being
  used."* **It is addressed to instructors, not to administrators or staff generally.** That
  limitation is worth printing: even the good version stops at the teaching relationship and never
  reaches the people who commissioned the report.
- **§3.2.4, the survey line, verbatim:** *"In the fall 2025 Tech Survey, more than two-thirds of
  students who responded felt that AI would be important in their careers, yet only 25% felt that
  MIT was adequately preparing them to use AI."* Sole quantitative Tech Survey figure in the report.
  No response count or denominator given — **do not print a percentage-of-students-at-MIT claim**;
  it is of students who responded.

### ★ The best find, and Illingworth does not have it — MIT disclosed its own AI use

**Appendix A, verbatim:** *"AI Use: None of the text of this report was generated with AI. We did
supply early drafts of the report to ChatGPT to identify redundant / overlapping sections. Some of
the graphs and statistical summaries in Appendix C were generated with Codex (OpenAI's AI coding
tool). Members of the committee also used AI to synthesize and present data and reports (e.g.,
summaries of other schools' published positions on AI) to inform committee discussions."*

That statement does, unprompted, all five things Illingworth's own template asks for: it names the
tools, names the tasks, names what did not happen, is checkable, and keeps the liability. **The
committee that wrote the rule put itself inside it.** This inverts the newsletter's framing and is
the stronger spine: the question for a board is not *why don't policies cover staff*, it is *your
policy has no Appendix A*.

### HEPI Policy Note 71 — Sam Illingworth, "What UK university AI policies actually do: A study of 96 institutions," May 2026

Primary fetched: `https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/What-UK-university-AI-policies-actually-do-A-study-of-96-institutions.pdf`
Open data: `github.com/sam-illingworth/uk-university-ai-policies`, CC BY 4.0, full `corpus.csv`.

- **163** UK institutions with degree-awarding powers searched, **16 February 2026**. **96** had a
  publicly accessible policy. **67** — 41%, "two in five" — *"either had no discoverable policy or one
  locked behind authentication walls (requiring a university login to access), broken URLs or content
  that loads dynamically."* ⚠️ That is a **findability** finding, not a no-policy finding. Print it
  as "could not be found by someone looking," which is the honest and still-damning version.
- **Computational framing analysis of the 96:** 83 (86%) education-dominant, 9 (9%) balanced, 4 (4%)
  detection-dominant. Vocabularies: *"34 terms associated with detection and surveillance (such as
  'plagiarism', 'misconduct', 'penalty', 'Turnitin' and 'zero tolerance') and 43 terms associated with
  education and support (such as 'critical thinking', 'literacy', 'agency', 'assessment design' and
  'student voice')."*
- **Qualitative coding of 19 policies, 31-code framework, verbatim:** *"of 19 policies I coded, five
  were genuinely educational, four were performatively educational, two were balanced and eight were
  detection-oriented."* So **8 detection-oriented**, and **12 of 19** where the computational
  classification was misleading once the performative four are counted. The newsletter prints the 8;
  the 12 is the bigger number and is the author's own.
- **★ The published staff finding — this is the one to use, and it is sharper than the claim it
  replaces.** The note's University of East Anglia example: the same green-light / red-light framework
  is applied to staff and students, *"But the register shifts depending on who is being addressed. The
  student section is directive and rule-bound. The staff guidance, written by senior academics, is
  rich in pedagogical reasoning, assessment redesign strategies and disciplinary examples."* And
  verbatim: **"Staff are addressed as professionals trusted with judgement. Students are given
  instructions."**
- **Author's declared interest, verbatim:** he *"excluded my own institution, Edinburgh Napier
  University, from the qualitative sample to avoid a conflict of interest; its policy remains in the
  computational analysis."* No funding declared in the note.
- Independent coverage: Times Higher Education, *"University AI policies 'promise support but deliver
  surveillance'"* — carries 163 / 96 / 67 (41%), his line *"They promise critical thinking but deliver
  audit trails. They name support yet deliver surveillance,"* the Southampton example, four commended
  policies (Durham, Stirling, Canterbury Christ Church, Arts University Plymouth), and the finding that
  no sector body coordinated any of it. **No pushback from any university or sector body found.**

## What's contested / watch out

- ⚠️⚠️ **"Not one of the policies asked that of staff" is NOT in the published research.** Searched
  the HEPI note, the open repository's analysis, and the THE coverage. The repo's own framing is
  education-vs-detection and deficit-model coding; it *"does not appear to contain specific analysis
  distinguishing whether policies impose disclosure or declaration obligations on staff versus
  students."* The claim exists in the Substack post and, as far as this pass can find, nowhere else.
  **Do not print it as a finding of the study.** Two honest routes: attribute it to him personally as
  a claim he makes about his own corpus ("Illingworth, writing about his corpus in September, says…"),
  or use the published UEA/register finding instead, which is better anyway.
- ★ **And it is testable.** He published the full corpus under CC BY 4.0. A claim about 96 policies,
  made by the person who released the machine-readable text of all 96 and did not test it in the
  paper, is a story on its own — and the pipeline could actually run the test. **Flagging, not
  recommending: that is a research task with a real chance of refuting the piece's own spark.**
- **Rule 2 — name the commercial interest.** The post sells *Slow AI* (book, "#1 Amazon bestseller in
  Education", two buy links) and a paid CPD-accredited programme, twice, inside the argument. The
  HEPI note itself declares no funding and does declare the Napier recusal, so **the research is
  cleaner than the newsletter carrying it.** Cite the note; do not cite the post as evidence.
- **The detector material is not a general finding about detector accuracy.** MIT's own hedge is that
  the tools are reasonably accurate on purely AI-generated text and fail on the mixed case, and that
  they will improve. Print the hedge or the claim is over-asserted.
- **Sector scope.** Everything here is universities. The transfer to a board or an MSP is Lars's
  argument, not the report's, and has to be made as an argument rather than smuggled as a finding.

## The economics

No cost curve in either primary; the economics of this piece are procurement, not inference. The
usable frame is Illingworth's own and it is the strongest line in the post for Lars's audience:
commission work, and suppliers who cannot see your position guess at it and price the risk in. A
disclosure paragraph at the bottom of a proposal is a differentiator precisely because it is cheap to
write and almost nobody writes one. That cuts both ways for an MSP — Tech Results issues AI policy to
customers and receives it from its own vendors — and the asymmetry is the piece's commercial beat. No
figure exists for any of this; do not manufacture one.
