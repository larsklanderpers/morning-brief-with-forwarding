## What's contested / watch out

- **The "Lenny's IT-satisfaction episode" label doesn't quite match the source.** The Stone
  episode is about *agency and accountability inside Netflix's culture*, not a measured claim that
  *IT/worker satisfaction rises when people keep agency over AI tools.* If what you want is the
  latter — a satisfaction/engagement *finding* — that's a different body of work (the DX / Laura
  Tacho developer-experience research, which lives on the *Engineering Enablement* and *Pragmatic
  Engineer* podcasts, **not** Lenny's). Decide which leg you actually want: Stone gives you a
  quotable *principle* (agency = accountability); DX gives you a *number* (satisfaction/productivity
  effects). Don't fuse the two into "a Lenny's episode found that…" — that would be a
  paraphrase-as-fact error the skeptic seat would catch.

- **"Two types of users."** Confident this is your framing of the productivity-divide (deploy-to-

  your corpus or the web. If you want it to carry weight as *someone's finding* rather than your
  own reframe, flag it and I'll hunt the origin. As your own reframe, it's fine unsourced.

## The economics (the leadership-deployment cost angle)
The through-line that keeps this from being a soft "engagement" piece: **cognitive disengagement is
a cost leaders choose, usually without pricing it.** Mollick's move reframes the scarce, expensive
skill as *management* (define the goal, set the quality bar, evaluate the output) — which every
experienced professional already has, so the "we can't find AI talent" spend is often misdirected.
The Stone frame prices the other side: strip agency/accountability out and you don't save money, you
buy worse output *and* a checked-out workforce (the double loss). The deployment decision — tools
that keep judgment engaged vs. tools that retire it — is the line item nobody puts on the invoice.
