## THE SPINE THAT IS AVAILABLE WITHOUT TOUCHING X-RISK

### 1. The government wrote its own hands out of the deal, in its own EO
**Executive Order 14409, 2 June 2026**, §3(c), verbatim:
> **"Nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental
> licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or
> distribution of new AI models, including frontier models."**
§3(b) makes the access framework **"voluntary."** §2(d)'s clearinghouse is *"in voluntary collaboration
with the AI industry."* The one element with teeth — the §3(a) benchmarking process — is **classified**,
with NSA setting the threshold.
★ **The whole piece in one document: the federal government took the problem seriously enough to write an
executive order, then explicitly disclaimed the power to compel anything, and classified the part that
bites.** That is far better than "the government is doing nothing." It did something, and what it did was
choose non-binding.

## THE COUNTER-CASE, AND IT IS STRONG
- **The patchwork is real, not rhetorical.** **Rep. Kat Cammack (R-FL):** *"The idea that we're not going
  to have any sort of framework or guardrails, that's just not realistic. But there's also the real
  concern… particularly startups — the companies are not going to be able to have a framework for 50
  different states and really survive."*
- **Preemption has carve-outs.** **OSTP Director Michael Kratsios:** *"we can't have preemption without
  other stuff attached to it… We're not going to be preempting the way that an individual state government
  can procure AI."* EO 14365 does carve out child safety, compute and data-centre infrastructure, and
  state procurement.
- **Hawley, of all people, makes the best pro-federal-standard case:** *"calling on Congress not just to
  say no to a bunch of state laws, but to put down a federal standard. That's a very different thing."*
- ★★ **The steel-man he must answer, and it attacks his own thesis.** From defence-side counsel, who have
  no reason to flatter AGs: *"State AGs possess broad enforcement authority, flexible legal tools and the
  ability to act quickly… civil investigative demands, subpoena documents and compel testimony."* **UDAP
  statutes are open-textured BY DESIGN, precisely because legislatures cannot anticipate every novel
  harm.** The AGs moved in **weeks**. Congress has moved in **fourteen months** and produced one committee
  vote. **On raw responsiveness the improvised instrument beat the designed one.**
- **His available reply, and it is a good one:** consumer-protection law compels *documents, after the
  fact*. It cannot set a pre-deployment standard, cannot compel site access mid-incident, cannot require
  reporting on a clock, and produces fifty different answers. **It is forensic, not preventive.**
  **We built compelled-access regimes for aviation (49 USC 1113/1134) and chemical plants (42 USC
  7412(r)(6)) and not for this.** That closing argument is politically homeless, which is exactly what
  makes it printable to this readership.

## 🇦🇺 THE AU PARALLEL — and it is the closing move, not a footnote
**Australia ran the experiment first and abandoned it first.** September 2024: the Department of Industry
consulted on **"Introducing mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings"** — a standalone AI Act with
binding obligations. **December 2025: the National AI Plan dropped them**, replacing the regulatory pillar
with *"regulate as necessary but as little as possible."*
★★ **That is the same choice EO 14409 §3(c) makes, arrived at independently, on the other side of the
world, within six months.** His AU readers are not watching a US dysfunction story from a safe distance —
**they are eighteen months further into the same experiment.** ⚠️ Sting in the tail: US firms are eyeing
Australia for AI data centres partly because it is *"politically stable."* **Being the jurisdiction that
chose not to build the instrument is, for now, a selling point.**
