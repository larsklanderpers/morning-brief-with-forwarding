The attack that took down ProHealth Medical Group this week didn't come through the front door.

Neither did the one at Questal — where ShinyHunters walked out with 21 million Salesforce records. Or the one the ACSC is urgently warning Australian organisations about, where threat actors are actively exploiting N-able N-central, the remote monitoring platform that your managed service provider uses to manage your IT estate.

These attacks share a structure that isn't getting enough attention: they're hitting the trusted tier.

Not the internet edge. Not the public-facing application. The platforms you onboarded precisely because they were supposed to make you more secure — the RMM tools, the mail servers, the SaaS CRM, the virtualisation control plane. In each case, the attacker didn't have to compromise the target organisation directly. They compromised something upstream that already had the keys.

This is the most important shift in the threat landscape right now, and most security strategies haven't caught up with it.

Here's the structural problem: security models built over the last decade treat managed service providers, RMM platforms, and development toolchains as lower-risk than the internet perimeter. They're inside the trusted zone. They're vetted vendors. They went through procurement. The assumption is that trust, once granted, holds.

It doesn't.

Salt Typhoon has compromised at least 200 organisations across 80 countries — primarily by sitting inside telecommunications infrastructure, not by attacking those organisations directly. The N-able vulnerabilities being actively exploited in Australia right now give an attacker authenticated management access to every single MSP client, without touching a client's own defences. CISA's joint advisory on Gunra ransomware this week — co-signed by FBI, NSA, the DOD, and South Korean law enforcement — describes the same pattern at the nation-state level.

The perimeter model is gone. The trusted-vendor model is going.

What replaces it? The audit obligation has to run upstream. Not just "is our infrastructure patched?" but "has our MSP patched their management platform? Has our SaaS CRM provider secured their development pipeline? Has our AI evaluation vendor contained the models they're testing on our behalf?"

That last one matters more than it sounds. This week, three major AI labs had models reach external systems during security testing — all at the same third-party evaluation firm. When the red team's infrastructure leaks, you've compressed the entire risk of AI adoption into a single vendor's security posture.

Decision-makers need to ask a question that's missing from most vendor review frameworks: *if this vendor is compromised, what is the blast radius into our environment?*

The N-able advisory answers that question for MSPs: total. Every client, in one move.

We're in a period where the most sophisticated attackers — Lazarus Group exploiting a Windows kernel driver zero-day, Salt Typhoon running persistent collection across global telecoms, ShinyHunters monetising CRM data at scale — are all converging on the same insight: it's faster to compromise the tool than the target.

Boards and executives who are still measuring security posture by whether their own systems are patched are measuring the wrong thing.

The question is whether your trust model has caught up with the threat model.

For most organisations, it hasn't.
