Your malware protection engine is the last thing that's supposed to fail.

On August 11, Microsoft disclosed a vulnerability in the Defender Malware Protection Engine. Two and a half hours later, a working exploit was public.

Not two weeks. Not two days. Two and a half hours.

That is the new baseline. And if your organisation is still running a 30-day patch cycle, you are not late — you are in the window that threat actors are actively using.

This week produced three separate data points that, taken together, change the strategic framing:

**First:** CISA, the NSA, and the FBI released a joint advisory confirming that threat actors are deploying AI-generated exploitation scripts against Siemens S7 PLCs across US critical infrastructure — energy, water, manufacturing, chemicals. The actors use Censys and ZoomEye to find exposed devices, then push AI-crafted payloads disguised as monitoring tools. This is not a theoretical concern anymore. It is a named advisory with named mitigations.

**Second:** ChainDrop, a self-propagating npm worm that began August 4, compromised 444 packages and over 2,200 versions in under four hours. The worm stole developer credentials, used them to publish poisoned packages with valid provenance attestations — bypassing the supply chain controls we've been told to implement — and pivoted its entire command-and-control infrastructure via a single Ethereum transaction. There is no registrar to call to take that down.

**Third:** IBM's X-Force 2026 Threat Index reports a 44% year-on-year increase in attacks starting with public-facing application exploitation, driven primarily by AI-enabled vulnerability discovery that compresses the exploit development timeline to hours.

The pattern across all three is the same: AI has removed the skills bottleneck that used to slow attackers down.

Sophisticated nation-state OT attacks required specialist ICS knowledge. ChainDrop-style worm development required significant malware engineering capability. Zero-day exploit development required rare reverse engineering talent. All three of those barriers are lower than they were 18 months ago, and dropping.

The operational implication for boards and executives isn't complicated, even if execution is hard:

The patching SLA conversation needs to happen at board level. If your current SLA is 30 days for critical vulnerabilities, that number was set in a world where a PoC appearing two hours after disclosure was unusual. It isn't anymore.

Supply chain controls need to be reassessed. Provenance attestation, which was positioned as the answer to supply chain attacks, does not help when the signing credentials are stolen. The control needs to be at the level of dependency behaviour monitoring and immutable build environments, not just signed packages.

OT "air gaps" need to be audited, not assumed. The CISA/NSA advisory on Siemens PLCs is specifically notable because the attack path starts with internet scanning — meaning the devices are reachable. If your organisation has OT assets, the question for your security team this week is: what does Censys see when it scans your IP ranges?

The threat landscape is not getting more complex because the problems are getting harder. It's getting more complex because AI has handed capable, motivated actors the tools to move at machine speed — and most organisational security processes still run at human speed.

That gap is the problem worth solving.
