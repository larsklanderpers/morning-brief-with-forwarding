This week, attackers breached a Check Point security management server, an F5 access proxy, a VeloCloud SD-WAN orchestrator, and an AI API gateway.

That's not a list of targets. That's the security stack itself.

We've spent a decade building layered defences — firewalls, proxies, access brokers, security management planes — and now those layers are the attack surface. The adversarial playbook has evolved: don't fight through the perimeter. Own the infrastructure that controls it.

The pattern is hard to ignore when you look at this week's incidents together. CVE-2026-93616 in Check Point's Security Management Server gave attackers unauthenticated script execution against the policy plane — the system that manages security for the entire network. CVE-2026-93952 in VeloCloud Orchestrator scored a perfect CVSS 10.0 and is under active exploitation — own the SD-WAN controller, own the branch networking visibility. F5 BIG-IP APM's zero-day (CVE-2026-94127) is in the access proxy — the system that decides who gets in. CISA issued an emergency directive: federal agencies have until Friday.

The ShinyHunters FBI breach via Oracle PeopleSoft adds another dimension. The group claims 2–3TB of FBI employee and applicant data, and has signalled it's using the same zero-day against Fortune 500 companies right now. An HR and identity management platform — not production infrastructure — became the entry point to AWS GovCloud.

Concurrently, AI is reshaping the threat calculus in ways that most organisations are still underestimating. BCG's 2026 data puts it plainly: 89% of companies experienced AI-enabled attacks last year. Microsoft's Digital Crimes Unit just dismantled EvilTokens — an AI-powered phishing-as-a-service platform that compressed high-sophistication token theft down to a commodity any criminal could operate. And this month's Microsoft Patch Tuesday included 20 wormable vulnerabilities, a volume researchers attribute partly to AI-assisted bug discovery accelerating what attackers (and researchers) can find.

Here's the strategic question that should be on every board agenda this week: **When the attack surface is your security infrastructure, what assumptions in your architecture are you no longer entitled to make?**

Most security frameworks still treat the control plane as trusted. Most vulnerability programmes prioritise production applications over security tooling. Most AI governance frameworks don't exist yet — the CSA found that only 23% of organisations are monitoring AI agents for threats, even as 77% have integrated generative AI into their security stacks.

Three things I'd be doing this week if I were a CISO:

First, audit the security infrastructure itself — management servers, access proxies, identity providers, SD-WAN controllers — as primary targets, not just as tools. They need the same isolation, monitoring, and patch prioritisation as production crown jewels.

Second, treat AI governance as a board-level risk item, not an IT initiative. The 89% AI-attack figure means you are already operating in an AI-threat environment. The 41% governance policy figure means most organisations are doing so without a framework.

Third, review Oracle PeopleSoft and legacy enterprise platform exposure today. If ShinyHunters is using a PeopleSoft zero-day against Fortune 500 companies, they are not advertising their target list in advance.

The threat landscape in September 2026 is a stress test of architectural assumptions that haven't been revisited since the perimeter was the primary defence concept. The organisations that weather it best will be the ones that started treating their security stack with the same adversarial thinking they apply to everything else.
