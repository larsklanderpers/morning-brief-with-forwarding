**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Late on Sunday night, people in Kyiv got an alert on the app they rely on to survive missile strikes. It warned of dangerously high radiation in the capital.

It was fake. So were the chemical threat and the "public disturbances". On the same night, Russia's Emergency Ministry app sent its own users fake alerts.

Nobody stole a database. The attackers went for something more valuable: the one notification people are trained never to ignore.

I keep coming back to that, because it is the pattern in almost every security story I read this morning.

Qantas confirmed it has been caught up in the Amadeus breach. Its own systems were not touched. Amadeus, the global booking platform, says the problem was contained in September. Qantas is still working out whether its customers were affected, and it is the Qantas name that will be on any notice.

BTCPay Server, an open-source Bitcoin payment tool, told every operator to switch their servers off after reports of hot wallets being drained. No root cause yet. No victim count.

A researcher showed that GitHub Copilot in Visual Studio could be tricked into rewriting its own configuration file, because the safety check compared a filename string and Windows quietly drops a trailing dot. One character, and the agent's guard rail was gone. Microsoft fixed it on 13 October.

And Radware reported 83 booby-trapped GitHub issues and comments, posted across 61 projects in 43 minutes, aimed at build pipelines that feed strangers' text straight into shell commands.

Different industries. Different attackers. Same shape.

None of these attacks broke the thing at the centre. They went for the messenger: the alert app, the booking platform, the payment server, the coding assistant, the build bot. The intermediary is trusted by default and, in most organisations, owned by nobody in particular.

That is the gap.

We spend our security budgets defending the core: the customer database, the payment system, the domain controller. We are getting better at it. Attackers have noticed, and they are going around.

Every organisation now runs on a chain of trusted intermediaries. Vendors who hold your customer data. Tools that act on your behalf. Apps that speak to your customers in your name. Agents that write code in your developers' IDEs. Each one can lie to you, or lie for you, and most of them would do it in your brand's voice.

In my world, gaming and hospitality in Australia and the US, that chain is long. Member data passes through loyalty platforms, booking systems, payment processors and marketing tools. When one of them is breached, the Privacy Act does not care whose server it was. The customer gave the data to you.

So here is the question I would put to any board this week. Not "are we patched?" but "which of our trusted messengers could be turned against us, and how would we know?"

Three things follow from that:

1. Map your intermediaries. Every vendor, tool and agent that holds your data or acts in your name. Most organisations cannot produce this list in a day.

2. Give each one an owner. Not "IT". A named person who knows what normal looks like and is accountable when it changes.

3. Build an independent fallback. When the Ukrainian app lied, the government fell back on sirens, SMS and broadcast within hours. What is your siren when your notification channel, your booking partner or your payment provider goes dark or goes rogue?

The attackers have already done this mapping for you. They just have not shared it.
