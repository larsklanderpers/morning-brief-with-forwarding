**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Your firewall, your email filter and your backup server walked into a CISA alert together this week.

On 14 October, CISA added four actively exploited flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. One in Palo Alto's PAN-OS. One in Cisco's Secure Email Gateway. Two in AhsayCBS, a backup platform.

Look at that list as a CTO, not as a patch queue.

Those are the three components every incident-response plan assumes are still on your side when things go wrong. The firewall tells you what came in. The mail filter is meant to catch it. The backups are how you recover without paying anyone.

Lose all three and you are not running an incident. You are running a negotiation.

The Cisco flaw is the one I keep coming back to. The email gateway's whole job is to open hostile attachments from strangers. A parser bug there gives an unauthenticated attacker root. And by BleepingComputer's account, it is not the first exploited zero-day in that product line recently.

When the same control fails twice in a short window, the board question changes. It stops being "are we patched?" and becomes "how long would it take us to notice if this happened again?"

Now add a second story from the same 24 hours.

Booz Allen ran frontier AI models against an isolated OT lab. Across eight scenarios, the models met every objective. In one, they went from a perimeter compromise to actions inside an industrial control network in about 16 minutes. In another, a model found a robotic arm, mapped its safety zones, and moved it.

Two caveats, stated plainly. Booz Allen sells security services, and the coverage does not name the models or the scenarios. Discount the framing accordingly.

But keep the timing.

The expensive part of attacking an industrial system has always been understanding an unfamiliar plant. That took specialists and weeks. If an agent compresses it to minutes, the skills moat around OT is gone. What remains is segmentation, and a human approving anything that moves physical equipment.

This lands the same week as the cyberattack that shut down refrigeration in hundreds of French supermarkets, and the Australian Cyber Security Centre's OT alert that followed it. For anyone running venues, cool rooms or building management on vendor remote access, this is not abstract.

The third thread is quieter. The FBI seized nearly 187 laptops from farms used to let North Korean operatives pose as US remote workers. Every one of those laptops was shipped by a hiring manager who believed they had onboarded a real person.

Here is what connects them.

None of these were novel attacks. Each one exploited a place where trust was assumed and never re-checked. The firewall console reachable from the internet. The mail filter nobody tests. The backup server run by a provider you have never audited. The remote hire you have never seen on an unscheduled video call.

And AI is shrinking the time between "assumed" and "exploited."

So my ask for decision-makers this quarter is not another tool. It is a list.

Write down every component in your environment that you trust by default. Then mark which ones you have independently verified in the last twelve months.

The gap between those two columns is your real attack surface. It is also cheaper to close than any single incident on this week's list.
