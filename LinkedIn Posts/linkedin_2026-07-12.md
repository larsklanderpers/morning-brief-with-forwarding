Imagine a bank where the robbers stopped attacking the vault and started forging the approval stamps instead.

That's this week in cybersecurity.

Wiz researchers just disclosed "GhostApproval" — a flaw in AI coding assistants from Amazon, Anthropic, Cursor, Google and others that lets a malicious code repository quietly bypass the human approval step and write an attacker's SSH keys straight onto a developer's machine.

Read that again. The human-in-the-loop — the one safeguard every vendor points to when they tell you agentic AI is safe for the enterprise — can be routed around by a symlink.

And it wasn't the only trust mechanism that failed this week.

A hacker calling themselves "888" claims to have lifted 35 GB from Accenture: source code, RSA and SSH keys, Azure access tokens. Accenture says it's an "isolated matter." Maybe. But a consultancy's credentials aren't one company's problem — they're a master key cut for hundreds of client environments. If your firm works with any major consultancy, your secrets-rotation policy just became very relevant.

Meanwhile, the Australian Cyber Security Centre issued a critical alert: a global campaign is compromising thousands of small-business websites through unpatched CMS platforms — WordPress, Joomla, Craft. Not to steal from those businesses. To turn their trusted websites into distribution infrastructure for the next wave of attacks.

And Microsoft? A SharePoint remote-code-execution flaw is now confirmed under active exploitation — a flaw Microsoft patched in May but didn't tell anyone about for weeks, after rating exploitation "less likely." Attackers diff the patches whether the bulletin exists or not. Silence only blinds defenders.

Four stories. One pattern.

Attackers are no longer going after your data directly. They're going after the machinery you use to decide what's trustworthy: the approval gate on your AI tools, the credentials of your advisors, the small vendors in your supply chain, the disclosure process of your biggest software supplier.

Trust is the new attack surface.

Here's what I'd put on the leadership agenda this week:

First, inventory your trust relationships the way you inventory assets. Every consultancy with credentials, every package registry your developers pull from, every AI agent with write access. If you can't list them, you can't defend them.

Second, assume rotation, not prevention. The question isn't whether a trusted party gets compromised — Accenture just showed us it happens to the best-resourced firms on earth. The question is how fast you can rotate the secrets they held and how quickly you'd even find out.

Third, treat AI agent approval gates as security controls — which means red-teaming them like security controls. GhostApproval won't be the last bypass. If your organisation adopted agentic coding tools this year, someone should be attacking your approval workflow before an adversary does.

The uncomfortable truth: we've spent two decades hardening the vault while handing out approval stamps to anyone who looked legitimate. This week the bill came due.

The organisations that come through the next decade intact won't be the ones with the thickest walls. They'll be the ones that know exactly who holds their keys — and can change the locks in hours, not months.

Who holds yours?
