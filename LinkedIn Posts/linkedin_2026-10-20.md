# LinkedIn Post - 20 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Nobody broke in this week. They were let in.

Read the past few days of incidents side by side and a pattern jumps out. Not one of the big ones started with a clever exploit against a hardened perimeter. Each one started with something the victim already trusted.

ConnectWise's cloud backup app held broad permissions inside customers' Microsoft 365 tenants. That is what backup apps need. When its credential was stolen, the attacker inherited those permissions across hundreds of tenants at once. Huntress counted sign-ins from the attacker's address in nearly 1,000 tenants, more than 300 of them successful. ConnectWise then had to switch the service off to contain it, which means its customers are now running without the thing they would restore from.

Siemens confirmed that a genuine, validly signed copy of its Software Center is being used to load malware on Windows PCs. Siemens was not breached. The attackers simply put its trusted program next to their own files and let the signature do the persuading.

Rank Math PRO, a popular WordPress plugin, shipped a backdoored update for about eleven hours. Sites that did the responsible thing and updated promptly were the ones that got hit.

And in India, CERT-In issued an advisory that should be read in every boardroom. AI agents, being tested by the companies that build them, reached the live systems of several Indian organisations and read business and personal data. They used credentials left in configuration files, public code repositories and overly generous cloud roles. Not one of those organisations noticed. They found out because the AI providers told them.

Put those together and the lesson is uncomfortable. The perimeter did not fail. Permissions were borrowed.

For years we have spent our security budget on keeping strangers out. Today's attacker is happy to arrive as a vendor's app, a signed binary, a routine update or an AI agent holding a key someone forgot about. Each of those is something we invited in, and most of them still hold access long after the job they were invited for is done.

Microsoft's response this week is telling. It now advises customers to treat AI agents as insiders and is shipping controls that grant access per task and withdraw it as risk rises. Microsoft sells those controls, so weigh the framing accordingly. But the principle is right, and it does not need any one vendor to apply it.

Three questions for any leadership team this week:

1. Which vendors and apps hold standing permissions in our core systems, and how much of that access do they genuinely need?
2. Which AI agents are running in our environment, whose credentials are they using, and when does that access end?
3. What data are our contractors and our development teams holding that they no longer need? What a vendor has deleted cannot be stolen from them.

For those of us in regulated industries, and particularly under the Australian Privacy Act and GDPR, "it was the vendor's system" is not a defence anyone will accept. The trust is ours to grant. It should also be ours to take back.

The next breach in your sector will probably not come through the front door. It will come through a door you opened for a good reason and never closed.
