# AI LinkedIn Post - 19 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

A customer emails your support desk: "Please delete everything you hold about me."

Your shiny new AI router reads it, considers its three options (billing, account access, product support) and picks one. The answer is valid. The ticket lands in a normal queue. No error, no alert, no flag.

And your statutory clock starts ticking on a request nobody knows exists.

That example comes from a newsletter I read this weekend, and it has stuck with me because it is the quietest kind of AI failure there is. Not a breach. Not a hallucination. A correct answer to the wrong question.

It was not the only story in that shape.

Bank of America's CEO said last week the bank brought roughly 1,000 contractors in, on top of about 3,000 of its own people, to patch software weaknesses that frontier AI models exposed. His quarterly remediation figure, "a couple hundred million dollars plus", is blended with other compliance work, so we cannot isolate the AI share. But the shape is clear: the models find flaws faster than people can fix them, and somebody pays for the gap.

JPMorgan, meanwhile, has confirmed it blocks outside AI agents from moving money on retail accounts, even when the customer handed the agent their credentials. The largest bank in the US has decided that "the customer authorised it" is not enough when the thing acting is software.

Three stories. One pattern. AI is creating obligations that none of our existing paperwork anticipated.

Your vendor contracts do not say who pays when a model finds a decade-old flaw in their code.

Your terms of service do not name an AI agent as a party that might log in.

Your support workflow does not have a lane for a privacy request that a classifier was never told exists.

None of these are exotic. All of them are cheap to fix before the incident and expensive after it.

Here in Australia the clock is real. The OAIC published its guidance on the new automated-decision transparency duties on 30 September, and those duties start on 10 December. The guidance suggests they may reach well beyond things anyone calls "AI": decision-support tools and embedded software that substantially inform decisions affecting people. If you operate a club, a lender, an insurer or anything that scores or screens customers, you have about seven weeks to know what your systems decide and say so in your privacy policy.

The pattern I keep seeing is that organisations are investing in AI capability and assuming the governance will follow. It does not follow. It has to be written down first.

So here is what I would do this week, if I were sitting in your chair:

1. Pull every AI classifier or router that touches customer communication and check its label set for a privacy or "send to a human" lane. Then count what lands there.
2. Add one clause to your next software renewal: who funds remediation when an AI model surfaces a flaw in the vendor's code.
3. Decide your position on third-party agents acting for customers before one turns up holding a patron's password.
4. Map every automated decision that significantly affects a customer, including the ones nobody calls AI, against the December deadline.

The capability race is loud. The obligations are quiet. The quiet ones are the ones that end up in front of a regulator.

What is the AI obligation your organisation has not written down yet?
