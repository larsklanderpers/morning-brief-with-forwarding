**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The platform the US government uses to share threat intelligence was quietly breached for weeks. No one knew.

The Department of Homeland Security confirmed this week that the Homeland Security Information Network — HSIN, the sensitive (but unclassified) platform connecting federal, state, local, and private-sector security partners — was compromised by an unknown threat actor, likely between late May and early June. Attribution is still open.

Think about what HSIN contains: who is watching what threat, which investigations are active, which sectors are on alert. If adversaries had read access for weeks, they didn't just steal data. They mapped the watchers. That's not a breach — that's a counterintelligence operation.

It happened the same week Microsoft confirmed two zero-days in its own antivirus. BlueHammer (CVE-2026-33825) — a local privilege escalation flaw publicly disclosed before Microsoft had a fix — is now actively used in ransomware attacks. A second Defender flaw, "RoguePlanet," appeared the following day. Two zero-days. In the software that's supposed to stop the attacks. In one week.

And if that's not enough: CISA quietly added a SharePoint remote code execution vulnerability to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog — a vulnerability Microsoft had actually patched in May but neglected to publicly disclose for weeks. Agencies had until today, July 4, to patch it.

The pattern here isn't just "lots of vulnerabilities." It's something more specific.

**Adversaries are targeting the infrastructure of trust itself.**

The platform you use to coordinate your security response. The antivirus you assume is your last line of defence. The patch vendor communications you rely on to know what to fix. Each of these is an attack on the assumption that the layer beneath you is functioning as intended.

This is why zero trust cannot remain a slide in a strategy deck. It has to be an operational reality. Not because your people are untrustworthy, but because the systems they rely on may already be compromised — and you need to be able to detect and respond regardless.

Three things decision-makers should be doing right now:

**1. Treat your security coordination platforms like they're already compromised.** HSIN is the cautionary tale. What's your equivalent? Your SIEM? Your threat intel feeds? Your MSSP's management console? Apply the same zero trust logic you apply to end users.

**2. Patch Defender. Today.** BlueHammer and RoguePlanet are local privilege escalation flaws — the entry points for ransomware lateral movement. If your Windows fleet isn't patched and you're running Defender as your primary endpoint control, you have an active gap right now.

**3. Stop assuming vendor communications are sufficient for patch prioritisation.** Microsoft patched CVE-2026-45659 in May and told no one until CISA forced the issue. An organisation without its own vulnerability intelligence function — one that monitors KEV, reads security researchers, and doesn't wait for vendor bulletins — will always be behind.

The 4th of July is a fitting day for a reminder: independence isn't just a political concept. In security, it means not depending on someone else to tell you when you're at risk.
