# AI LinkedIn Post - 30 September 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

"Robinhood does not control, supervise, monitor, recommend, or audit agents."

That sentence is from Robinhood's own disclosure for its new AI trading agents. The one before it says the customer assumes all risk for trades the agent executes, and for any use of their data by third-party LLM providers.

Read it twice. It is the clearest answer anyone has yet given to the question every board is circling: when an AI agent acts on someone's behalf, who carries the consequences?

The answer, for now, is whoever signs last.

On the same day, Washington tried four ways to govern agents.

A voluntary White House accord, signed by six frontier labs, commits them to four layers of control, including an outside auditor. The companies choose the auditor, publish nothing, and face no penalty.

The FTC confirmed a consumer-protection probe into OpenAI, Anthropic and the evaluator METR, with information demands and executive testimony to follow.

A Senate subcommittee held its first hearing on rogue AI agents. One witness testified that a frontier lab now runs roughly three agent-workdays for every human workday.

And in London, the Governor of the Bank of England wrote that society must keep a "right to intervene" in frontier AI, starting with rigorous testing before and after deployment.

Notice what none of these does. None of them assigns liability when an agent gets it wrong.

That gap will not stay empty. It is being filled right now, one terms-of-service page at a time, and the direction of travel is consistent: risk flows downstream, to the customer, the deployer, the business that switched the agent on.

For anyone holding sensitive customer data, that should change how you read vendor contracts this quarter.

Three things I would do now.

First, read agent terms the way you read an indemnity clause. If a vendor's agent can touch your systems or your customers' data, find the sentence that says who is responsible when it acts outside scope. If you cannot find it, the answer is you.

Second, ask for names, not principles. The accord promises independent auditors. Ask your AI vendors which firm, what scope, and whether you can see the findings. A control you cannot inspect is a press release.

Third, keep your own record of what agents did. Logs of agent actions, not just prompts, held on infrastructure you control, under a jurisdiction you understand. When regulators arrive, and the Bank of England just told us they intend to, they will ask the deployer first. The deployer who can answer with a query rather than a reconstruction will have a very different conversation.

There is a privacy dimension here that is easy to miss. Robinhood's clause covers "any use of your data by third-party LLM providers." That is a customer's financial behaviour flowing to model providers the broker explicitly does not supervise. Under the Australian Privacy Act or GDPR, "we told them in the disclosure" is a weak defence for a business that chose the architecture.

The labs are spending real money on containment. OpenAI's research chief said this week that 5 to 10% of its compute has moved to safety monitoring. If the builder thinks that is the cost of watching its own agents, a deployer spending nothing on oversight is not saving money. It is self-insuring without knowing the premium.

Rules are coming. Until they arrive, accountability is a contract term. Make sure you wrote yours.
