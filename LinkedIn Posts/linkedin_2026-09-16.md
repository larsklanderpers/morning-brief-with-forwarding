Your security team patches web servers, VPNs, and firewalls on a defined cadence.

Does that cadence also cover your LiteLLM gateway? Your Kestra workflows? Your AI agent execution harness?

This week, CISA added seven actively exploited vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Three of the seven are in AI infrastructure components — LiteLLM, Kestra, and Starlette. Qilin ransomware operators are already using the LiteLLM chain as an initial access path. Microsoft separately documented how attackers are using these AI infrastructure vulnerabilities to deploy crypto miners, harvest API keys, and establish persistent access — not as a proof-of-concept exercise, but as an observed, documented campaign.

At the same time, Anthropic published its September 2026 threat intelligence report covering nine months of Claude misuse. The cases include a state-sponsored espionage campaign that systematically engaged 24 Ukrainian government and defence institutions over 130 days; a data extortion operation targeting hospitals, emergency services, and government agencies with ransom demands up to $500,000 per victim; and five blocked attempts at biological misuse. The report's clearest finding: threat actors have moved from using LLMs as advisors to using them as orchestrators — autonomous agents executing across reconnaissance, exploitation, persistence, and exfiltration without a human in the loop at each step.

Then there's the PaperCut incident, documented in TechCrunch's mid-year breach roundup: hundreds of AI agents were used to compromise 440 systems across 395 organisations in 48 countries. Machine-speed attack at scale.

None of this is theoretical. All of it happened in the last nine months.

Here's what I think security and technology leaders need to hear clearly: the AI governance conversation and the AI security conversation are running on separate tracks, and that's a problem. Boards are being briefed on AI governance — bias, hallucination, data privacy, responsible use. Those questions matter. But the infrastructure security question — who has access to your AI gateway, what credentials does your AI orchestrator hold, is your agent execution harness included in your vulnerability management programme — is being handled as a backlog item, if it's being handled at all.

That's exactly backwards. The threat actors aren't waiting for your AI governance framework to mature.

Three questions worth asking this week:

First — is your AI infrastructure stack in scope for your red team? Most aren't. LiteLLM, Kestra, LangChain, ChromaDB, MCP servers: these run with elevated access and broad API key permissions, and they're rarely tested the way a web application or VPN would be.

Second — what does your AI agent harness have access to? The ChatGPT prompt-injection flaw this week — where a planted instruction exfiltrated a victim's Gmail data without their awareness — is the production-confirmed version of a threat model most organisations still treat as theoretical.

Third — what's your detection coverage for AI-native post-exploitation? Reverse shells from a workflow engine, API key harvest from a database backing an AI gateway, miner deployment via a containerised AI workload: these are live attack patterns, not hypothetical scenarios. Do your detection rules cover them?

The perimeter isn't the edge anymore. For many organisations, the AI stack is where the most sensitive API keys, model configurations, and system credentials now live. Treat it accordingly.
