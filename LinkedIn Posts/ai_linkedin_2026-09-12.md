# AI LinkedIn Post - Saturday, 12 September 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

On Saturday a frontier AI lab offered outsiders a desk, a badge and a laptop.

Dario Amodei's essay "We Must Pace the Frontier" is long and ambitious, but the part that actually commits Anthropic to anything is small and concrete. Third-party evaluators get employee-like access inside the company, and they get the right to publish what they find without Anthropic editing it. Within a couple of hours Sam Altman said OpenAI would do the same.

You can argue about motive. Anthropic is reportedly heading for an IPO next month, and critics are already calling the wider plan a moat for incumbents. I think the evaluator piece is right on the merits regardless, because it names the real gap: until now, nobody independent was in the building.

Now apply that test to your own organisation.

The same week the labs volunteered for inspection, three ordinary stories showed where customer data actually leaks.

Google shipped a Gemini desktop app for Windows that opens over whatever window is active and drafts from Gmail and Drive. For Workspace tenants it is on by default wherever Gemini is enabled, and Google says plainly that "there is no end user setting." Only an administrator can switch it off.

Meta's assistant looked at one mother's video and suggested the prompt "Who is the child passenger?" It then assembled her daughters' names from her posts and her own mother's posts, and she says it showed her a photo she deleted years ago. No single post leaked anything. The model built the dossier.

And Anthropic's own misuse report alleges that a Chinese lab routed hundreds of thousands of its users' prompts to Claude and served the answers back as its own. Those users typed into one vendor and were processed by another, in another jurisdiction, and nobody told them.

None of these is a frontier-safety story. Each is a question about the inference path: what saw the data, where, and who can prove it.

That is the question Australian organisations will have to answer in writing soon. From 10 December, privacy policies must describe the kinds of personal information used in automated decisions that significantly affect individuals. The duty comes from a 2024 Act, it is not waiting on the current consultation, and the OAIC's promised guidance has not appeared yet. The deadline has not moved.

In Europe the picture is the reverse. The EU has pushed its high-risk AI obligations back to December 2027. That gives you fifteen extra months, and fifteen months disappears quickly if nobody owns the work.

Here is my read. The pacing debate governs how fast models get more capable. It does nothing about what your deployment sends where. If a lab needs an independent evaluator to be believed, so does your AI program.

So before the end of this quarter:

1. Inventory every system that scores, triages or declines a customer. That list becomes your December disclosure.
2. Review every AI feature that arrived switched on, starting with anything that reads the active screen. Default-on is a decision someone else made for you.
3. Ask each AI vendor for its full inference path, sub-processors included, and whether it will give your auditors the access it just offered METR.
4. Put someone independent in front of it, internal audit or an outside firm, and let them write down what they find.

The labs just conceded that grading your own homework is not good enough. That standard applies to the rest of us too.
