The US government just confirmed what OT security practitioners have been warning about for two years: attackers are using AI to write industrial control system exploits. Not in a lab. Against live Siemens S7 PLCs in critical infrastructure.

That's not a prediction. It's in a joint CISA/DoD advisory published this week.

At the same time, researchers from Anthropic and EPFL released a preprint demonstrating AI agents can infect each other through system prompt files — functioning as a worm propagating across multi-agent architectures. And CISA added an MLflow Server-Side Request Forgery to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, the first AI/ML infrastructure tool to land on that list.

These three items aren't coincidental. They're the same story told from three different angles.

The story is this: the attack surface has moved into infrastructure that most organisations haven't brought under consistent security governance yet.

OT environments have been treated as a deferred IT problem for a decade. The thinking was that air-gapped or segmented industrial networks were inherently lower risk. That assumption was already fraying when threat actors used conventional scripting to probe PLCs. It's now formally wrong — AI-generated exploit scripts change the economics of targeting OT systems at scale.

ML infrastructure has been treated as internal tooling. MLflow, for most data science teams, lives in the same mental category as a Jupyter notebook — useful, internal, not really a production attack surface. But MLflow has network access, credential flows, and increasingly, connections to production systems. An SSRF in that context isn't a low-severity finding.

Agentic AI has been treated as software, not as a network. The agent-worm research changes that framing. Multi-agent systems have trust relationships between components — one agent calls another, passes context, and acts on what it receives. That's a propagation path. Prompt injection as a worm vector is not a theoretical risk any more.

The organisations that will handle the next twelve months well are the ones that have already asked: what parts of our environment have we been exempting from the security controls we apply everywhere else?

The answer, for most enterprises, includes at least one of: operational technology, ML infrastructure, and agentic AI.

The Siemens advisory is useful not because Siemens S7 PLCs are universally deployed, but because it marks the moment AI-assisted ICS exploitation stopped being a red team technique and became a documented threat actor behaviour. Firsts like that tend to set ceilings on response timelines.

If your board hasn't had a conversation about OT security posture, ML infrastructure access controls, or the security architecture for agentic AI deployments, those conversations are overdue — not by weeks, by months.

The threat surface expanded while the governance didn't. The gap is where the next major incident is being scripted right now.
