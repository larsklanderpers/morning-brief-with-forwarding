One tweet. 170 million views. And suddenly everyone is asking the same question I've been asking regulated-industry clients for two years: who is actually authorised to do what with your data?

An Anthropic researcher quit this week and wrote that neither of the labs he'd worked for is "acting responsibly." The tweet went viral in ways AI safety discourse never has before — CNN, CBS, TMZ, celebrity Instagram, Joe Rogan. Dario Amodei published a 4,000-word essay calling for voluntary pacing. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are now formally coordinating on safety standards for the first time.

The mainstream conversation is finally catching up to the operational question.

But here's the thing: the existential risk debate is not the right frame for most of your stakeholders. Your board doesn't need to resolve whether AI will kill everyone. They need to know the answer to three questions before the December 10 deadline hits.

**What is your AI authorised to do with customer data?**

Australia's Privacy Act now requires you to disclose in your privacy policy when a computer system makes significant decisions about individuals. Employment. Credit. Healthcare triage. Benefits. If you can't point to the clause that covers your AI-driven process, you're 83 days from non-compliance with penalties up to AUD 50 million.

**Can you demonstrate that authorisation in a regulatory audit?**

The EU AI Act has been fully enforceable since August 2. As of April 2026, 78% of organisations had taken no meaningful compliance steps. The enforcement lottery tends to favour visible, high-profile cases — and regulators in both Canberra and Brussels have new teeth this year.

**Is your data actually sovereign — or just nearby?**

This is the one most Australian enterprises haven't faced squarely. Since January 31, the Australia-US CLOUD Act Agreement gives US law enforcement a direct, treaty-backed channel to compel data from any US-incorporated provider's Australian data centre. Sydney region. Australian employees. US legal reach. That's not sovereignty.

The practical answer is running on infrastructure you actually control — which is increasingly viable. Qwen3 runs locally at sizes from 1.7 billion to 235 billion parameters. Open-weight models matched the closed frontier on core benchmarks in June. Ollama installs in one command. The performance excuse is gone.

Scotland's Parliament voted this week to pause AI data centre planning for 12 months, after realising the UK hosts 4% of the world's AI compute but controls roughly 0.1% of it. That's the number your risk team needs to know about your own stack.

The political window on AI governance is open right now in a way it hasn't been before. The question is whether regulated-industry leaders use it to get ahead of the requirements — or spend the next twelve months reacting to enforcement actions they could see coming.

Your December deadline is 83 days away. What's your disclosure?
