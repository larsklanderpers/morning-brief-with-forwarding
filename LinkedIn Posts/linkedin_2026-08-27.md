Two organisations. Same attack. Same adversary tradecraft. Same vulnerabilities exploited.

One detected nothing.

CISA published "A Tale of Two SOCs" yesterday — the results of simultaneous red team assessments against two US critical infrastructure organisations. The government services entity was fully owned: keyloggers on SOC defenders' machines, admin emails read, cloud resources accessed. Nobody noticed. The water utility's team isolated compromised hosts within 20 minutes of the phishing payload executing and severed command-and-control before the intrusion could spread.

The difference was not budget. It was not tooling. It was whether the SOC had a functioning detection-to-containment pipeline — and whether they actually ran it.

I keep coming back to this because the technology industry has a vested interest in framing every security failure as a product gap. Buy this platform. Ingest these feeds. Deploy this agent. The CISA report is inconvenient for that framing. Both organisations had security tools. The gap was operational: triage discipline, escalation speed, and the willingness to isolate a host before you've finished your investigation.

Set that alongside three other things that dropped this week:

The ACSC confirmed active exploitation of N-able N-central vulnerabilities in Australia — targeting managed service providers specifically. MSPs are the hub-and-spoke play for attackers: compromise one RMM platform and you inherit keys to dozens of client environments. Patches have been available for three weeks. The exploitation is happening anyway.

AnonyMousKIT, a Phishing-as-a-Service platform, is now running AI voice agents that call stolen iPhone owners, impersonate Apple Support, and socially engineer passcodes out of them to bypass Activation Lock. 506 domains. 168 storefronts. Rented by the call. This is not a sophisticated nation-state operation. It is industrialised, commoditised social engineering.

And Kaspersky documented the first Android malware campaign explicitly built for vehicle infotainment systems — abusing the car's own firmware update mechanism to enroll the vehicle's internet connection into the BADBOX proxy botnet. The attack surface now includes your commute.

The through-line: the adversary is getting more *systematic*, not more exotic. They're industrialising known techniques — phishing, credential theft, supply chain pivot, trusted software abuse — and running them at scale against organisations that still treat security as a product acquisition problem rather than an operational discipline problem.

The organisations that are holding are the ones running detection and response as a rehearsed, disciplined process. The ones that are getting owned are the ones that have the tools and haven't operationalised them.

That's the lesson from the CISA report. Both SOCs probably had similar product stacks. Only one had a SOC that actually functioned as a SOC.
