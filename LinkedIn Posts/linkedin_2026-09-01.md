The ransomware crew didn't need a zero-day. They had something better: an AI agent with a chat window and a to-do list.

This week's Aurora ransomware story deserves more attention than it's getting. A Russian-speaking affiliate ran Claude Sonnet through Cursor — a coding assistant most developers consider a productivity tool — and used it to plan intrusions, run reconnaissance, install VPN clients, and execute certificate attacks across victim networks. Not on one target. On more than twenty, across nine countries, over four months. At least seventeen reached domain-level access. At least seven are confirmed breached.

The exposed directory that blew this open contained the Cursor AI chat logs, in Russian. Which means we have, for the first time, a documented transcript of an AI agentic loop being used end-to-end in a live ransomware operation. This isn't a proof of concept anymore. It went into production.

Three things stand out to me about why this matters beyond the headlines.

First, the attack surface has changed shape. We've spent years worrying about AI being used to write better phishing emails. The Aurora case shows a more dangerous application: AI as an operational coordinator, handling the reconnaissance and lateral movement tradecraft that previously required either deep expertise or significant time investment from a human operator. That's a skills-barrier reduction that compounds the supply-side ransomware problem.

Second, breakout time is now at 29 minutes on average — a 65% increase in speed from two years ago. In most enterprise environments, 29 minutes is before the first alert reaches a human analyst for triage. Detection without automated response isn't a security control at that velocity; it's a logging exercise.

Third, look at the week in context. The same seven days brought us: a China-nexus APT burning through unpatched VMware vCenter systems across 47 countries (six weeks after a CVSS 9.8 patch was available), two Perth 21-year-olds arrested for running a supply chain attack campaign that compromised over a thousand organisations globally, and ACSC flagging active exploitation of RMM platform vulnerabilities in Australian MSP environments. The operational tempo on the adversary side is not slowing down.

The honest question for boards and security leadership isn't "are we ready for AI-assisted attacks?" That ship has sailed. The question is: what specific detection gaps exist in your environment for AI-assisted lateral movement that doesn't look like your attacker baseline? And if you can't answer that precisely, you don't have an AI security strategy — you have an AI security aspiration.

Aurora used a consumer coding tool. Your adversaries are not waiting for enterprise-grade attack infrastructure. They're using whatever's available, and right now, what's available is very good.
