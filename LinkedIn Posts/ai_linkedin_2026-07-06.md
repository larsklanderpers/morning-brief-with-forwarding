# AI LinkedIn Post — July 6, 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Last week, on the same calendar, two of the world's most influential regulators moved in opposite directions on AI. Australia tightened the screws. Europe loosened them. And if your business holds customer data, the gap between those two moves is where your next compliance headache lives.

Here's what happened.

On 1 July, APRA's CPS 230 took effect. Buried in the detail is a line that should reset how every regulated Australian firm thinks about AI: vendors processing customer or member data — including AI vendors — are now "material service providers." That means registers, resilience testing, and enforceable contractual accountability. In plain terms: "our AI vendor stores data in a Sydney data centre" is no longer a good enough answer. You now have to prove *who can legally compel access to that data* — and that's a very different question.

Meanwhile in Brussels, the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations were supposed to bite on 2 August. Except the harmonised standards are running late, so the real deadline is quietly sliding toward 2027. At the same time, the Commission is floating relief on GDPR paperwork for smaller firms. Europe, the region that wrote the global privacy rulebook, is easing off.

So one regulator is accelerating and the other is coasting. If you run a single global AI policy, you're now being pulled in two directions at once.

The trap is to treat that divergence as an opportunity to relax. It isn't. Australia's automated-decision transparency rules land on 10 December — inside six months — with penalties up to AUD 50 million. The stricter regime is the one with the nearer deadline and the bigger stick. Build to it, and Europe's softening becomes optional headroom rather than a gap you have to scramble to close.

And here's the part that should change the conversation in your leadership team: the technical excuses for non-compliance have quietly expired.

For years, the argument for shipping customer PII off to a US cloud endpoint was capability — that's where the good models lived. That's no longer true. Six frontier labs are now within a rounding error of each other on quality. Open-weight models like MiniMax M3 run million-token, vision-capable workloads entirely on hardware you control. And sovereign-by-design vendors — Mistral in Europe, with a €15 billion fund and government partnerships behind it — are proving there's a real commercial market for AI where jurisdiction is a feature, not an afterthought.

When capability was scarce, "we had no compliant alternative" was a defensible line. When six labs are at parity and you can self-host a frontier-class model, that line collapses. What's left isn't a technology problem. It's a governance problem.

Specifically, it's an inventory problem. Most organisations cannot answer, with confidence, a simple question: *which models currently touch our customers' data, and where does the actual inference happen?* Storage residency is easy to verify. Processing residency — where the computation runs, who can subpoena it, whether it silently routes offshore under peak load — is where the real exposure sits. And it's exactly what CPS 230 and the December ADM rules now require you to know.

So if you're a decision-maker balancing AI capability against PII obligations, here's my recommendation. Before you evaluate one more shiny model, run the audit you've been avoiding. Build a defensible inventory of every AI system touching customer data. Map where each one actually processes — not just stores. Then decide, deliberately, which workloads belong on-prem or with a sovereign provider, and which can safely live in the cloud.

The firms treating AI adoption as shadow IT are about to discover that December has a way of forcing the audit anyway. Better to run it on your own terms.

Where does your customer data actually think? If you can't answer that today, that's your Q3 project.
