# AI LinkedIn Post — 11 September 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Your AI vendor's chief scientist just told you something important. You should believe him.

OpenAI's chief scientist said this week that no lab has solved alignment or monitoring. That wasn't a critic or a regulator saying it — that was the person responsible for the science at the most deployed AI platform on earth.

The same week, an Anthropic researcher resigned publicly and cited a 10% chance that human civilisation doesn't survive the next decade. Another Anthropic researcher confirmed those odds on the record.

Meanwhile, OpenAI and the U.S. General Services Administration just expanded ChatGPT access to 23 million government workers in a single deal.

I am not writing this to scare anyone. I am writing this because the gap between those two facts is where regulated-industry risk lives, and most boards haven't seen it framed that way yet.

Here is the structural problem.

ENISA — the EU's cybersecurity agency — has just been granted access to Anthropic's Mythos 5 after months of negotiations. Mythos 5.1 is already out. The safety auditors are testing the model from last quarter. If the people whose job is to assess frontier AI safety can't get current access, your internal risk team certainly can't.

The Australian Privacy Act grace period ends December 10. That's 90 days.

And the EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duties are already in force — with fines up to €35 million for prohibited AI practices.

Here is what the enterprises that figured this out are doing.

Uber cut its AI cost per session by 52%. Pinterest is running at under 8% of what closed frontier models cost. AT&T cut its AI bill by 56% with a 2% quality reduction. The method in every case: open-weight models running on infrastructure they control, not on someone else's cloud in someone else's jurisdiction.

DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash on Wednesday under MIT licence. 552 billion parameters, only 8 billion active at inference time. Claims 98% of the frontier's benchmark performance at 1.4% of the cost. MIT means no royalties, no vendor relationship, no data leaving your infrastructure.

The open-model migration has been a cost story for six months. This week it became a governance story.

When the alternative to sending your customer PII through U.S. inference infrastructure is a model that runs locally, costs 1.4% as much, and performs near-frontier — the data residency argument and the budget argument are suddenly pointing in the same direction.

The organisations that treat the Australian Privacy Act deadline and the open-model cost story as the same conversation will be ahead. The ones that treat them as separate problems will spend December explaining themselves to the OAIC.

Three questions worth putting on the agenda before the end of this quarter:

Where does inference actually happen for every AI deployment touching customer PII? Not where is the data stored — where does the model process the prompt?

What is the governance answer when your AI vendor's own scientists say the model's behaviour is not fully predictable?

What is the board-level accountability structure if a model deployed in your stack is later found to have been processing data in a way your vendor didn't disclose?

The frontier is not the only option. For regulated industries, it may not even be the right one.
