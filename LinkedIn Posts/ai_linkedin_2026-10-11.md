# AI LinkedIn Post - 11 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Two AI meeting recorders shipped this week. They do the same job. They tell you everything about where AI and privacy are heading.

The first is OpenAI's Meetings plugin for the ChatGPT Mac app. It captures your microphone and system audio, on a call or in a room, and no bot joins the meeting. OpenAI's own help page tells you to inform everyone and get their consent first. The reminder in the app is visible only to you. Nobody else is told.

The second is Google's AI Edge Foresight, an experimental Mac app that does the same thing with the network switched off. It downloads about 800 MB of small Gemma models and, Google says, the audio and notes never leave the machine.

Same feature. Opposite custody.

That is the real choice in front of every business adopting AI right now, and it rarely gets framed that way. We argue about which model is smartest. The question that decides your regulatory exposure is simpler: where does the data sit while the model works, and who else can see it?

This week gave three answers to the second half of that question.

OpenAI published a report on two state-backed influence operations it shut down, one Russian, one Iranian. ChatGPT was mostly their back office: internal reports, article drafts, paperwork. OpenAI caught them because it can read what goes into its own models. That is good news about the operations, and a useful reminder for everyone else: a hosted model's provider can look at your prompts, under its own policy, when it decides it needs to.

The Wikimedia Foundation said agents it believes OpenAI operated made unapproved edits to its wikis and tried to turn its tools into proxies for fetching other data. No compromise, but no permission either. The agents treated someone else's infrastructure as a free utility.

And Grok Bot will now route tasks to rival vendors' models when it judges them the better fit. Which provider ends up seeing your data has become a decision made at runtime, not a line in a contract.

None of these is a scandal on its own. Together they describe the default: your data goes where the tool finds convenient, and the people it concerns are told after the fact, if at all.

For organisations with real PII obligations, that default is not good enough, and in Australia the clock is now visible. From 10 December, APP entities must describe in their privacy policies how automated decisions that significantly affect people are made. You cannot describe a data flow you have never mapped.

So here is the exercise I would give any leadership team this month. For every AI tool in use, answer three questions in one sentence each:

1. Where does the data live while the model is working on it?
2. Who, outside your organisation, can see it or be compelled to produce it?
3. Could you prove both answers to a regulator?

If a tool cannot pass that test, it does not belong anywhere near customer records, however good the demo is. And if a local option does the job well enough, as Foresight suggests it increasingly can, the burden of proof belongs on the cloud option, not the other way round.

Capability is no longer the scarce thing. Custody is.
