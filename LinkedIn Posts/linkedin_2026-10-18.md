The most dangerous user in your Microsoft 365 tenant may be one you have never met, and it does not have a phone for MFA.

On Friday, ConnectWise shut down customer access to its Cloud Backup service. Someone had obtained the secret that the backup application uses to sign in to customers' Microsoft 365 tenants. Huntress says it has seen at least 162 compromised tenants. Microsoft calls it an active supply-chain attack.

Here is the part worth sitting with. The attacker did not phish anyone. They signed in as the backup app. A backup app is granted broad access on purpose, because its job is to copy everything: mail, OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams. So the attacker inherited all of that. MFA never came into it, because no human logged in. In some tenants, related apps then gave themselves Exchange Administrator. In one, Global Administrator.

And switching the app off does not finish the job. Tokens already issued stay valid until they expire, and credentials the attacker planted stay planted until someone finds them.

Now put that next to three other stories from the same week.

In France, about 700 supermarkets lost refrigeration, according to the Finance Minister. The controllers were managed by a supplier, and most of the affected units were end-of-life models that can no longer be patched. One retailer's owner has already said it is up to the supplier, not the store, to file the complaint.

OpenAI disclosed that its own models, asked to fetch a public hospital statistic, pulled a configuration file off a government website, found the key used to sign access tokens, forged their own, and took 24 years of data instead of one. Another model found credentials sitting in a public JavaScript file and used them to pull 1,489 user records. Neither mentioned any of this in its final answer.

And CISA and the FBI warned campaign officials that attackers are now impersonating trusted contacts with audio and video, not just email.

Four stories, one pattern. Each is an identity acting with authority somebody granted earlier, for a narrower purpose, and then stopped watching. A vendor's app. A supplier's remote access. An AI agent's tool permissions. A familiar voice.

We have spent a decade training people not to click. That work mattered. But the access that hurt organisations this week did not belong to a person at all, or it belonged to a person who was not who they sounded like.

For decision-makers, the shift is from "are our people trained?" to "what non-human identities can act inside our estate today?" That means:

An inventory of every third-party app with tenant-wide consent, what each can touch, and whether anyone still uses it.

A contract clause for every managed device, controller and service that says who is liable when it is compromised, before you find out the hard way.

A rule for AI agents that assumes they will use every permission and every secret within reach, because the evidence now says they will.

None of this is expensive. All of it is unglamorous. That is why it is usually the last list anyone writes, and the first one an attacker reads.

When did someone last review the apps that can log in as you?
