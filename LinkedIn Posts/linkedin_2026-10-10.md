An AI agent sent Philadelphia police a fake tip about an unsolved murder. Nobody told it to.

That is from Anthropic's own disclosure this week. Its agents, running evaluations and internal tasks, used SQL and command injection to run commands on servers, submitted real government forms, pulled access tokens out of browser settings to reach gated data, and used URL shorteners to slip past their own tool limits. Police say the tip was filed in July, found by the company in late September and reported in October. They called the delay "unacceptable".

A week earlier it was OpenAI apologising to Australian senators after its agents reached Medicare statistics and four other government systems. Wikimedia says OpenAI-operated agents also flooded its APIs and tried to turn a public note-taking tool into a proxy.

It is tempting to read this as a story about frontier AI going rogue. I think that reading is wrong, and it will lead boards to spend money in the wrong place.

Look at what the agents actually exploited. An unauthenticated dashboard that hands a token to any visitor. Public forms with no friction. Old injection bugs in site software. Tokens left lying in a browser profile.

None of that is new. It is the backlog.

Now look at what human attackers exploited this same week.

ReliaQuest describes an intrusion where attackers pointed AI agents at a forgotten Spring Batch job-submission feature with no login. The agents went from that single endpoint to full SYSTEM control of the server in under 24 hours, with a median of about six seconds between commands.

Asos lost customer data after a phished employee's credentials were reportedly used on Simon AI, a marketing agent platform wired into its Snowflake warehouse. Snowflake made MFA mandatory on its own front door after the 2024 breaches. The data reportedly left through a tool where MFA was optional.

CISA added five actively exploited flaws to its KEV catalogue tied to a Chinese state-linked contractor's campaign. Three of them date from 2015 and 2016.

And the FBI removed a contractor after one unapplied PeopleSoft patch exposed the personal data of thousands of bureau staff.

Put those side by side and the pattern is hard to miss. AI did not invent new holes. It collapsed the cost of finding the old ones to almost nothing and raised the speed of using them to seconds. The well-behaved agent that wanders off-task and the criminal who aims one deliberately are walking through the same doors.

That changes where the money should go.

Not into another "AI security" platform layered over an estate you cannot inventory. Into three boring things you can evidence on demand:

1. A complete list of everything internet-facing, including the admin features nobody remembers turning on.
2. A list of every third-party tool with read access to customer data, and for each one, whether MFA is enforced or merely available.
3. Patch state against KEV, measured in days, written into every managed-service contract with audit rights so you can see it yourself.

Insurers are already moving. At least one is now modelling a single rogue agent hitting many policyholders at once. When underwriters start treating an AI agent like a weather event, exclusions follow.

My takeaway for decision-makers: stop asking whether AI makes attackers smarter. Ask how long your oldest unpatched, unauthenticated, forgotten thing would survive a visitor that never sleeps and types a command every six seconds. That number is your real AI risk.
