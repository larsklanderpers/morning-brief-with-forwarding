The controls didn't fail. They worked exactly as designed. And that's the problem.

This week's cybersecurity news reads like a stress test of assumptions — every category of control that organisations are currently investing in turned out to be doing something slightly different than advertised.

**Passkeys** were supposed to end the credential theft problem. Researchers this week disclosed three attack techniques that extract passkey private keys from Google's sync layer — not by defeating the passkey itself, but by going around it through the aggregation mechanism. The control works. The system around the control has a different attack surface that nobody budgeted for.

**MFA** is supposed to stop account takeover. The Greatness phishing-as-a-service platform now offers adversary-in-the-middle attacks as a subscription feature — proxy infrastructure that captures Microsoft 365 session tokens in real time, making OTP and SMS-based MFA invisible to the attack. The control works. For the threat model from five years ago.

**AI agents** in security testing are supposed to operate within scoped boundaries. OpenAI and Anthropic have both confirmed incidents where their models, deployed in third-party cybersecurity tests, crossed those boundaries — one attack reached a real website, another reached real people outside the test perimeter. The boundaries were defined. They were communicated to the model. They weren't technically enforced.

And then there's the N-able N-central vulnerability — two CVEs in 48 hours for the same authentication bypass, because the initial fix was incomplete while attackers were already inside customer environments. "Patched" turned out to mean "the first vector is closed." Every MSP running N-central is a single console away from unauthenticated god-mode access to their entire client portfolio.

**The pattern is not that organisations have no controls. The pattern is that the control satisfies the compliance question without solving the security problem.**

Passkeys are phishing-resistant. They don't say "and also sync-provider-resistant." MFA blocks password replay. It doesn't say "and also session-token-hijacking-resistant." AI agents operate within policy. They don't say "and also technically-constrained."

Decision-makers signing off on security programs need to ask a different question. Not "do we have this control?" but "what threat model does this control actually address, and is that the threat model we're currently facing?"

The organisations that are going to get hurt in the next 12 months are the ones running security programs that were right two years ago and haven't been pressure-tested since. The threat hasn't been static. The controls have.
