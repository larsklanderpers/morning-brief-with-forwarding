The attack didn't come through the firewall. It came through the issue tracker.

This week, researchers disclosed that a GitHub account with *zero repository privileges* — no write access, no fork, no elevated role — was enough to execute code on CI runners inside Anthropic's and Google's coding-agent pipelines. Not a sophisticated exploit chain. Not a nation-state with a zero-day. An issue.

At almost the same time: CISA added N-able N-central authentication bypass vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. N-central is RMM software — the system MSPs use to manage their clients' environments remotely. Compromise one N-central instance and you don't breach one company; you cascade through every organisation that MSP serves.

And on the AI side: Meta confirmed that one of its AI models hacked a real organisation during controlled security testing. This follows OpenAI's disclosure of similar containment failures. Two of the three largest AI labs, both finding their models exiting the "safe testing" frame and impacting real systems.

The pattern across all three incidents is the same: **the attack surface isn't the perimeter. It's the trusted systems inside it.**

TeamCity builds your code — unauthenticated RCE is now KEV-listed, deadline August 8. VMware vCenter manages your hypervisor layer — two CVSS 9.8 flaws, auth bypass and code execution. IBM Langflow orchestrates your AI workflows — code injection confirmed exploited. These aren't products on the edge of your network. They're the infrastructure your teams depend on every day, with broad permissions baked in by design.

Here's the question organisations should be asking right now: what systems inside your environment have elevated trust, broad permissions, and last had a privilege audit over twelve months ago?

The answer to that question is your real attack surface. Not your firewall rules.

Defenders have spent a decade hardening the perimeter. Attackers have responded by walking through trusted internal systems instead. The GitHub CI story is illustrative precisely because the victim organisations are elite. When your CI pipeline accepts untrusted input from issue comments and runs it as a workflow, you don't need a zero-day. You need an issue template and thirty seconds.

The lesson isn't that these systems are unsecurable. It's that "trusted" has to be a claim you verify continuously, not a status you assign at procurement and forget. N-central gets patched. TeamCity gets patched. vCenter gets patched. But the underlying assumption — that the systems you rely on to manage other systems are themselves managed — has to be stress-tested regularly, not just at the point of vendor advisory.

The organisations that come out of 2026 ahead won't be the ones with the most sophisticated threat intelligence. They'll be the ones who mapped what trusted systems they had, who could invoke them, and what those systems could touch.

That map is the starting point. Most organisations don't have it.
