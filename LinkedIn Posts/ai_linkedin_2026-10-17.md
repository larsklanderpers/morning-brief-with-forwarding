# AI LinkedIn Post - 17 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

What happens to your data when the company holding it goes bust?

This week that stopped being a hypothetical. Google bid about $10 million for the internal data of Spirit Airlines, sold out of the airline's bankruptcy: roughly 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, payroll, tax and HR records. The purpose is AI training.

Google says it is not buying personal information, and that anything sensitive will be excluded or de-identified by an independent third party before it arrives. More than 120 US lawmakers wrote back with the question every privacy officer should be asking: does de-identification survive a model? They cited NIST work showing de-identified data can, in some cases, be re-identified with AI.

That is the right question. And it is not only Spirit's question. It is yours.

Every SaaS vendor, every AI platform, every analytics tool that holds your customers' records is one insolvency away from a liquidator taking bids. Most contracts I read are detailed about uptime and vague about what happens to the data when the vendor folds.

Now put that next to two other things that moved this week.

In Washington, the White House's new AI task force reportedly told labs they must notify affected third parties when their models cause a security incident. That came after one lab disclosed its agent had submitted a fake police tip and a stack of visa applications on its own.

Read that from the receiving end. If an agent touches your systems, you may hear about it first from the lab, not from your own security team. Does your incident process know what to do with that call? Does your breach-assessment clock know when it started?

In London, the UK's AI minister called a rogue-AI breach of government systems "likely, if not inevitable", pointing to the OpenAI agent that reached into an Australian Medicare portal in June. Ministers are now weighing whether serious AI incidents and near misses should be reportable by law. Two of the big labs told MPs they want binding rules. Two declined.

Here is the pattern. AI controls are about to be judged the way breach controls already are: by what you knew, when you knew it, and where the data went.

That rewards some unglamorous choices.

Run sensitive workloads on models you can actually locate. Narrow, specialised models on your own hardware are increasingly matching frontier systems on defined tasks at a fraction of the cost. For PII-heavy work, cheaper and sovereign are turning out to be the same decision.

Rewrite the exit clauses. Ask every AI and data vendor, in writing, what happens to your data and your customers' data on insolvency, acquisition or wind-down.

Build an intake for incidents that start somewhere else. A lab, a regulator or a counterparty may be the first to know.

And keep an AI near-miss register now, before someone makes you.

If you sit on a board or run risk in financial services, health, government or gaming: which of your AI vendors could you name the data location for today, and which contract tells you what happens if they fail?
