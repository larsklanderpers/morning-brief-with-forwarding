# AI LinkedIn Post — 24 June 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Last Friday, the US government switched off two of the world's most capable AI models. Overnight.

Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 — gone. Disabled globally to comply with a Trump administration export control order. Anthropic's own employees in non-US offices lost access. Enterprises mid-workflow, mid-project, mid-deployment: left holding a very expensive silence.

This is the moment the "AI sovereignty" conversation stopped being abstract.

---

**We've known the risk exists. We just didn't think it would be exercised this fast.**

Every organisation that built production workflows on cloud-hosted frontier models made an implicit assumption: the model will be there tomorrow. That assumption just got stress-tested in real time. The Anthropic ban is being called the "Huawei moment" for AI — the point where geopolitical risk and enterprise infrastructure risk become the same conversation.

And it won't be the last time.

The US government's willingness to cut off foreign access to a major AI provider — even partially, even controversially — tells you something important about the long-term regulatory posture you're planning against. If you handle customer PII, operate under APRA's prudential framework, or have customers in the EU, "what happens to our AI stack if a model gets export-controlled?" is no longer a hypothetical. It's a gap analysis you should be running this week.

---

**Meanwhile, Australia's regulators are moving — independently of the geopolitics, and faster than most organisations realise.**

APRA's April letter to banks, insurers, and superannuation trustees wasn't guidance. It was a warning. Four AI governance failures documented. An active 12-month supervisory program announced. Enforcement signalled for entities that don't manage AI risk proportionately to their scale.

The OAIC's first-ever compliance sweep is live — 60 organisations across high-risk sectors being examined right now. And from 10 December 2026, any organisation using AI in decisions that significantly affect individuals must disclose it in their privacy policy.

Six months. That's how much runway you have to get compliant with Australia's automated decision-making transparency obligations.

---

**The organisations that are ahead of this aren't waiting for the September guidance.**

They're running their AI supply chain audit now. They're asking: which AI systems process customer PII? Where does inference happen? Who are our fourth-party dependencies in our AI vendor's stack?

They're also looking seriously at on-premise and open-weight models — not because cloud AI is bad, but because they understand that keeping inference inside their own perimeter eliminates an entire category of compliance risk by design. Over half of enterprise AI inference globally now runs on-premises. That's not a niche position. That's where the risk-aware market has already moved.

---

**The strategic read for a PII-obligated business in June 2026 is this:**

Your AI stack is now a regulatory and geopolitical exposure, not just a technology decision. The Anthropic ban proved that US-controlled frontier models carry sovereign risk. APRA proved that Australian regulators are watching AI supplier relationships specifically. And the OAIC is about to prove that automated decision-making transparency isn't optional.

The organisations that come out of 2026 with their AI programs intact — and ahead — are the ones that treat AI infrastructure like any other critical third-party dependency: mapped, audited, and with a plan for what happens when the vendor can't perform.

The window to get ahead of this is now. Not September. Not December.

Now.
