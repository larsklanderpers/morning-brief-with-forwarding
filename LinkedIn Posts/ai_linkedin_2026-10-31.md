The most valuable thing left in a 95-year-old law firm may be its files.

This week Crosby, a two-year-old AI law firm, bid for parts of Weil, Gotshal & Manges as Weil prepares to wind down. The reported list includes the trademark, the brand and "unspecified internal data". The bid may not succeed. Terms are undisclosed. A rival looked at the same deal and passed, arguing that the data belongs to the clients, and that rival has its own interest in saying so.

Set aside who is right. Look at what just happened to the idea of a client file.

For decades, an adviser's archive was worth almost nothing once the partners left. It sat in storage until a retention schedule said it could be destroyed. Today it is potential training material for agents. That is a new kind of asset, and it appears on a new kind of sale schedule.

It is not an isolated case.

Character.ai, the companion chatbot company, is in Chapter 11 and expects to be sold by late November or early December. CBS reported that its privacy policy allows chat transcripts, voice recordings, images and videos to be disclosed in connection with a sale. People told those bots things they did not tell their families.

Now put that beside this week's market.

The Nasdaq closed its worst month since March 2025. Alphabet lost about $500 billion in a single day. The Financial Times reports that OpenAI cut its funding target from $30 billion to $20 billion, with the first close expected below that. None of this means any of these companies is about to fail. Most of them will be fine.

But "most of them will be fine" is not a data protection strategy.

Almost every AI risk assessment I have read asks the same three questions. Is it accurate? Is it secure? Where is it hosted? Each one quietly assumes a supplier that stays solvent, keeps its owners and honours the promises it made at signing.

A privacy promise is only as durable as the company that made it, and the terms that company can change.

In the same week, four of the largest AI labs backed a single robots.txt signal to opt out of AI training. It is a useful step. It is also a preference rather than an access control, and it does nothing for material already collected. Meanwhile the UK's competition regulator opened a case into Meta's terms after a Norwegian court temporarily blocked them, which tells you how far commercial terms can travel if nobody pushes back.

This is where the case for keeping sensitive work close stops being about ideology.

A model running on your own hardware cannot be sold in a bankruptcy. Its logs do not appear on anyone's asset register but yours. That does not make local AI the answer for everything: the capability gap with the frontier is real, and you should pay for capability where it earns its keep. But the sensitive 20% of your workload deserves a different answer from the other 80%.

So here is the fourth question for your AI vendor review: when this company is sold, refinanced or wound up, what happens to what we gave it?

Three things to do this month.

Find the change-of-control and insolvency clauses in your AI contracts. If they do not exist, ask for return-or-delete terms that survive a sale.

Ask your professional advisers the same question. Your lawyers, your auditors and your consultants hold your most sensitive material, and some of them are now tooling up with AI.

Then look at what you are sending in the first place. The data you never sent is the only data that cannot be sold.

You have the most leverage while your supplier still needs your signature. Use it now.
