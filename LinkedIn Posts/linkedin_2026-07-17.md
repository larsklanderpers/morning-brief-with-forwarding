CISA just gave federal agencies three days to patch a SharePoint zero-day.

Not three weeks. Three days.

If that deadline sounds aggressive, look at what else happened this week — because the three-day window isn't bureaucratic drama. It's an accurate read of how fast compromise now happens.

A new ransomware crew called Spirals ran a complete corporate intrusion — initial access, data theft, encryption — in under 24 hours. Not dwell-for-months, map-the-network, pick-your-moment ransomware. Same-day ransomware.

Sygnia published incident findings showing an attacker using AI-assisted tooling who went from a single AWS foothold to full environmental compromise in roughly 72 hours. Work that used to take a skilled operator weeks — reconnaissance, privilege mapping, lateral movement — compressed into three days by automation.

And new research this week found that identity attacks have now overtaken vulnerability exploits as the leading cause of ransomware incidents. Attackers aren't breaking in. They're logging in. And logging in is fast.

Put those four data points together and a pattern emerges that should reshape 2026 security budgets:

The window between "exposure exists" and "you are compromised" has collapsed below the response cadence most organizations planned for.

Here's the uncomfortable math. If your SOC triages alerts on a next-business-day rhythm, a 24-hour ransomware operation completes before your first analyst opens the ticket. If your incident response retainer assumes a two-week engagement tempo, an AI-accelerated attacker finishes in the first quarter of it. If your patch cycle is monthly — and Microsoft just shipped a record 570 fixes in a single month, so good luck — the exploited-in-the-wild subset needs a completely different lane.

Most security programs were architected for an era when attackers were slow and patient. That era is ending. The attackers kept the patience but lost the slowness.

The business consequences aren't hypothetical. This same week, Coca-Cola disclosed that a ransomware attack on its Fairlife dairy subsidiary halted production across the United States. A subsidiary's IT compromise became a nationwide product outage for one of the world's most recognizable brands — in days, not months. Speed of compromise translates directly into speed of business impact, and the balance sheet doesn't care which entity in the org chart got popped.

So what does a decision-maker actually do with this?

Stop funding detection depth and start funding containment speed. The question to put to your security leadership this quarter is not "what's our coverage?" It's "what can we isolate automatically, in hours, without waiting for a human decision?" Segmentation that limits blast radius. Automated session revocation when identity signals go anomalous. Pre-authorized containment playbooks so the 2am decision is already made.

And treat identity as the perimeter it has become. Phishing-resistant MFA and identity threat detection are no longer nice-to-haves competing with the next scanning tool — they address the number one entry vector, today, by the data.

Three days used to be how long it took to schedule the meeting about the vulnerability. Now it's the entire window you get. Plan accordingly.
