**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Ransomware groups are now running their C2 infrastructure on the blockchain. Let that sentence sit for a moment.

Microsoft published a technical breakdown this week of DeadLock ransomware — a Rust-built, double-extortion operation with over 80 confirmed victims, most of them in Europe. The novel part isn't the encryption or the data theft. It's that DeadLock routes its command-and-control through Polygon smart contracts. You can't seize a Polygon contract. You can't get a court order against a blockchain node. The takedown playbook that worked against Conti, against REvil, against LockBit — it doesn't apply here.

At the same time, CISA is out with a multi-agency advisory on Gunra ransomware — another Conti derivative, now operationally mature enough to warrant a joint alert from the FBI, NSA, and the Republic of Korea's National Police Agency. Conti's source code leaked in 2022. Four years later, it's still generating viable threat actors. Leaked code doesn't expire.

Here's what these two stories tell you together: attackers have moved past the "spray and pray" phase. They're building durable infrastructure, solving operational security problems (blockchain C2 is a real solution to a real problem they had), and iterating faster than most enterprise patch cycles.

Meanwhile, on the vulnerability side, today is a heavy day for SAP. CVE-2026-58231 is a CVSS 10.0 in SAP Commerce Cloud — unauthenticated remote code execution. CVE-2026-34265 is a CVSS 9.8 memory corruption in SAP NetWeaver ABAP. CVE-2026-14450 is a CVSS 9.9 that lets any pod in a Kubernetes cluster mint ServiceAccount tokens in other tenants' namespaces by forging two HTTP headers. These are the systems that run supply chains, financial operations, and manufacturing floors. Not edge devices. Not legacy OT gear. Core enterprise infrastructure.

The AI dimension this week is genuinely significant. OpenAI released GPT-5.6-Cyber — a model that completes 95% of exploit chain development tasks, versus 1.5% for the safeguarded version of the same underlying model. Researchers at Nanyang Technological University used an AI tool to find 84 flaws in 5G core networks; 23 remain unpatched, including one that lets an attacker redirect a subscriber's entire data session to themselves.

I'm not raising the AI angle to generate alarm about superintelligent cyberattackers. I'm raising it because the economics of vulnerability discovery have changed. Finding a zero-day used to require rare, expensive human expertise and significant time. AI-assisted discovery compresses both. The backlog of unpatched vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure was already a crisis; the rate at which the backlog grows just increased.

What should decision-makers be thinking about this week?

First: if you use an MSP, ask them specifically about CVE-2026-18556 in N-able N-central. An auth bypass in that platform is a lateral movement vector across every organisation they manage. This is not a question to put in a quarterly review — it's a question for this week.

Second: the "disrupt the attacker's infrastructure" strategy that justified a lot of law enforcement investment in the ransomware space just got significantly harder. Resilience — network segmentation, offline backups, least-privilege access, practiced incident response — is now the primary defence. Not the backup plan.

Third: AI is finding vulnerabilities in production telecommunications infrastructure faster than carriers can patch them. If your sensitive traffic traverses mobile networks and you're not encrypting end-to-end on the assumption the carrier network is secure, review that assumption.

The threat landscape in August 2026 is not more sophisticated than it was a year ago in a generic sense. It's more systematised. That's a different problem, and it needs a different response.
