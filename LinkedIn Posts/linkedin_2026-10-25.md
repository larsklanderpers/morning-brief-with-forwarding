One of the private keys was the number four.

Not a 384-bit random number. Four. Another was 56. Another was 115. That was the state of eight intermediate certificates at eMudhra, a certificate authority trusted by every major browser, and it survived two independent audits over 18 months before a researcher worked it out. This week eMudhra announced it is leaving the certificate business.

I keep coming back to that detail because it captures the week better than any single breach did. The problem was not that controls were missing. The problem was that the reports said the controls were there.

Look at the rest of the week through that lens.

Kaspersky published its analysis of BlackX, a UEFI bootkit it links with medium confidence to APT41. It bypassed Secure Boot on Windows machines that were fully patched. The fix for the underlying 2022 flaw needs an administrator to manually revoke an old Microsoft certificate after installing the update. None of the victims had done it. Their dashboards said green. Their boot chains said otherwise.

Then there is PHP2Shell. Censys counted more than 41 million internet-facing PHP services still on affected versions as of Thursday, against roughly one million on fixed releases. The reason we are not reading about mass compromise is that public exploits keep failing against ASLR in real-world configurations. That is a lab constraint, not a defence, and one research firm says it has already built a way through it. Meanwhile someone has built a scanner, and target lists are being assembled.

And Microsoft's write-up of the ConnectWise supply-chain attack shows what the other side looks like now. A Chinese state-aligned group used an open-source agentic pentesting framework to abuse third-party apps that already held consent in Microsoft 365 tenants. In several cases it went from first targeting to privileged cloud persistence in under three hours, at around 2,900 sign-in attempts a minute.

Put those together and a pattern emerges.

Our security programs are built on status. Patched. Audited. Compliant. Consented. Each of those is a report about a control, and each one can be true on paper while the control is absent in practice. That was always a risk. What has changed is the speed at which the gap gets found. When an attacker's agents can try every path at once, around the clock, a control that only reports as done is a control that will be tested before you get round to verifying it.

So here is the question I would put to any board or executive team this week, and it is a deliberately uncomfortable one:

Which of our "done" controls have we actually verified, and which ones only report as done?

Three practical places to start:

1. Any fix that needs a manual second step. Assume it was not taken until someone shows you evidence.
2. Every third-party app with consent into your core tenant. If you could not list them and revoke them within an hour, that is your real exposure, not your patch percentage.
3. Every trust bundle you do not control: appliances, containers, vendor tools. If you cannot say where they live, you cannot say whether eMudhra's certificates are still trusted inside your estate.

None of this is glamorous. It will not appear in a vendor demo. But the attackers of 2026 are not out-innovating our tooling. They are out-verifying our assumptions.

The audit said the key was fine. The key was four.
