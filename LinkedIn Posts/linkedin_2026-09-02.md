A ransomware group is inside a water utility's OT network. A CI/CD pipeline is pushing malicious builds to production. A phishing email arrived via a legitimate Google Firebase URL, so the gateway let it through.

None of this is hypothetical. All three happened this week.

The unifying thread isn't sophistication — it's speed. Attackers are moving from public vulnerability disclosure to hands-on exploitation in under a week. PaperCut disclosed two zero-days on 27 August; by the weekend, threat actors had escalated from scanning to active intrusion. Federal agencies now have a hard patch deadline of 14 September. The rest of us don't have a mandate — just a closing window.

**The management layer is the new perimeter.**

Every conversation about security still gravitates toward endpoints and email. But the three most consequential vectors right now are sitting above that:

SolarWinds N-Central — the RMM platform MSPs use to manage entire client portfolios — is being actively exploited in Australia via two authentication bypass flaws patched in August. An attacker who gets into your RMM doesn't breach one company; they breach every company you manage.

TeamCity On-Premises CI/CD servers are under active exploitation in Australia. Compromise here doesn't mean data theft — it means your software supply chain is compromised at source. Every build that ships from a hijacked pipeline is a potential delivery vehicle.

Cloudflare, Azure, Google Firebase, AWS. These are the hosts for the latest phishing campaigns — not shady domains in offshore registrars, but the same infrastructure your own applications run on. The URL is clean, the certificate is valid, and your email gateway has no way to distinguish it from legitimate traffic.

**The AI angle isn't future-tense anymore.**

The State of AI Cybersecurity 2026 report puts hyper-personalised phishing as the top concern — ahead of automated exploit chaining, adaptive malware, and deepfake fraud. The WEF found 94% of organisations believe AI is the defining cybersecurity force of 2026. Gartner has global infosec spend reaching $240 billion this year.

That spending number is instructive. It reflects a market that has accepted the premise: you cannot out-manual a machine-speed adversary. The organisations that are still treating cybersecurity as a compliance exercise — patch when the vendor says, train staff once a year, review controls quarterly — are fighting with a calendar against an adversary working on a timer.

**What decision-makers should be asking right now:**

What is our actual patch velocity for critical vulnerabilities — not our target SLA, but our measured time from disclosure to deployment? If it's longer than seven days for internet-facing systems, you are operating in the exploitation window.

Who manages our RMM, CI/CD, and cloud storage infrastructure, and when were access controls last audited? These are no longer backroom IT concerns; they are board-level exposure questions.

What does our email gateway do with a link to `storage.googleapis.com`? If the answer is "passes it," your phishing controls have a gap that training alone cannot close.

Speed asymmetry is the defining risk posture problem of 2026. The organisations closing that gap aren't buying more tools — they're changing the timelines under which security operates.
