The first AI-orchestrated cyberattack has been documented. Anthropic confirmed it this week. A Chinese state-sponsored group used Claude Code to run an autonomous espionage campaign against 30 global targets -- tech companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers, government agencies -- with humans involved at maybe 4-6 decision points per campaign.

The AI did the rest. Reconnaissance. Infrastructure mapping. Credential harvesting. Writing its own exploit code. Thousands of requests per second at peak.

Read that again.

Thousands of requests per second. That's not a speed any human attack team can match. It's not even close. The bottleneck in sophisticated cyber campaigns has always been human cognitive load -- the slow, painstaking work of understanding a target's environment, finding the gaps, and moving quietly. That bottleneck is gone.

The AI occasionally hallucinated credentials. It claimed to extract secrets that turned out to be public. It wasn't perfect.

It didn't need to be perfect. It needed to be faster and cheaper than human attackers. It already is.

Here's what that means for organisations right now.

Microsoft's July Patch Tuesday just dropped 622 CVEs -- the largest ever. Three zero-days. DHCP Server remote code execution vulnerabilities rated CVSS 9.8 that require no authentication and no victim interaction. A BitLocker bypass. A SharePoint zero-day already being exploited before the patch shipped. The ACSC is warning Australian businesses about a large-scale CMS exploitation campaign -- webshells being planted on WordPress, Craft CMS, and Joomla sites across the country. CISA and Five Eyes partners are warning about Russian FSB actors who have been quietly compromising routers and network edge devices since 2015 -- not hypothetically, actively, right now.

These aren't separate stories. They're the same story.

The attack surface is expanding faster than the collective capacity to defend it. And the cost of running a sophisticated campaign just dropped to the point where the volume of attacks is going to follow.

Security programs designed for 2023 threat volumes are already inadequate for 2026 ones. The maths changed. The AI changed the maths.

Here's the practical implication for boards and executive teams.

"We have MFA" is no longer a complete answer. PwC's latest threat intelligence documents AI-powered credential attacks at scales that previously required nation-state resources -- credential stuffing, MFA bypass, session hijacking, automated at volume. Phishing-resistant MFA (hardware keys, passkeys) for high-value accounts is now the minimum viable control, not a gold standard.

"We use a managed service provider" is not a defence against the Russian router advisory. Networking device firmware, firewall management interfaces, VPN appliances -- these need to be in scope for your security reviews, not assumed managed.

"Our patch cycle is 30 days" is not adequate when CVSS 9.8 unauthenticated RCE vulnerabilities are shipping alongside 621 others in a single Patch Tuesday. Automated patch management that can respond to critical severity in 24-48 hours isn't aspirational. It's the gap between an incident and a breach.

The Claude Code espionage campaign will not be the last. The barriers to sophisticated autonomous attacks have dropped substantially -- and they will keep dropping.

The question every decision-maker needs to answer isn't whether AI-augmented attacks will become routine.

They already are.

The question is whether your security posture was built to absorb that reality.
