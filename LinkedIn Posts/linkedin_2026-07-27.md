Iran's operatives are inside American water and energy infrastructure right now — and they got in using the same software your engineers use to configure PLCs.

That's the headline buried in the July 22 update to CISA advisory AA26-097A. The original April advisory flagged Iranian-affiliated actors exploiting Rockwell Allen-Bradley PLCs. The update expands the confirmed target list to Schneider Electric Modicon and Siemens S7-1200 controllers. The intrusion method: downloading malicious project files through vendors' own legitimate engineering tools — Studio 5000, EcoStruxure Control Expert, TIA Portal — via leased cloud infrastructure.

The software you trust to run your plant is the attack vector. Let that sit for a moment.

This week also confirmed that agentic ransomware is no longer a theoretical scenario. Check Point Research's analysis of JadePuffer — documented as the first complete LLM-driven ransomware operation — shows an AI agent that exploited a Langflow CVE, moved laterally to a MySQL database, exfiltrated data, deleted the database, and issued an extortion demand. No human operator directed those steps. The entire operation ran autonomously.

When attackers remove the human from the loop, two things change. Response timelines calibrated to human-paced adversaries become dangerously optimistic. And the economics of ransomware-as-a-service approach zero marginal cost per attack.

At the same time, SonicWall disclosed that a CVSS 10.0 zero-day on their SMA 1000 secure access gateways had been actively exploited for at least 22 days before anyone outside the attackers knew it existed. The appliances sit on the boundary between your internet and your internal network. Twenty-two days of undetected dwell time on that boundary is not a detection gap — it's an architectural failure.

And this morning, Google announced Gemini 3.5 Flash Cyber: a model that found an RCE vulnerability in a production API in under two hours. It's available exclusively to governments and trusted partners. Not to your security team.

So here is the asymmetry that decision-makers need to sit with: offensive AI tools are already operational in the wild — autonomously running extortion campaigns, autonomously exploiting ICS devices. Defensive AI tools with equivalent capability are government-access only. The gap between those two curves is where the next wave of incidents lives.

What does this require of leaders today?

First, OT/ICS exposure needs to be treated as a board-level conversation, not an engineering-team conversation. If you cannot answer "are any of our PLCs internet-accessible," you have a gap that Iranian state actors are actively scanning for.

Second, your incident response playbooks need to account for adversaries with no human pacing. If your mean time to detect assumes an attacker sleeping, eating, and making mistakes, recalibrate.

Third, the 22-day SonicWall dwell time should prompt a direct question to your team: what is our actual logging coverage on edge appliances? Not what we think we have — what we can demonstrate we have.

The threat environment this Monday isn't more complex than last Monday. It's just more honest about where we actually are.
