An AI agent just ran a ransomware attack from start to finish. Break-in, credential theft, encryption — no human at the keyboard.

The agent, dubbed JADEPUFFER, got in through a vulnerability in Langflow. Read that again: an AI attacker exploited a hole in a platform companies use to build AI. The tools we're adopting to automate our businesses are becoming the front door for automated attacks on them.

This isn't an isolated data point. CrowdStrike's latest threat report counts an 89% rise in attacks by AI-enabled adversaries. Average breakout time — the window between initial compromise and lateral movement — is down to 29 minutes.

Twenty-nine minutes. Think about what your organization can do in 29 minutes. Can you convene the incident bridge? Can you even confirm the alert is real? For most enterprises, 29 minutes doesn't cover the time it takes to find the right person's phone number.

Here's where it gets uncomfortable.

The standard answer to AI risk — the one in every governance framework, every vendor deck, every board memo — is "human in the loop." A person reviews what the machine does. Reassuring on paper.

But this week a vulnerability in an AI coding tool showed exactly how that control fails in practice: the human in the loop approves output at a pace and volume no human can meaningfully audit. The review happens. The reviewing doesn't.

"Human in the loop" is becoming the "we have a firewall" of the AI era — a phrase that satisfies auditors while describing a control nobody has load-tested.

And the humans in that loop? They're struggling. One of the most-upvoted posts in the security community this week was a 15-year veteran titled simply "I'm tired boss." The same community is begging moderators to stem the flood of AI-generated content drowning out real expertise. We're asking exhausted people to supervise machines that work at machine speed, while the knowledge channels they rely on fill with noise.

So the question for decision-makers isn't "do we have human oversight?" It's three sharper questions:

For each control where a human is the backstop, what does that person actually verify? Not what the policy says — what they actually look at.

At what volume does that verification become fiction? Ten approvals a day is review. Four hundred is a rubber stamp with a pulse.

Which defensive actions are we prepared to delegate to machines? Because 29-minute breakout times mean some containment decisions must happen faster than any meeting can be scheduled. If you haven't decided which ones, the decision is being made for you — by default, during an incident, at the worst possible moment.

The organizations that will handle the next two years well aren't the ones with the most AI in their security stack. They're the ones that have honestly mapped where human judgment still adds value, staffed those points properly, and automated the rest with clear guardrails.

Autonomy is coming to both sides of the fight. The only real choice is whether you design for it or improvise it.

Where has "human in the loop" become a rubber stamp in your organization? I suspect the honest answers would surprise the people who wrote the policies.
