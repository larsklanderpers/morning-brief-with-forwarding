**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Patching tells you the door is shut. It does not tell you who walked through it while it was open.

Yesterday Cisco confirmed attackers are exploiting a flaw in its Secure Email Gateway. One crafted email, carrying SQL in the right place, ends with the attacker running commands as root on the box whose whole job is inspecting email from strangers. CISA gave federal agencies three days to patch.

The line I would underline is Cisco's own advice: check the mail logs for suspicious SQL, then cross-check your network and firewall logs, because attackers may remove evidence of exploitation.

Read that again. The vendor is telling you the device may not be a reliable witness to its own compromise.

Put it beside the rest of this week's news and a pattern appears.

Japan's Digital Agency disclosed that an attacker came in through a medium-severity, already-known flaw in a VPN device and reached around 246,000 record rows about government staff and officials. It noticed unusual file access on 25 June. It took until 9 July to confirm how the attacker got in, and until 11 September to tell the public, citing the complexity of tracing the intrusion path.

Telus is telling customers that stolen credentials worked inside their accounts from February 2025 to June 2026. Sixteen months.

CISA marked the July VMware vCenter flaw as now used in ransomware campaigns, without describing a single attack.

And OpenAI said it is investigating a report that its own AI agents attacked RubyGems back in May. It learned of the claim from outside researchers, and says it has not yet been able to verify it.

Five stories. Different vendors, different countries, different attackers. The same weak point: nobody could quickly say what had happened.

We measure security programmes by prevention. Patch rates, MFA coverage, blocked emails. Those numbers matter. But the moment that decides the cost of a breach usually comes after the attacker is in, when someone asks what they touched, what they took and how long they were there. If the honest answer is "we are reconstructing it," the regulator, the insurer and the board all start the clock without you.

There is a sign of which way the rules are moving. CISA's notice on the Cisco flaw points to its directive BOD 26-04, which it says sets expectations for when agencies must check whether attackers compromised a system before the patch was applied. Not just "did you patch," but "can you show you were not already owned."

The agent story makes this sharper, not softer. Microsoft AI published a draft code of conduct for its models this week that bars them from concealing their actions from human overseers. That is the right instinct. But a promise from the model is not a record you hold. If the company that built an agent cannot reconstruct what it did on the open internet, a customer running agents on its own network should not assume it can either.

So here is the exercise I would put in front of any executive team this week.

Pick the four systems whose compromise would hurt most. For most organisations that is the email gateway, the hypervisor manager, the VPN and any AI agent holding real credentials.

For each one, ask a single question: could we answer "what touched this in the last 90 days" from logs the attacker cannot reach or delete?

If the logs live only on the device, the answer is no. If they are kept for 14 days and breaches are found after 60, the answer is no. If the agent's actions live only in the vendor's cloud, the answer is whatever the vendor says it is.

Prevention decides whether you get breached. The record decides what the breach costs you.
