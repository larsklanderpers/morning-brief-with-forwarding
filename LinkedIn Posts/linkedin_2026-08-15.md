The attackers aren't moving faster. They've changed the economics of the game entirely.

This week's threat landscape made that plain. Microsoft patched 421 CVEs on Tuesday — a number that should stop anyone cold. Sixty-two were critical. One was a zero-day already being exploited in the wild, almost certainly by nation-state actors: a use-after-free in Windows' core socket driver (afd.sys) that turns local access into SYSTEM privileges. Tenable researchers noted that every previous afd.sys zero-day since 2022 has been linked to nation-state groups, including North Korea's Lazarus. That's not coincidence — it's a toolchain.

At the same time, CISA and five partner agencies issued a joint advisory on Gunra ransomware — a double-extortion operation built from leaked Conti source code, now operating as a structured ransomware-as-a-service platform. Criminal infrastructure that used to require serious technical capability to build now comes as a franchise package. The barrier to running a professional ransomware campaign has dropped to roughly the same level as starting any other subscription business.

Then there's the supply chain story that got misattributed. Initial reporting blamed a compromise hitting 2,500+ organisations on LiteLLM, an AI tool. Turned out to be Trivy — the open-source container vulnerability scanner that lives in CI/CD pipelines with elevated access to build systems. The error matters less than what it illustrates: security tooling is now a prime target precisely because it sits closest to the systems defenders are trying to protect. Your scanner is your attack surface.

And underlying all of it, SecurityWeek ran a pointed expert column this week making an argument that deserves board-level attention: if an AI can write a working exploit from a public vulnerability description in under 20 hours, the patching window has collapsed below any realistic enterprise response cycle. You cannot out-patch a machine that turns CVEs into weaponised code overnight. The question isn't "how do we patch faster?" — it's "what happens to our business model if we can't?"

The ACSC published board-level guidance this week on Frontier AI cyber threats. The timing is not coincidental. Regulators are watching AI accelerate on both sides of the threat equation and are starting to make governance a board accountability question rather than an IT hygiene matter.

The organisations that will fare best through the next 18 months are not the ones with the largest security teams or the fastest patch cycles. They're the ones that have already shifted their mental model: assume breach, shrink the exploitable surface, make lateral movement expensive, and ensure their vendors' security posture is part of their own risk picture. Clop took 874GB from FIS Global this week. If you use FIS, that's your data question to answer, not FIS's.

The convergence of professional ransomware operations, AI-accelerated exploit development, and attacks targeting developer infrastructure isn't a trend line to watch. It's the current state of play.

What's your organisation doing differently in response to AI-accelerated threat development? I'd genuinely like to hear from practitioners.
