The US federal bankruptcy courts could not send a single notice this week. Nobody hacked the courts.

They hacked the post room.

The Administrative Office of the US Courts confirmed on Thursday that a cyberattack on BAE Systems, the contractor that runs the Bankruptcy Noticing Center, has taken those systems and websites offline. The courts are still open. They just cannot do one of the things the law requires them to do, because the function lives with someone else.

That is the pattern in almost every story I read this morning.

BeyondTrust patched a Remote Support zero-day that Mandiant and Google say a suspected China-nexus espionage crew has been exploiting since late August. Remote support software is a master key you give to outsiders on purpose. Seven weeks of quiet use before disclosure means the patch closes the door, but it says nothing about who is already inside.

Self-hosted Nextcloud servers are being backdoored through a flaw in the Collabora document-editing app that ships alongside them. Many organisations chose self-hosted Nextcloud for sovereignty reasons: keep the files onshore, off the hyperscalers. That is a sound instinct. But sovereignty means you also own the patch cadence of every plug-in someone switched on once for a demo.

And the Citizen Lab, working in parallel with ad-security firm Confiant, says an advertising platform was built to deliver Candiru spyware to targets the moment an ad rendered on their screen. Programmatic advertising is a global system for running a stranger's code on your device, auctioned in milliseconds. Someone worked out it targets people as well as it targets shoppers.

Four stories, one shape. In each case the organisation handed a function to someone else, a contractor, a tool, a plug-in, an ad network, and then stopped thinking of it as theirs.

Outsourcing is not the mistake. Nobody should build their own bankruptcy notice system or remote support platform. The mistake is believing that handing over the function also hands over the accountability. It does not. Regulators will not accept "our vendor was breached" as the end of the conversation, and neither will customers whose data walked out through someone else's door.

In my world, gaming technology for clubs and casinos across the US and Australia, this is not abstract. A venue's member data, its payments, its cooling and its security cameras increasingly run on services operated by third parties. The breach that hurts a club next year is more likely to start at a supplier than at the club.

So here is the exercise I would ask every executive team to do before the end of the month.

Write down every function you have outsourced that, if it stopped tomorrow, would stop you meeting a legal, regulatory or customer obligation. Next to each one, write the name of the person on your side who would notice first. Not the vendor's account manager. Your person.

Then write down the manual fallback.

If either column is blank, you have found your real risk register. It will be shorter than the one you already have, and far more useful.

Your attack surface is not your network anymore. It is your supplier list.
