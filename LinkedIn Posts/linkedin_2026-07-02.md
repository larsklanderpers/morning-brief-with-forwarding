Your security team has 29 minutes.

That's the average time between initial compromise and lateral movement in 2026. Not 29 hours. Not 29 minutes from detection. Twenty-nine minutes from the moment an adversary gets in until they're already somewhere else in your environment.

CrowdStrike's latest data puts it bluntly: AI-enabled adversaries have compressed the breakout window by 65% in two years. The security model most organisations are still running — detect, alert, escalate, review, respond — was built for a world where that window was measured in hours. That world no longer exists.

This week's threat landscape makes that clear in concrete terms.

**81 million login attempts. Two weeks. 78 accounts compromised across 64 organisations.**

That's the scale of the automated Azure CLI password-spray campaign running through mid-June. Not a targeted intrusion — a machine-speed, industrial-scale credential harvesting operation. And it worked. The accounts it hit weren't consumer inboxes — they were Azure CLI credentials. Developer credentials. Pipeline credentials. The keys to cloud infrastructure, not just email.

Meanwhile, CISA added a SharePoint Server RCE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and gave US federal agencies 48 hours to patch. Forty-eight hours. That's not a standard remediation window — that's CISA saying this is already being exploited at a tempo that makes normal change management timelines dangerous.

And beneath all of this: AI is no longer just accelerating attackers. It's becoming the attack surface itself.

Darktrace's 2026 research documents more than 90 organisations where legitimate enterprise AI tools were weaponised — used to generate malicious commands and exfiltrate data from inside the perimeter, authenticated, indistinguishable from legitimate use. The WEF's Global Cybersecurity Outlook puts 94% of organisations on record: AI is the dominant force reshaping threat in 2026. Security leaders' top concerns now include self-mutating malware and AI-driven evasion techniques.

The US government's decision to impose export restrictions on Anthropic's Fable 5 — days after its public release — is the clearest possible signal that regulators have concluded frontier AI models cross a dual-use threshold that justifies trade controls. That's historically significant. It puts AI models in the same regulatory category as encryption tools and defence hardware.

So what does this actually demand of decision-makers?

Three things:

First, the response-time assumption at the heart of your security architecture is broken. If your SOC's alert-to-response median is hours, the 29-minute breakout window means you are systematically late. Automated detection and response isn't a nice-to-have — it's the only way to operate at adversary speed.

Second, credential hygiene across DevOps and cloud toolchains needs immediate attention. The Azure CLI campaign compromised accounts that most organisations don't think of as high-risk targets. Developer tokens and pipeline credentials are increasingly the path of least resistance into production environments.

Third, AI governance is now a security control requirement, not an IT policy question. If legitimate AI tools in your environment can be used to generate malicious outputs and exfiltrate data, "acceptable use" training is insufficient. You need audit, monitoring, and behavioural detection applied to AI tooling — the same way you'd apply it to any privileged system.

The organisations that survive 2026's threat environment aren't the ones with the biggest security budgets. They're the ones that have genuinely recalibrated their assumptions about how fast this is moving.

Twenty-nine minutes is not a buffer. It's a forcing function.
