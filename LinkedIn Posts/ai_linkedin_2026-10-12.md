# AI LinkedIn Post - 12 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

For seventeen days, Britain's AI Security Institute could not look at the frontier models it exists to test.

Not because of a technical failure. Because the White House asked the labs to stop sharing. On Sunday, according to The Telegraph, that request was reversed after some quiet lobbying in Washington.

Read that again as a CISO would. The only independent, government-run test bench outside the US was switched off and back on by a phone call. Its access was never a right. It was a courtesy.

That is the whole data sovereignty argument in one fortnight.

The same weekend, Microsoft's president Brad Smith told the BBC that AI is less regulated than milk, and called for mandatory incident reporting and external evaluation before release. A day earlier Satya Nadella asked for an "emergency brake": documented actions, external control, the ability to pause a model mid-task.

It is worth naming the interest. Microsoft sells governance tooling and competes with the labs it is nudging. But the proposals are not wrong. They are what any of us would demand of a payments processor or a cloud host. Tell me when you fail. Let someone independent check. Give me a switch.

Now look at the other side of the market.

A SemiAnalysis review reported by Reuters found that Chinese labs published a safety evaluation for 3.6% of 857 model releases. Those are the open-weight models many teams are quietly running on their own hardware, precisely because local deployment solves the residency problem.

It does. It does not solve the evidence problem. When you download the weights, you inherit the safety case. If nobody published one, you are writing it.

And the licence matters as much as the weights. StepFun's Step-5 is already live on third-party servers with no official licence, days before its promised open release. An open model without a licence is a lawsuit with a download button.

Here in Australia, the clock is specific. The Privacy Act's automated decision-making transparency duties start on 10 December, and the Information Commissioner keeps repeating the date. Separately, reports suggest Canberra is drafting standards that would make developers prove their safety systems work, borrowing from banking and aviation. Both put the burden of proof on the operator.

So what should a business holding sensitive customer data actually do?

Stop outsourcing assurance.

1. Keep your own evidence chain for every model that touches personal information: licence text, your own evaluation results, and the date you last ran them.
2. Finish your register of automated decisions and your disclosure wording this month, not on 9 December.
3. Put a kill switch in your architecture that you control, not one that lives in a vendor's console.
4. Treat any assurance that depends on someone else's permission to look as provisional.

Capability you can rent. Accountability you cannot.

What is the one control in your AI stack that would still work if your vendor's priorities changed overnight?
