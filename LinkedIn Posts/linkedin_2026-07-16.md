**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Microsoft just shipped 570 patches in a single day. A working exploit for a new Windows zero-day was public within hours.

That's not a patching problem. That's a physics problem.

Think of it like a fire department that takes three weeks to respond to a fire that spreads in 29 minutes. That's not an exaggeration — 29 minutes is now the average "breakout time" for eCrime actors moving laterally after initial access, per CrowdStrike's latest threat report. It's 65% faster than two years ago, driven largely by AI-enabled tooling.

Meanwhile, the defensive side is still running on calendars. Patch Tuesday. Quarterly reviews. Monthly vulnerability scans.

Three stories from the last 48 hours tell the same story from different angles:

Microsoft's July Patch Tuesday was the largest on record — 570 flaws, roughly 60 critical, including actively exploited holes in SharePoint and Active Directory Federation Services, plus two unauthenticated remote-code-execution bugs in Windows DHCP Server scoring 9.8. No enterprise patch team on earth triages 570 items quickly. The ones that survive this month will be the ones that ruthlessly prioritise the handful under active exploitation and let the rest queue.

CISA and international partners published a joint advisory on Russian FSB Center 16 actors compromising internet-exposed network devices around the world. Not with exotic zero-days — with misconfigurations and unpatched edge gear that's been sitting there for years. Australia's ACSC is simultaneously warning about active campaigns against Fortinet firewalls and VPN gateways. The devices we bought to secure the perimeter have become the perimeter's weakest point.

And hackers breached TRICARE West, exposing health data of US military beneficiaries — records that are simultaneously a criminal commodity and a foreign-intelligence asset.

Here's the thread connecting them: offensive and defensive timelines have decoupled. Attackers operate in minutes and hours. Defenders operate in weeks and quarters. And every year, AI widens that gap on the offensive side faster than most organisations close it on the defensive side.

So what do you do when you can't out-sprint the adversary?

You stop trying to win a footrace and start changing the track.

Three moves for decision-makers:

First, spend remediation capacity where exploitation is actually happening. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is a free, curated priority list. If your patching SLAs are driven by CVSS scores instead of active exploitation, you're optimising for the wrong number.

Second, treat your network edge as contested territory. Inventory every internet-facing device this quarter. The FSB doesn't need to be clever if your VPN gateway is misconfigured and forgotten.

Third, decide — deliberately, in advance — how much response authority you're willing to delegate to automation. A 29-minute breakout doesn't leave time to convene a call. The organisations that handle the next major incident well will be the ones that made that delegation decision in a conference room, not during the breach.

The uncomfortable truth of July 2026: your security operating model was probably designed for an adversary tempo that no longer exists. The question isn't whether to redesign it. It's whether you do it before or after the 29 minutes that matter.

What's your organisation's honest answer?
