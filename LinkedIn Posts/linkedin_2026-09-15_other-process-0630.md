One human. One AI framework. 395 organisations across 48 countries — compromised in parallel.

That's what happened with PaperCut. A single Russian-speaking operator deployed hundreds of AI agents simultaneously: finding targets, developing exploits, conducting attacks — all at once. The operation that would have required a nation-state team running for weeks was done in what looks like hours.

We keep talking about AI-enabled attacks as a future threat. They are a present operational reality.

Three things happened this week that, taken together, tell you where we actually are.

**First: the timeline has collapsed.** Separately documented this week, a human ransomware operator using frontier AI models and agentic frameworks fully compromised an enterprise network in under ten hours. The same operation previously took skilled human teams around two weeks. That's not an incremental improvement in attacker efficiency — it's a structural change in the game. Most enterprise security operations are built around mean-time-to-detect measured in days. That window no longer exists.

**Second: the regulatory clock is now live.** As of yesterday — 14 September 2026 — the EU Cyber Resilience Act's mandatory incident reporting regime kicked in. Manufacturers selling products with digital elements into the EU must now notify ENISA's Single Reporting Platform of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents. The notification window is 24 hours. If you haven't stress-tested your incident response process against a 24-hour notification requirement, you haven't stress-tested it at all.

**Third: the patch backlog is industrially unsustainable.** Microsoft shipped 1,169 CVEs in this month's Patch Tuesday alone. Two critical Windows DNS RCEs at CVSS 9.8 — one reachable by unauthenticated attackers. A perfect-score Azure AD B2C authorisation bypass. Twenty-two critical Office patches, twelve of which are exploitable just by opening the Preview Pane. At 1,169 CVEs in a single month, manual triage is not a process — it's a fiction. Organisations without automated risk-scoring in their patch pipeline are making prioritisation decisions on vibes.

What does this add up to?

The asymmetry that has always characterised cyber conflict — attackers need to succeed once, defenders need to succeed every time — has been amplified by an order of magnitude. AI gives attackers parallel scale that human defenders cannot match on human timelines. The regulatory environment now compresses the disclosure window to hours. And the raw volume of vulnerabilities has passed the threshold where human-speed patch management can keep pace.

The answer isn't more headcount. It's rebuilding security operations around AI-speed detection, automated triage, and response playbooks that don't require a human to initiate. That transition is not a future project. It needed to start yesterday.

For boards and executives reading this: the question is no longer whether AI-enabled attacks will affect your organisation. The question is whether your security investment assumes the attacker timeline is hours or weeks. If it assumes weeks, the model is wrong.
