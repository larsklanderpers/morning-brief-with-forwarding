**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The Five Eyes just told us AI can break into government systems in months, not years. This week, we got the proof of concept — and it wasn't a nation-state.

It was a ransomware group, a university ERP system, and a two-week head start.

ShinyHunters exploited a CVSS 9.8 zero-day in Oracle PeopleSoft between May 27 and June 9. Oracle didn't publish its advisory until June 10. That means the vulnerability was unknown to defenders — and fully weaponized — for the entire duration of the attack. The result: 100+ organizations breached, 455,000 student records exposed, and an extortion campaign actively underway. Sixty-eight percent of victims are universities — institutions with large attack surfaces, lean security teams, and ERP systems that are routinely excluded from normal patch cycles because they're "too critical to touch."

That phrase — "too critical to touch" — is now the single most dangerous sentence in enterprise security.

This week also brought Microsoft's largest-ever Patch Tuesday: 206 vulnerabilities, three zero-days, and multiple wormable CVEs with CVSS scores of 9.8. One of them, a Windows Kernel flaw, can self-propagate with no user interaction. A BitLocker bypass dropped with a working proof of concept. And separately, CISA set a June 28 deadline — tomorrow — for federal agencies to patch an actively exploited Cisco flaw that allows unauthenticated attackers to write files to the OS and escalate to root.

The patch window is no longer measured in weeks. New data shows that 28.3% of CVEs are now exploited within 24 hours of disclosure. That number is AI-driven. Automated vulnerability discovery and exploit generation have turned the old 30-day patch cycle into a liability.

And then came the Five Eyes statement.

The intelligence agencies of the US, UK, Australia, Canada, and New Zealand issued a joint warning that frontier AI models are improving fast enough to outsmart government and enterprise defenses within months. The warning followed a report that an AI agent penetrated nearly all classified systems managed by the NSA and U.S. Cyber Command during a testing exercise — in hours.

Let that land for a moment.

The same AI capabilities that are accelerating defensive security — threat detection, vulnerability analysis, anomaly identification — are simultaneously enabling offensive operations at a pace and scale that human defenders cannot match manually. The advantage is going to whichever side deploys AI faster and more effectively.

For boards and executive teams, this week's news is not a collection of unrelated incidents. It's a pattern: velocity is the defining characteristic of the modern threat landscape. Attackers are moving faster than patch cycles. Faster than vendor disclosure timelines. Faster than security teams triaging a 206-vulnerability Patch Tuesday.

Three things decision-makers should lock in this quarter:

First — identify your "too critical to touch" systems and build an emergency patching capability for them specifically. ERP platforms, communications infrastructure, DHCP and HTTP-layer services — these are now primary targets, not secondary ones.

Second — assume your employees' credentials are already in circulation. The 24 billion records exposed this month are not an isolated event; they're the latest addition to a credential market that has been compounding for years. Phishing-resistant MFA and passkey adoption are not future roadmap items. They're the baseline.

Third — get ahead of the AI threat timeline. The Five Eyes warning is a marker: the window between "AI as emerging risk" and "AI as active adversary in your environment" has closed faster than almost anyone predicted. Defensive AI investment needs to move from strategy slide to funded program — now.

The attackers already know what's at stake. The question is whether the defenders do.
