Japan's banks have been told to stop trusting driving licences.

That is the line I keep coming back to from the past few days. According to the Financial Times, Japan's digital minister used the words "state of emergency in cyberspace" on Friday, after roughly twenty major companies reported breaches in a few weeks. Kyodo, citing the National Police Agency, puts the ransomware count at 53 companies or more. And the financial regulator responded with something more telling than any statistic: it warned banks not to accept driving licences as proof of identity, after a car-sharing firm lost a trove of scanned IDs.

Think about what that means. A breach used to be a cost to the company that was breached. Here it has become a cost to every bank onboarding flow in the country. Identity itself got more expensive.

Three other stories from this morning's brief have the same shape.

Daiichi Kosho, Japan's biggest karaoke operator, disclosed that malware on a single employee's computer at its contractor, Nippon Columbia, exposed records for 8.6 million customers. The operator did not lose the data. Its vendor did. The operator still owns the breach.

Symantec reports that the crew behind Warlock ransomware is still getting into water utilities, telecoms and government through SharePoint flaws that were patched more than a year ago. Patched is not the same as clean. If attackers took your machine keys before the fix, the fix did not evict them.

And coverage of the Japan and South Korea campaigns keeps returning to AI-assisted tooling that compresses an attacker's preparation from days to minutes. Read past the AI headline, though, and the finding underneath is old: several victims lacked basic identity verification and access controls. AI did not invent the gap. It just found it faster.

Put those together and the common failure is not technology. It is custody.

Who holds the data? Often a vendor you have not audited since the contract was signed.

Who holds the keys? Possibly someone who got them before you patched.

Who checked after the fix? Too often, nobody, because "patched" closed the ticket.

In my own world, gaming and hospitality across the US and Australia, this lands close to home. Venues scan IDs for age checks, membership and responsible gambling obligations. Many keep those scans long after the check is done, sometimes at a third-party provider. Japan has just shown what happens when enough of those copies leak: the document stops working as identity for everyone.

So here is the takeaway I would put to any board in a regulated industry this week.

Make a list of every place a copy of a customer ID image, credential or session token lives. Include your vendors and their vendors. Next to each one, write down why that copy still needs to exist.

Every line you cannot justify is a liability you are carrying for free. Delete it, or put a date on it.

AI is making attackers faster. The best response is not a faster defence. It is having less to steal.
