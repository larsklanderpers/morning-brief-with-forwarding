**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The compliance clock just ran out of runway.

This week's numbers: 55% of enterprise AI inference is now running on-premise, up from 12% three years ago. Open-weight models are three months behind frontier capability — down from seven. The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations went live on 2 August. Australia's Privacy Act automated decision-making amendments land 10 December. And the Stripe acquisition of OpenRouter just centralised the billing and routing layer for the entire industry inside a payments company.

None of this happened suddenly. But the window for treating it as a future problem closed this week.

Here's what I keep coming back to: the organisations that will be fine in December aren't the ones that have perfect AI governance frameworks. They're the ones that asked the right question three months ago — "where does inference actually run?" — and followed the answer to its logical conclusion.

That question sounds simple. It isn't. You can have an AI vendor claiming "Australian hosting" who routes your queries through Singapore during peak load. You can sign a data processing agreement that satisfies GDPR on paper while being fully exposed to the US CLOUD Act because your provider is Delaware-incorporated. You can have a risk committee that reviews AI use cases and an audit trail that an operator holds but no independent verifier can read.

Visible governance isn't the same as governance capability. That distinction matters a lot more when the penalties are AUD $50M or 6% of global turnover.

The practical version of this is actually straightforward. Audit where inference runs — not where data is stored. Understand which of your AI decision flows meet the December definition of "automated decision-making" under the amended Privacy Act. Read your AI vendors' training data disclosure policies (63% of the top 500 AI companies don't have one). And if your staff are uploading internal documents to free-tier AI tools, assume those documents are training data until you have a contract that says otherwise.

The organisations that are going to be explaining incidents to the OAIC and the APRA next year are the ones that treated AI compliance as a legal checkbox. The ones that won't are the ones that treated it as an infrastructure question — the same way they treat network security and data backup.

The window for the second category is still open. But not for long.
