Here is a test for your platform team. If every secret in your deployment platform leaked tonight, how many of them would still work tomorrow morning?

For most organisations I talk to, the honest answer is "all of them". This week showed why that matters.

On 21 October, someone took over the release pipeline of LiteLLM, an open-source AI gateway with a reported 92 million monthly downloads. For under two hours, the official package carried a stealer that ran every time Python started. It went for cloud credentials, Kubernetes configs, SSH keys, .env files and the API keys for every AI model provider the gateway was connected to.

Security firms flagged it within six minutes. That is impressive, and it did not help anyone whose build pulled "latest" in that window.

The same week, cloud host Railway told all of its users to rotate every secret stored in their environment variables. An attacker may have obtained decrypted values, and Railway says the attack was part of a series aimed at AI inference providers. Railway paused deployments across the platform while it investigated.

Put those two together and the target is obvious. An AI gateway is a keyring by design. Its job is to hold the keys to every model you pay for, and often the keys to the data those models read. Attackers have worked that out faster than most security programmes have.

Now add the timing problem.

Amazon says attackers were probing the PHP2Shell flaws on 17 October, four days before the patch existed. Fortinet has asked customers to apply critical updates across FortiOS, FortiManager and more, and Australia's ACSC says the details will not arrive until 10 November. Arista patched its fourth exploited zero-day of the year in the console that manages entire SD-WAN estates.

So the picture is this: the secrets are the prize, and the window between "attacker knows" and "defender knows" is getting wider, not narrower. You cannot patch your way out of that. You can only make the prize worth less.

That is the shift I would push for in any board conversation this quarter:

1. Stop treating environment variables as a vault. They were designed for configuration, not for holding the keys to production. Move model, database and registry credentials into a secrets manager.

2. Make credentials short-lived. A key that expires in an hour turns a platform breach from a crisis into an inconvenience. Static keys turn it into a weekend of rotation and a regulator notification.

3. Pin what sits between your apps and your models. AI gateways and agent frameworks should be installed by version and hash, not by "latest". They hold too much to be updated on trust.

4. Put a budget and a fallback on AI dependencies. On 22 October, CISA and Canada's Cyber Centre published guidance warning about "denial-of-wallet" attacks and token exhaustion. A stolen API key is not just a data risk. It is someone else spending your quota until your customer-facing service stops.

None of this is exotic. It is the same discipline we applied to database passwords a decade ago, now applied to the newest and most valuable credentials in the estate. The organisations that do it will treat the next LiteLLM as a Tuesday. The ones that do not will find out what their AI keys were really worth.

Rotation after the breach is the expensive version. Expiry before it is the cheap one.
