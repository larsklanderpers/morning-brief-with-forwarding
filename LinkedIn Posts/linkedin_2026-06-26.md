The malware analyst's AI tool was lying to them.

Not because the model was wrong. Because the attackers had engineered the malware to fool it.

"Gaslight" — a North Korean macOS implant disclosed this week by SentinelOne — embeds fabricated system-failure messages specifically designed to trigger an LLM-assisted triage agent into refusing the analysis. It doesn't attack the sandbox. It attacks the *perception* of the AI running inside it.

That's the inflection point we're at in 2026.

Adversaries are no longer just evading antivirus signatures or EDR agents. They're studying *how defenders deploy AI* and engineering their payloads against the AI layer itself. Gaslight is the first documented live example. It won't be the last.

This week's news cycle made the pattern hard to miss.

Researchers published a self-replicating AI worm that runs entirely on local, open-weight models — no cloud API, no centralised infrastructure required. Air-gapping commercial AI access as a control is now obsolete. The worm propagates autonomously, adapts, and operates offline.

Novee Security disclosed "Cordyceps" — a CI/CD workflow flaw allowing any unauthenticated GitHub account to push code, forge approvals, or steal credentials across 300+ major repositories, including organisations like Microsoft, Google, Apache, and Cloudflare. One free account. Unlimited supply chain blast radius.

And Mandiant confirmed that a threat actor was inside Cisco's Catalyst SD-WAN infrastructure for at least two months before the vulnerability was publicly disclosed — using anti-forensic techniques to selectively delete and restore configuration files to avoid detection. Two months inside the network fabric. Controlling routing, segmentation, visibility. Patching after disclosure doesn't undo any of that.

What connects these stories isn't sophistication for its own sake. It's *strategic patience combined with architectural exploitation*.

The Cisco SD-WAN actor wasn't rushing. The Gaslight malware developers weren't improvising. The Cordyceps researchers found a flaw hiding in plain sight in GitHub's most "trusted" workflows. These are deliberate choices to attack the seams of how organisations have built their defences — the extension with a Featured badge (10 million installs, arbitrary JavaScript execution on demand), the CI/CD pipeline everyone assumes is safe, the AI tool analysts have learned to trust.

The UK's NCSC put the geopolitical dimension bluntly this week: 75% of the 200+ critical infrastructure incidents they handled this year trace back to state actors. The word they used was "prepositioning." These aren't intrusions for intelligence collection. They're battlefield preparation.

Here's what decision-makers need to reckon with:

The trust model is broken. Not the technical controls — the *trust*. We've outsourced confidence to vendors (Chrome's "Featured" badge), to platforms (GitHub's review process), to tools (AI-assisted triage), and to frameworks (SD-WAN as network controller). Adversaries have catalogued every one of those trusted signals and are working their way through them.

The answer isn't paranoia. It's *zero trust applied to trust itself* — out-of-band verification for high-stakes decisions, adversarial testing of AI tooling before deployment, supply chain reviews that include the CI/CD layer, and assume-breach posture on every piece of network infrastructure.

The patch cycle cadence we've used for 20 years isn't viable against agentic adversaries who can weaponise a CVE in hours. The question for boards and CISOs isn't "are we patched?" It's "what are we trusting that we shouldn't be — and what does an attacker do the moment that trust is exploited?"

Start there.
