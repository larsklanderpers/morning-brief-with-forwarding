**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The company you hired to verify identities for you just handed 153 million of them to cybercriminals.

That's the IDScan.net situation. The FBI confirmed it Thursday. A dark-web marketplace called "Nexus" is now selling full front-and-back scans of US and Canadian driver's licenses — with infrared and ultraviolet captures — alongside 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents, and half a million medical cards. The timestamps on the data trace back to specific IDScan transactions: car rental counters, retail ID checks, dispensary verifications.

IDScan.net wasn't the target. It was the method.

This is what a trust-chain attack looks like at scale. Every organisation that plugged IDScan into their identity verification workflow — and trusted that third party to handle the sensitive end of KYC — now owns a share of that breach, regardless of whether they touched the data themselves. Their customers are the ones looking at compromised identity documents on a dark web storefront.

At almost exactly the same time this week, VMware vCenter CVE-2026-59310 went from "critical patch released" to "ransomware deploying through it" in five days. Five days. Across 47 countries. The attack chain runs from the path traversal exploit into a reverse SSH binary into Babuk-derived ransomware — and it's targeting the hypervisor, the layer every virtual machine in the data centre trusts by definition.

And then there's Langflow. The AI agent framework, CVSS 9.8, actively exploited, attackers querying environment variables to harvest OpenAI API keys and AWS credentials. It's the 12th exploited Langflow CVE in 2026. Attackers aren't trying to compromise AI models. They're going after the credentials the models were given to do their work.

The common thread is legible once you see it: **attackers are targeting the infrastructure that other infrastructure trusts.**

Not the endpoint. Not the user. The trusted intermediary: the identity verification layer, the hypervisor control plane, the AI pipeline credential store. One successful compromise of a trusted node and every relying party downstream is exposed simultaneously.

This is the architecture of the current threat landscape, and it demands a different question in the boardroom. Not "are our vendors secure?" — every CISO already asks that. The question is: "What is our blast radius if every vendor we currently trust is compromised at the same time?" Because that's the scenario attackers are already running. Not in theory. This week.

Three immediate calls for decision-makers:

**On IDScan:** Get legal and risk on this today. If you used IDScan.net for identity verification, your indemnity position and breach notification obligations need to be clear before Monday. Class actions are already filed.

**On vCenter:** If your virtualisation team is still on a monthly patching cadence for critical infrastructure, that cadence just became an open invitation. Five days is the window. Adjust accordingly.

**On AI pipelines:** Rotate credentials. Every environment variable holding a cloud provider key, model API key, or database string in a Python-based AI framework — rotate it now, not after the next security review. Langflow is the current vector; the pattern applies to every similar framework.

The trusted intermediary is the attack surface of 2026. The organisations that understand that earliest will be the ones that don't spend next week doing breach notifications.
