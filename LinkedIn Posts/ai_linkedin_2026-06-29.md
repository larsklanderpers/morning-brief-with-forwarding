# AI LinkedIn Post — 29 June 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

The most powerful open-weight AI model ever released dropped its weights two weeks ago.

Most Australian boardrooms haven't heard of it.

GLM-5.2 — a 753 billion parameter model from Z.ai — is MIT-licensed, runs on your own hardware, and outperforms GPT-5.5 on coding benchmarks. You can download it right now and run it inside your own infrastructure, with zero data leaving your network. At 1/6th the cost of OpenAI's flagship.

On the same day I'm writing this, APRA's April governance letter is sitting unread in the inboxes of hundreds of compliance teams. That letter named four governance failures it observed across major banks, insurers, and super funds. One of them: dangerous vendor concentration in AI, with no tested exit strategies when a model disappears.

That's not hypothetical. It happened last week. The US government issued an export control directive and Anthropic pulled Fable 5 and Mythos 5 from the market overnight. If your business was relying on those models, your contingency plan just got stress-tested without warning.

Here's the tension at the centre of AI adoption in mid-2026:

The tools for privacy-compliant, data-sovereign AI have never been better. GLM-5.2. MiniMax M3. Ollama v0.30.8 running on Apple Silicon. OpenAI's Privacy Filter — an open-source, on-device PII sanitisation model you can deploy for free. The technical barriers to running frontier-capable AI without sending a single byte of customer data offshore are effectively gone.

But the governance barriers are getting taller, not shorter.

Australia's Privacy Act amendments kick in December 10. If your organisation uses algorithmic scoring, recommendation engines, or AI-assisted workflows that affect customer outcomes — hiring, lending, insurance, customer analytics — you have less than six months to document and disclose. The penalty ceiling is AUD 50 million.

APRA CPS 230 took effect July 1. Voice AI vendors are now classified as material service providers. You need a formal register, business continuity planning, and tested substitution strategies.

And in Europe, the EU AI Act's high-risk compliance obligations — deferred to December 2027 under the Digital Omnibus — are accelerating the same foundational work: AI system inventories, data flow mapping, human oversight documentation.

Two compliance timelines. One underlying question: can you actually demonstrate that your AI deployments meet your privacy and data residency obligations?

The organisations getting this right aren't waiting for regulators to tell them what to do. They're building hybrid architectures — local inference for PII-heavy workloads, cloud AI for non-sensitive tasks — and they're doing AI system audits now, not in November.

The organisations getting it wrong are still treating "our data is stored in Australia" as a sufficient answer. It isn't. APRA and the OAIC are increasingly distinguishing storage residency from processing residency. If your inference engine is overseas, the risk is material — even if the data centre is in Sydney.

The capability is here. The regulation is coming. The board literacy required to connect those two facts is still the critical gap.

That's the AI story for the second half of 2026.

What's your organisation's answer to "where does the inference happen?"
