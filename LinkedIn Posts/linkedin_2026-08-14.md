Five days.

That's how long it took an APT actor to compromise 361 VMware vCenter systems across 47 countries after Broadcom disclosed the flaw. Not five weeks. Five days — and by day seven, 95% of the eventual victims were already owned.

This isn't a story about a bad vulnerability. It's a story about a broken assumption: that "patch when tested" is a viable security posture for critical infrastructure.

Here's what happened this week that should be concentrating minds:

**Microsoft pushed 421 patches — 62 of them critical — on Tuesday.** One was already a zero-day being actively exploited. Three more were unauthenticated remote code execution flaws at CVSS 9.8, touching Windows DNS, the QUIC protocol stack, and Deployment Services. CISA's patch deadline is August 25. That's eleven days for your security team to test and deploy fixes across foundational network infrastructure — infrastructure that most organizations deliberately slow-track because a failed patch breaks production.

Attackers understand this. They're betting on the gap.

**The same week, CISA joined the FBI, NSA, and South Korea's national police to warn about Gunra ransomware** — a fully industrialized RaaS operation built on Conti's leaked source code, now recruiting dark-web affiliates to hit healthcare, utilities, government, manufacturing, and finance simultaneously. Nine sectors. One advisory. The attack vector: exposed VPNs, firewall appliances, and RDP endpoints that organizations have been promising to lock down for years.

Meanwhile, an identity theft protection company lost 900,000 customer records to a phone call. A vishing attack. No zero-day required.

The pattern here isn't bad luck or sophisticated adversaries. The pattern is **asymmetric surface area**: defenders must protect every exposed system, every day; attackers need one that slipped.

There are three questions every CISO and board should be asking right now:

First — what's the actual time between "patch released" and "patch deployed" in your environment for CVSS 9.8 flaws? If the honest answer is more than seven days, you're already behind the exploitation curve on the vCenter story.

Second — which of your edge devices (VPNs, firewalls, remote access platforms) have known CVEs outstanding? The Gunra advisory names CVE-2024-5559 and CVE-2025-24472 specifically. Check your inventory tonight.

Third — is your board asking about AI cyber risk, or still treating it as an IT department issue? The Australian Cyber Security Centre and the AICD published board-level guidance on exactly this question this week. The ACSC doesn't publish board governance frameworks unless there's regulatory expectation behind it.

The arithmetic of this week's threat landscape is simple and unfavorable: more attack surface, faster exploitation, broader ransomware infrastructure, and a patching backlog that grows faster than most teams can clear it.

The response isn't to hire your way out. It's to reduce the number of tracks the race is run on — by ruthlessly pruning internet-exposed attack surface, prioritizing edge device hygiene, and making patch velocity a board-visible metric rather than an IT footnote.

The window isn't closing. It's already closed.
