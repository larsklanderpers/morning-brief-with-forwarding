The attackers didn't break in. They took over the keys your IT team handed to someone else.

That's the story behind this week's N-able N-central breach — and it's the same story underneath the Langflow exploit, the RovoBlast demo, and the Clop hit on FIS Global.

In every case, the attack didn't target your perimeter. It targeted the trusted intermediary sitting inside it.

**The MSP master key**

N-central is the RMM platform MSPs use to manage their clients' endpoints — patches, remote access, monitoring. An authentication bypass (CVE-2026-18556/18577) let attackers walk in as admins, unauthenticated. From there, they used N-central's own Take Control feature to reach every managed device downstream. Then they installed a Cloudflare tunnel so they'd retain access even after the server was patched.

One platform compromise. Every customer exposed. The initial fix was incomplete, leading to a second exploitation wave before a proper patch landed on August 2.

If your organisation uses an MSP, and that MSP uses N-central, you need to ask whether your endpoints have been audited for tunnel services — not just whether the MSP told you they're patched.

**The AI trust problem**

This week's other instructive case is RovoBlast — a demonstrated exploit where a single click on a malicious Jira ticket caused Atlassian's AI assistant, Rovo, to exfiltrate sensitive data. No special permissions required. Rovo's legitimate cross-project access became the attack surface.

Meanwhile, CISA added IBM Langflow to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog — a code injection flaw in the most popular visual builder for AI agent workflows. The platform organisations are using to build agentic AI automation is now a proven attack vector.

This is the pattern. Every AI tool that gets broad context access — Rovo, Copilot, Langflow, your internal LLM integrations — carries the implicit assumption that broad access equals useful. From an attacker's perspective, broad access equals a target.

**What the Clop/FIS hit means for financial services**

FIS Global processes payments for thousands of banks globally. Clop claims 874GB exfiltrated. The concern isn't just FIS — it's every institution in that supply chain that doesn't yet know their data was in the exfil set.

The gap between when a ransomware group publishes a claim and when the breached company discloses formally is where reputational and regulatory risk accumulates fastest. Financial services third-party risk teams should be requesting FIS's incident disclosure today, not waiting for a notification.

**The question that's changed**

For most of the last decade, the security question was: is our perimeter hardened?

That question is still worth asking. But the attack surface that's actually being exploited in 2026 sits behind the perimeter — in the RMM platforms, the CI/CD pipelines, the AI assistants, the payment processors. The trusted intermediaries.

The better question now: which of our trusted tools has administrative access we haven't modelled as a threat surface?

If you can't answer it quickly, that's the gap your adversary is already mapping.
