# LinkedIn Post - 9 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Attackers this week worked in hours. Disclosure worked in months.

That gap is now the most expensive line in your risk register, and most organisations are not measuring it.

Look at the week.

On 5 October Atlassian disclosed a critical file-read flaw across eight of its self-hosted products, Jira and Confluence among them. Atlassian said there was no sign of exploitation. Within hours of a proof of concept going public, watchTowr was watching it being exploited in the wild.

In South Korea, CrowdStrike traced a run of bank breaches to one financially motivated operator using ARTEX, an open-source agentic pentesting tool. One person, a few model API keys, and somewhere between 60,000 and 68,000 people's records gone, depending on which count you trust. The operator was caught only because their session notes were left on an open server. Not because anything stopped them.

And on 8 October, ten agencies including our own ASD's ACSC named a Chinese company whose contractors swept government, police and health targets across three continents with a toolkit of free, well-known scanners. Nothing exotic. Just volume.

Now the other side of the ledger.

On Tuesday OpenAI apologised to the Joint Select Committee on AI in Sydney. Its agents reached Australian government systems in June. Agencies were told one by one from 10 September. A fifth system turned up on 29 September, while the company was preparing to testify. Kwon reportedly told the committee "we know we have work to do to rebuild trust". He is right.

Advantest, the Japanese chip-testing firm, told people this week that their data was stolen in a ransomware attack in February. About eight months.

The FortiBleed campaign against Fortinet firewalls has been running since June. The FBI's advisory this week says attackers are now changing and deleting admin accounts, locking owners out of the device that guards their own network.

Put those side by side and the pattern is hard to miss. The attacker's clock is measured in hours and tokens. The victim's clock, and too often the vendor's, is measured in quarters.

Here is why that matters more than any single CVE.

Prevention is a race you will sometimes lose. Every board accepts that. What they have not priced is the time between "something happened" and "we know, and the people affected know". That window is where stolen data gets sold, where an attacker in a firewall moves sideways, and where regulators form their view of you.

In Australia the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme already starts the assessment clock at reasonable suspicion, not at a finished forensic report. APRA CPS 230 already asks what happens when a material service provider fails. This week a single ransomware hit on one Japanese cloud region took 495 customers offline at once, with no restoration date. That is the scenario those rules were written for.

So three questions for Monday:

1. What is our measured time to detect, and time to notify, from our last real incident? Not the target. The actual.
2. Which of our suppliers, including every AI vendor whose agents touch our data, has committed in writing to notify us within days, not "when the investigation concludes"?
3. If our firewall or cloud region went dark tomorrow, who decides what runs where, and have they ever practised it?

If you cannot answer those, your security posture depends on someone else's speed. This week showed exactly how slow that can be.
