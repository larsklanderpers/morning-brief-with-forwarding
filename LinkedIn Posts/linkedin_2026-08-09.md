Your AI assistant just exfiltrated your Jira data. You didn't click anything. You didn't open an attachment. Someone uploaded a file to your Confluence space — and that was enough.

This week, two security firms independently demonstrated that Atlassian's Rovo AI assistant can be manipulated into collecting Jira and Confluence data from a signed-in user's account and silently sending it to an external server. One attack route used an uploaded file. The other used a malicious link parameter. Only one of those routes is confirmed closed as of Friday.

This isn't a niche research finding. It's a preview of the enterprise security problem of the next three years.

**The trust chain is the attack surface.**

Every AI assistant you deploy inherits the access rights of whoever is using it. Rovo can read whatever your project manager can read — and that includes your M&A documentation, your security assessment findings, and your customer data. Prompt injection turns that access into an exfiltration capability with no additional authentication required.

Ruflo — an AI agent platform — had a critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-59726) this week that allowed unauthenticated attackers to abuse its Model Context Protocol bridge to execute commands, steal API keys, and — this is the part worth sitting with — alter the AI's stored memory. Not just steal data. Poison the knowledge base. Change what the AI "knows" so that it influences future decisions on behalf of users who have no idea anything happened.

That's a new category of persistent compromise that has no analogue in traditional security frameworks.

**Meanwhile, attackers are routing around the controls we spent a decade building.**

UNC6671 — a financially motivated group targeting private equity, financial services, and professional services — is calling employees on their personal mobile phones, posing as IT helpdesk, and directing them to spoofed portals that intercept MFA tokens in real time. The call doesn't touch corporate infrastructure. There's no DLP alert, no SIEM event, no anomalous login from the corporate network — because by the time the login happens, it looks completely normal.

A parallel campaign (linked to Storm-2755, tracked as "Payroll Pirates") is doing the same thing at scale against Microsoft 365 accounts, then patiently maintaining compromised sessions on 8-hour intervals while quietly identifying which accounts belong to people who process wire transfers and payroll runs.

MFA is not the problem here. Conditional access is not the problem here. The problem is that these attacks are designed around the specific gaps in how organisations have implemented those controls — and they're working.

**The exploitation window is now 48 hours.**

CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report, released this week, found that 88% of exploitation of publicly disclosed PoC vulnerabilities in the first half of 2026 occurred within 48 hours of release. Not 30 days. Not 14 days. Two days.

This week's KEV additions include a command injection flaw in Progress Kemp LoadMaster (CVSS 9.6, 792 confirmed exploitation attempts) and a maximum-severity zero-day in Metabase — the BI tool sitting on top of your production databases — that allows unauthenticated SQL injection to full admin access.

Vulnerability management programs built around monthly patch cycles are not vulnerability management programs anymore. They're a false sense of control, documented.

**What this means for decision-makers:**

The question you need to be asking isn't "are our controls technically sound?" It's "have we ever mapped the trust relationships between our systems, our AI tools, and our third-party providers as an attack surface?"

N-able N-central's compromise this week wasn't a single-victim incident — it was a supply chain event, because N-central holds administrative access to every managed endpoint across every MSP customer. One vulnerability, one exploitation, and attackers were inside managed systems before the vendor even issued a patch.

Your AI assistant, your RMM platform, your BI tool, your load balancer — they're all trusted by design. That's precisely what makes them valuable, and precisely what makes them dangerous when that trust is exploited.

The perimeter didn't disappear. It just moved somewhere most security programs aren't looking.
