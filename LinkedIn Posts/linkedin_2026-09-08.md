Your LG TV might be recording this conversation.

Not hypothetically. Not "in some theoretical future state." Right now, as of this week, security researchers have confirmed that 216 million LG smart TVs worldwide can be made to record audio while the screen is off, scan your home network for every connected device, and upload that data to LG's advertising division when they next get internet access. There are remote code execution vulnerabilities in webOS — LG's TV operating system — that let an attacker access those stored recordings remotely.

If you have an LG TV in your home office or your boardroom, that's not a consumer privacy story. That's a physical security problem.

I say this because too many security conversations treat IoT and smart device risks as peripheral. Background noise. The concern of a different department. But 216 million units is not a niche attack surface — it's a surveillance network that was never designed to be secure and is now sitting in the most sensitive rooms in every organisation's orbit.

This lands the same week CISA added seven vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, including a CVSS 10.0 flaw in SonicWall edge appliances and SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox. Attackers hitting those vulnerabilities aren't writing new playbooks — they're deploying reverse shells and crypto miners through doors that should have been patched weeks ago. And researchers discovered a 220-million-record border security database sitting accessible on default credentials in a cloud environment.

Default credentials. On a database containing passport numbers, nationalities, and travel histories going back to 2017.

The pattern across all three stories is the same: the attack didn't require sophisticated tradecraft. It required that no one checked. Patching got delayed. Defaults never got changed. A TV got plugged in and never got scrutinised.

Which is what makes OpenAI's $1 billion Daybreak commitment this week genuinely significant — not for the AI angle, but for what it reveals about the defensive landscape. OpenAI is subsidising frontier AI access for water utilities, local governments, community banks, and open-source maintainers. The reason they're doing it is that those organisations — the ones holding the infrastructure that everything else depends on — can't afford to defend themselves. The asymmetry between the sophistication of the threats they're facing and the resources available to counter them is now large enough that a major AI lab has concluded it needs to actively close the gap.

That asymmetry is the policy problem that no single vendor can solve. It's also the most important security story of 2026.

Organisations that have patched their SonicWall devices, changed default credentials, and asked hard questions about what their managed service providers are running will have a better week than those that haven't. That's the entire brief.

The bar is not high. We just keep missing it.
