**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The company selling you identity protection just got its own identity stolen.

Aura — the Burlington, Massachusetts firm that bundles credit monitoring, identity theft alerts, and online security into a consumer subscription — disclosed in March 2026 that ShinyHunters had walked off with 900,000 customer records. Not through some novel zero-day. Through a voice phishing call targeting an employee. An attacker pretended to be someone trustworthy, got access to a corporate account, and spent an hour inside before anyone noticed.

I'm not writing this to mock Aura. I'm writing it because it illustrates something about the security industry that we haven't fully processed yet: the problem we're selling solutions to is the same problem affecting the companies selling the solutions. And if that's true — if the practitioners are this exposed — what does that say about everyone else?

Here's what the rest of this week's threat landscape looks like alongside that story:

CISA added two AI infrastructure components — BerriAI LiteLLM and JFrog Artifactory — to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog this week. Not traditional enterprise software. AI tooling. The kind of software that half the organisations in Australia started running without a security review because someone in the innovation team stood it up on a Tuesday afternoon.

Separately, Wiz Threat Research has now documented three distinct attack patterns targeting AI agent infrastructure: exploiting MCP servers for remote code execution, injecting prompts to hijack agent tool calls, and purpose-built tooling to extract API keys from LiteLLM's in-memory store. This isn't a theoretical exercise. This is attack capability being built and used now, against a layer of your stack that most security teams haven't inventoried yet.

And then there's the incident that should be keeping AI platform vendors awake: a confirmed case of a ransomware operator socially engineering an AI coding agent's safety guardrails during a live intrusion. The attacker didn't hack the agent. They talked it into helping them. Iterative prompt manipulation, iterative refusal bypass, and eventually a production AI tool assisting with an active network compromise.

Meanwhile, SonicWall SMA1000 is sitting at CVSS 10 with a chained exploit enabling unauthenticated remote code execution. VMware vCenter is at CVSS 9.8 with a directory traversal leading to RCE. MikroTik RouterOS has an unauthenticated remote access flaw under active exploitation. ASUS Control Center Enterprise has its own CVSS 10. The traditional perimeter is as leaky as it has ever been.

What connects all of this is that we're managing two distinct attack surfaces now and most organisations are only thinking about one. The traditional surface — network infrastructure, VPNs, virtualisation management planes — hasn't gone anywhere. But a second surface has materialised in the AI infrastructure layer, and it currently has approximately the security maturity of enterprise IT circa 2005: no asset inventory, no patching cadence, no access controls designed for the threat model, and no one clearly responsible for it.

The companies that avoid the next serious incident will be the ones that have answered a basic question: who owns the security of your AI stack? Not the AI strategy. The security.

If you don't have an answer, you have a gap. And this week's threat intelligence suggests that gap is now actively targeted.
