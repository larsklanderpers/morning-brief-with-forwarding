**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Three reports dropped in the last 48 hours that, read together, tell you something important about where we are.

CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report found that 88% of vulnerabilities with a public proof-of-concept exploit were weaponised within 48 hours. Not within a week. Not within a patch cycle. Forty-eight hours — and in the fastest case they observed, initial foothold to lateral movement took 27 seconds.

IBM's breach cost report, released the same week, puts the average AI-enabled breach at $6.04 million. One in four malicious breaches this year involved AI on the offensive side. Shadow AI incidents — tools your staff adopted that your security team never inventoried — now account for 43% of AI-related breaches.

Then, yesterday, the NSA, CISA and FBI issued a joint advisory naming six Chinese AI companies that have spent two years systematically extracting capabilities from Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok at industrial scale, using fraudulent accounts and proxy routing to avoid detection. The agencies said this is not commercial free-riding. It is military and cyberattack capability development.

These three stories are not separate.

The 48-hour exploitation window exists because AI code-generation tools can read a published proof-of-concept and produce working exploit code in minutes. The $6.04 million AI breach premium exists because AI-enabled attackers operate faster and at greater scale than human-only crews. And the distillation campaign exists because the same frontier models being used to build enterprise products are being systematically mined to close the capability gap between state and non-state adversaries.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that AI has not created a new category of risk — it has compressed the timeline of every existing risk category simultaneously.

What does that mean operationally?

Vulnerability management programmes built on weekly scan cadences are now structurally misaligned with the threat. If 88% of weaponisation happens within 48 hours of PoC publication, a Monday-morning vulnerability review is not a risk management programme. It is a retrospective.

Shadow AI is not a productivity governance problem. IBM's data shows it is now almost half of AI-related breach incidents, averaging $5.39 million each. You cannot manage risk in tools you have not inventoried.

And AI API access controls — the rate limits, anomalous-use detection, and terms-of-service enforcement that AI providers run — are now the functional equivalent of export controls. The NSA advisory makes that explicit. If your organisation is routing significant AI workloads through third-party gateways, that infrastructure has a security profile that most IT teams have not assessed. (The separate finding this morning that 9.6% of exposed LiteLLM gateways still accepted the default "sk-1234" admin key is a useful data point on how that assessment tends to go.)

None of this is abstract. The September Patch Tuesday this week includes a DNS flaw that security researchers are calling a SigRed successor — wormable, unauthenticated, remote code execution across most Windows versions. There is also a Kerberos authentication bypass where one phished workstation account is sufficient to reach domain controller compromise.

The question boards should be asking is not "are we protected against AI-enabled attacks?" The question is: "Does our patch cadence, our AI tool inventory, and our API access governance reflect a world where the exploitation window is 48 hours and the breakout time is 29 minutes?"

For most organisations, the honest answer is no. That is the gap worth closing before Q4.
