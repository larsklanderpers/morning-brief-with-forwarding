On Tuesday, a Japanese cloud provider told its customers to back up their data, disconnect from the internet and pull the power cord out of the wall. Before noon.

That was GMO, whose Onamae Desktop Cloud was breached on 20 October. The attacker published stolen data, demanded about $50 million, and threatened to fire ransomware already planted on customer servers. GMO refused to pay. Its fallback was asking up to 26,739 customers to unplug. Compensation: about $30 each.

I keep coming back to that instruction, because it shows plainly who carries the risk when you buy a managed service.

It was not the only example this week.

In the UK, the police inspectorate reported that suppliers holding police data have been breached repeatedly. Most supplier contracts carried no ongoing security assurance. Police teams could not routinely see their own suppliers' logs.

In Brazil, a card skimmer reached dozens of fashion retailers' checkouts through a shared analytics script on the platform they all used. None of those brands wrote the code that stole from their customers. It arrived as a marketing tag.

And Warp, the agentic development environment, disclosed that AI agent decision logs and telemetry were exposed through an unnamed third-party vendor. Think about what an agent's decision log is: a record of everything it read and did inside your codebase. Most organisations have a classification policy for source code. Almost none have one for agent traces.

Four stories, one shape. The data lives with a supplier. The visibility does not.

Now add the clock.

On Monday, CISA confirmed the PHP2Shell flaws were being exploited. On Tuesday, it confirmed the same for the Brotli compression flaw that sits inside Node, .NET and countless HTTP stacks. Both went from disclosure to confirmed attacks inside a week. Australia's ASD, in its annual threat report this week, put it bluntly: defenders who once had months to patch now have days, sometimes hours.

Put those two trends together and you get the uncomfortable conclusion. Your exposure window is shrinking to days, but your view into your suppliers is still governed by a contract signed years ago and a questionnaire filled in once a year.

That gap is where the next breach in your organisation is most likely to come from. Not your firewall. Not your people. The vendor you cannot see into.

So here is what I would ask any board or executive team this week:

1. For every supplier that holds customer data, can our security team see their logs, or at least get them within hours?
2. Can we list every third-party script that runs on our payment and login pages, and would we know if one changed overnight?
3. Do we treat AI agent logs as sensitive data, and do we know where they go?
4. If a provider told us to unplug before noon, could we?

If the honest answer to any of these is "the contract covers it", it does not. A contract tells you who pays after the fact. It does not tell you what is happening now.

Buying a service means renting someone else's controls. You still own the outcome.
