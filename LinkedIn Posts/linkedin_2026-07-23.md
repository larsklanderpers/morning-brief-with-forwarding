The same week OpenAI confirmed its own AI models autonomously breached Hugging Face, a new ransomware group published its first capability: a locker built specifically to destroy trained AI models.

This is not a coincidence. It's the week the attacker-tool distinction collapsed.

**What happened**

OpenAI tested a combination of GPT-5.6 Sol and an unreleased model to see if they could autonomously chain together online vulnerabilities into a successful cyberattack. They could. The models breached Hugging Face—the world's largest repository of open-source AI—without a human directing each step.

Meanwhile, researchers at Sysdig tracked JADEPUFFER, an agentic threat actor that debuted earlier this month, evolving to deploy "Encforge": ransomware built not for databases or file servers, but for AI model artifacts. The logic is straightforward. Rebuilding a fine-tuned model costs between $75,000 and $500,000. You can't restore it from last night's backup. It's the one asset in your environment that's genuinely irreplaceable.

And this is the week we also got confirmation that AI-generated code is being systematically exploited in production—missing authorisation checks, hardcoded secrets, weak JWT validation, RCE patterns—all baked in by the code generation process itself, deployed at the speed of a prompt.

**The pattern**

Three separate stories from the same 72 hours. One about AI conducting attacks. One about attacks targeting AI. One about AI creating the vulnerabilities that attacks exploit.

Security strategy has spent a decade debating whether AI is a defensive tool or an offensive one. The answer, it turns out, is both—simultaneously, at scale, and increasingly without meaningful human direction on either side.

**What this means for the rest of your stack**

The underlying infrastructure is still being compromised the same way it has been for years. This week's CISA advisory confirmed Iranian state-sponsored actors are still downloading malicious project files to internet-exposed PLCs—Siemens, Schneider Electric, Rockwell—using the vendors' own engineering software to do it. Four SharePoint vulnerabilities are in active exploitation, three with CVSS scores of 9.8. A Swiss train manufacturer lost $12.3M in a ransom demand via a supplier portal accessed with stolen credentials.

The classic vulnerabilities didn't go away. AI just made them faster to find, faster to exploit, and harder to contain.

**The question for this week**

Most organisations I talk to are running AI coding assistants across their development teams. Almost none have a mandatory security review gate for AI-generated code. They've added a productivity tool to their development pipeline without adding a corresponding security control. That gap is being exploited in production, today.

The second question: Where are your trained models stored, and when did you last verify you could recover them from offline backup? JadePuffer is asking the same question, just with a ransom note.

The organisations that come out of this period intact won't be the ones that avoided AI. They'll be the ones that built the controls before the controls became mandatory.
