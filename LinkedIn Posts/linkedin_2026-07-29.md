The attacker went to sleep. The AI kept working.

That's the detail that should keep every CISO up at night from a breach disclosed this week. A threat actor ran an open-source AI agent — Hermes, in "YOLO mode" with approval prompts disabled — against Thailand's Ministry of Finance and then simply walked away. The agent autonomously handled post-exploitation. The human only needed to show up for the initial breach. The rest was automated.

We've been talking about AI-enabled attackers for two years. This week's news makes clear we've crossed from "enabled" to "autonomous." And the implications are significant.

**The speed gap is widening.**

The ACSC issued a CRITICAL alert this week on a large-scale campaign exploiting 17+ vulnerabilities across WordPress, Joomla, and Craft CMS installations globally — hitting small and medium Australian businesses at industrial scale. The advisory explicitly cites AI as the reason exploitation is now happening faster than organisations can patch. Five Eyes intelligence chiefs said the same thing in a joint statement: AI is collapsing the window between vulnerability disclosure and active exploitation.

For organisations patching quarterly, that window is already closed before the patch cycle opens.

**Meanwhile, the breach queue keeps moving.**

Origin Energy confirmed 900,000 Australian customers had their data exfiltrated — names, addresses, dates of birth, partial payment details — in a breach detected on 22 July. AnMed hospital in the US is diverting emergency patients and cancelling surgeries after a ransomware attack gave them a 72-hour extortion deadline. Coca-Cola confirmed data was stolen in the Fairlife ransomware incident. Ransomware claims in the first half of 2026 are up 25% year-on-year — 25 attacks per day.

These aren't random. They're the predictable output of an industrialised attack economy that found AI before most defenders did.

**The decision gap is the real problem.**

Operation BlueDash, a phishing campaign active right now, deploys two independent remote access tools simultaneously — Level RMM and ScreenConnect — via a fake Microsoft Teams update. The reason for the redundancy is instructive: attackers know that defenders will detect and block one channel. The second channel is there for when that happens. They've already war-gamed the incident response playbook and built around it.

Defenders, by contrast, are still routing response decisions through change management committees that weren't designed for 72-hour extortion clocks or AI agents that don't sleep.

**What changes now:**

The organisations that will weather this period are the ones that restructure security programs around a single uncomfortable assumption: the attacker is not waiting for business hours. AI-autonomous attack tooling means breach dwell time begins counting the moment initial access is achieved — not when a human operator logs back in.

That means patch cycles need to compress. Exception processes need to shrink. Incident response needs a pre-approved decision tree for the scenarios that are actually happening — not the ones from five years ago.

The Hermes attacker left their logs exposed on an open server. Researchers found them and published the details. The next operator will not make that mistake. We should be grateful for the look while it lasts.
