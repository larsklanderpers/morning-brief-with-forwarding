**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Alpine Linux updated GitLab this month and stayed vulnerable anyway.

Nobody there was careless. The bug did not live in GitLab. It lived in Ruby's regular expression engine, and Alpine runs its own image on standard Ruby. So the version number moved, the ticket closed, and the hole stayed open. An attacker downloaded the same container image, apparently to tune an exploit to that exact build, and came at the server on 20 and 21 October. Alpine says the attack failed. It also says it cannot rule out code execution, so it rebuilt from scratch and said so in public.

That is a good incident response. It is also the most useful security story of the week, because it describes something that happened three more times in the same few days.

Ivanti customers who moved quickly on the Connect Secure flaw CISA flagged on 13 October did the right thing. On Friday CISA added two more flaws to its exploited list. Ivanti says attackers have been chaining all three, and the fix for the new pair sits one release past the one those customers installed. One of the two carries a CVSS score of 4.3. Medium. Nobody prioritises a medium. It is a link in a chain that hands over a VPN appliance.

The Linux kernel has a race condition, CVE-2026-98432, reaching back to version 4.4. The kernel team scores it 9.8. Red Hat scores it 5.9 and calls it a potential denial of service. Then Anthropic published a working remote root exploit written by an unreleased model. It took about 53 minutes, largely because a lost race simply fails and lets the attacker try again. Anthropic sells that model family, so read it with that in mind, but the exploit does not need your belief. Meanwhile the obvious mitigation of switching off Multipath TCP does not cover it, per CIQ, because the bug can also be triggered through ordinary TCP. And since Go 1.24 turns Multipath TCP on by default on Linux, some of your services may be running a feature nobody chose.

WordPress shipped 7.1.4 on 28 October. Defiant, which sells Wordfence, saw its first matching attack nine minutes after deploying its firewall rule, and more than 18,500 requests in 21 hours. No CVE number yet, so any programme that keys off CVE feeds cannot see it.

Four stories, and one pattern. In each, a competent team had a good reason to believe it was finished.

We built vulnerability management around a simple unit of work: one flaw, one score, one patch, one closed ticket. That unit made sense when exploits took weeks and were written by a small number of specialists. It does not survive contact with chains, with dependencies you compiled yourself, or with a model that will retry a race for 48 minutes without getting bored.

So here is what I would change on Monday.

Stop reporting "patched" as a status. Report what was checked: which build, which underlying runtime, which link in which chain. If you package your own images, you own the dependency tree, and no vendor notice will come for you.

Treat every exploited edge-device bug as a compromise question as well as a patch question. Fixing the door does nothing about whoever walked in before you fixed it.

And use severity scores to sort the queue, not to decide what is safe to ignore. The score describes one flaw on its own. Attackers do not buy flaws on their own.

The question for your next risk meeting is not how fast you patch. It is how you know you finished.
