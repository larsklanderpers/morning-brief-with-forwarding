The attacker's cost curve just collapsed. And most boards haven't noticed.

At Black Hat last week, OpenAI's Michael Dalton said it plainly: AI-orchestrated, fully automated offensive attacks are real now. Not theoretical. Not coming soon. Happening.

Let me put that in context with what landed in my brief this morning.

Microsoft just patched 421 CVEs in a single release — 62 of them critical. One is a kernel-level Windows vulnerability (CVE-2026-68820) that's already being actively exploited. Last June, there were 371 critical CVEs discovered across major tech firms. In June 2025 there were 25. That's a 15x increase in one year.

This isn't a patch cycle problem. It's a discovery velocity problem — and AI is in the discovery loop on both sides.

Here's the structural shift that matters: the UAT-10147 campaign, reported this month, demonstrated AI automating target selection, vulnerability identification, and payload customisation at scale — hitting multiple sectors across geographies, each with platform-specific implants. What used to require a skilled team of humans to orchestrate over weeks now runs autonomously.

Simultaneously, CISA and the FBI have issued a joint advisory on Gunra ransomware — a double-extortion operation running as a service, open to affiliates with no technical expertise, derived from leaked Conti code. The RaaS model means the sophistication of the attack decouples from the sophistication of the attacker. You're not defending against one group. You're defending against a marketplace.

And while all this is happening, researchers just found 28,000 publicly exposed .git repositories leaking credentials, internal source code, and employee data — the result of routine deployment mistakes, not sophisticated attacks.

The threat environment is not harder because attackers got smarter. It's harder because the labour cost of attacking dropped to near-zero.

The question I'd put to every executive and board member reading this: your security programme was designed around human attacker pacing. Detection windows, response timelines, analyst review cycles — all calibrated to human-speed adversaries. Is it still fit for purpose when attacks are machine-speed?

Because the controls don't change until the threat model does.

Three things that should be on your agenda before the end of the week:

1. Confirm patch status on CVE-2026-68820 (Windows) and CVE-2026-73570 (Zimbra) — both are in active exploitation and federally mandated.
2. Ask your CISO where N-able N-central or similar MSP management platforms sit in your supply chain, and whether the August patches are applied.
3. If you're running SharePoint on-premises with internet exposure, find out today. Unauthenticated remote code execution doesn't wait for your next review cycle.

The cost of defending is still linear. The cost of attacking isn't anymore.
