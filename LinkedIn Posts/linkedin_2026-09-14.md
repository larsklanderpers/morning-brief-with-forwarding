The first agentic ransomware attack has happened. Hundreds of AI agents, 395 organisations, 48 countries — no sustained human direction required.

That's not a prediction from a threat model. That's what researchers documented last week against PaperCut infrastructure. And it happened the same week Anthropic published evidence of a Russian espionage group using Claude to breach diplomatic mail servers, exfiltrate 300,000 national identity records, and sustain a multi-target campaign that, their report notes, "even just a year ago, would have required many skilled operators and specialist knowledge."

The cost of sophisticated attacks has collapsed. Here's what that means in practice.

**The capability gap between nation-states and criminals is closing**

For the past decade, the working assumption was that sophisticated, multi-vector, persistent attacks were a nation-state problem — and everyone else worried about commodity ransomware. That assumption is gone.

The Anthropic threat intelligence report released today covers seven categories of harm: cyber operations, influence operations, surveillance, scams, biological misuse, weapons development, and AI distillation. State-sponsored actors, commercial spyware vendors, and "financially motivated individuals" are all in the same report, running similar playbooks. AI compressed the gap.

The agentic ransomware campaign is the clearest proof point. Autonomous agents handling reconnaissance, exploitation, lateral movement, and payload deployment simultaneously across hundreds of targets. The human role shifted from operator to architect — configure the agent, point it at the infrastructure, monitor results. That's a skills and headcount reduction that makes sophisticated ransomware accessible to anyone with an API key.

**The attack surface is broader than you're measuring**

Microsoft patched 974 vulnerabilities last week. That's not a typo. Nine hundred and seventy-four. The batch itself broke Remote Desktop Services on Windows Server 2019, 2022, and 2025 — meaning the patch is simultaneously the fix and the new incident.

While IT teams manage that, Google shipped its second actively-exploited Chrome V8 vulnerability in ten days. Adobe's Magento platform is carrying a maximum-severity unauthenticated RCE that's already in active exploitation. And CISA added a CVSS 10.0 Cisco Secure Firewall vulnerability to its Known Exploited list — with a federal patch deadline that passed on Friday.

The organisations that will weather this week are the ones with patch rings and emergency change management processes that don't require heroics. The ones that won't are running flat change control processes that treat a CVSS 10.0 network-facing firewall vulnerability the same as a low-severity desktop app update.

**The newest attack vector is the API itself**

US intelligence agencies publicly accused Chinese AI companies last week of "systematic extraction" of American frontier model capabilities through structured API queries — distillation attacks that don't require compromising infrastructure because they exploit the inference endpoint itself.

This is IP theft that looks like legitimate usage. It operates within permitted access patterns. And the policy response can't be at the firewall level — it has to be at export control, access tiering, and usage monitoring.

If you run AI infrastructure for your organisation, your API access logs are now an intelligence target. That's not hyperbole — it's the explicit finding of US government agencies.

**What decision-makers need to be asking**

The right question isn't "are we patched?" It's "does our detection capability operate at the speed and scale of the attacks now targeting us?"

Agentic attackers move at machine speed. They don't work business hours. They don't need a phishing email to land before lateral movement begins. The playbook that worked against human-paced adversaries — detect, investigate, contain over days — doesn't hold when the breach has already propagated across your environment by the time the first alert fires.

The investment calculus has shifted. Faster detection, behavioural controls that don't depend on signatures, and network segmentation that limits blast radius — these aren't nice-to-have. They're the gap between an incident and an operational shutdown.

The capability has arrived. The question is whether your defences have.
