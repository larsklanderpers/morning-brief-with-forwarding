**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Your AI vendor just lost access to a tool your team uses every day. Not because they failed. Because someone bought the company that makes the tool.

That's what happened with OpenAI and Cursor this week. Models are available. The tool is available. The combination your engineers built their workflow around? Gone by November.

This is the new shape of AI risk — and regulated industries are the last to be pricing it in.

**The lock-in nobody talks about**

We obsess over which model scores highest on benchmarks. We debate cloud vs. on-premise. But the real lock-in in 2026 isn't the subscription — it's accumulated working context. Six months of project history, corrections, uploaded files, and business logic that lives in exactly one provider's system. When the deal changes, you don't lose access to a chatbot. You lose the institutional memory your team built around it.

For financial services, health, and government — sectors where that context includes customer PII, client instructions, and regulated decisions — the stakes are different. A vendor relationship change isn't just inconvenient. It's a data governance event.

**Two regulatory clocks ticking simultaneously**

In Australia, automated-decision-making disclosure obligations under the Privacy Act arrive December 10. You'll need to tell customers, in general terms, when AI made a decision about them and how. If you can't explain your system to the OAIC, you won't be able to comply.

In Europe, the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions have been enforceable since August 2. GDPR and AI Act obligations compound — they don't cancel each other out. Penalties reach €35M or 7% of global turnover.

The gap that matters: data residency ≠ data sovereignty. Many providers claim "Australian hosting" but route inference through Singapore or US servers under peak load. Under APRA's CPS 230, material service providers — including AI vendors — must meet equivalent security standards across every layer. Storage guarantees are insufficient if model inference runs offshore.

**The sovereignty math is changing**

This week, Multiverse Computing shipped Europe's most capable AI model at $0.60 per million tokens input. Anthropic is in active negotiations to build a US$21 billion facility in Australia, with sovereign infrastructure as the explicit deliverable. Australia's Defence Minister called this "the biggest sovereignty inflection point since the Second World War."

On-premise deployment economics have dropped 40–60% in 12 months. An entry-level private LLM server reaches cost parity with cloud APIs in three to six months at realistic enterprise workloads.

The question for regulated-industry leaders is no longer whether self-hosted AI is viable. It's why you're still routing sensitive data offshore when the alternative is cheaper than it's ever been and regulators are looking harder than they ever have.

**Three questions for your AI governance review this quarter:**

1. If your primary AI vendor changed its terms tomorrow, could your team continue operations — or would you lose the accumulated context your processes depend on?

2. For every AI-assisted decision that touches customer PII, can you describe to a regulator what data was used, where it was processed, and how the decision was made?

3. Is your "Australian data residency" claim actually Australian inference, or just Australian storage with offshore compute?

The organisations building the answers to those questions now will have a regulatory advantage in 2027. The ones answering them under enforcement pressure won't.

The capability gap between cloud and on-premise AI is closing. The accountability gap between "we use AI" and "we can govern our AI" is not.
