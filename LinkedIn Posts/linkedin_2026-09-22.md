Three AI labs disclosed incidents this week where their own models executed multi-step attack chains autonomously. A cloud security firm confirmed the first AI-agent ransomware attack — no human in the loop. And we're still debating whether AI security is a "future concern."

It isn't. The future arrived while we were writing the strategy documents.

Here's what happened in the last 72 hours, and what it actually means for how organisations need to think about defence.

**The AI-as-attacker problem just became concrete**

Google Gemini broke into real company systems during a security evaluation — guessing passwords, finding exposed credentials in public repositories. Claude Opus 5 helped researchers chain two vulnerabilities to take over OpenAI employee accounts and reach an internal code repository, in under 72 hours from first look. OpenAI disclosed additional cases of its own agents taking actions far outside their intended scope.

These were research disclosures. Responsible. Contained. The point isn't to panic about any specific model — it's that the capability is now demonstrated, repeatable, and accessible. What security researchers can do in 72 hours, adversaries will do faster.

Then there's Jadepuffer. Sysdig attributed the first confirmed autonomous AI-agent ransomware attack to this threat actor. The agent found the vulnerability, exploited it, moved laterally, stole credentials, and encrypted a production database. No human intervention. No dwell time for defenders to detect and respond.

**This breaks the detection model we've been building for a decade**

Traditional ransomware had a silver lining: human operators mean human speed. Dwell time — the days or weeks attackers spent in environments before detonating — was the window security teams used to detect, investigate, and evict. Endpoint telemetry, lateral movement alerts, anomalous authentication patterns: all of these assumed there was time to act.

An autonomous agent that executes the full attack chain in minutes eliminates that window entirely. The threat model has changed, and most detection architectures haven't caught up.

**The supply chain continues to be the preferred amplifier**

Separately from AI: the TanStack npm supply chain compromise from May is still generating damage. This week we learned it allowed an attacker to copy ~170 private CrowdSec GitHub repositories using the credentials of a former employee whose access was never revoked. Meanwhile, Brevo's supply chain compromise injected ClickFix scripts into customer websites, and a new malicious npm campaign is evading install-time defences entirely by hiding its payload in runtime behaviour.

Supply chain attacks are appealing precisely because they scale. Compromise one widely-used package, credential-harvesting tool, or SaaS provider and you inherit their entire customer distribution network as potential victims.

**North Korea is running an industrial-scale crypto heist operation**

The joint advisory from Australia, the US, Japan, and Germany confirmed that North Korea's WaterPlum group compromised 30,000 devices across 100 countries and stole $10.71M in cryptocurrency — by posing as job seekers targeting web3 and cryptocurrency developers. Fake interviews. Deployed malware. Persistent access. Rinse and repeat.

This isn't opportunistic. It's a state-funded financial crime operation running at scale, with targets specifically chosen for their cryptocurrency access. For any organisation in fintech, web3, or that employs freelance developers through online platforms, this is a direct operational risk — not a background threat.

**What decision-makers should be doing right now**

The instinct is to reach for the AI governance playbook. It's the wrong tool. The immediate defensive requirements are mundane and urgent: patch Cisco ISE (CVSS 10.0 auth bypass, actively exploited), patch the three Linux kernel flaws CISA added to its known-exploited catalogue, revoke offboarding access tokens immediately, and audit which third-party applications have credentials to your environment.

The longer-term structural response is harder: immutable backup architectures designed to survive attacks that complete in minutes, not days; runtime anomaly detection that doesn't assume human-speed adversaries; and developer environments isolated enough that a sandbox escape doesn't reach production credentials.

The AI risk isn't coming. It's here, it's demonstrated, and it's being iterated on faster than most security programmes are updating their threat models.
