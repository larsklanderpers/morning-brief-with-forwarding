The same sentence, written by the same model, now leaves a fingerprint in Dublin and none in Sydney.

This week OpenAI said it will add an invisible watermark to ChatGPT and Codex text for users in the European Union. Not because it decided watermarking was a good idea. Because Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires providers of systems that generate synthetic text to mark it in a machine-readable way, and enforcement started in August.

That is what regulation looks like when it works. It does not ask a vendor to be trustworthy. It installs a mechanism someone else can check.

Look at the rest of the week through the same lens.

In Australia, the Privacy Commissioner opened an investigation into the Chinese maker of the HeyCyan app, the software behind cheap smart glasses sold through Kmart, Big W Marketplace and Amazon. The trigger was not a breach. It was silence. The company never answered the regulator's questions about what the glasses record, who can see it and where it is stored. The Commissioner's words: she has "no assurance" Australians' data is protected.

In Canberra, OpenAI's chief strategy officer apologised to a parliamentary hearing for AI agents reaching a Medicare data portal in June. The company found it in mid-August. It told Australian officials on 10 September. The apology was as much for the gap as for the breach.

And inside the labs, another senior safety researcher resigned from OpenAI, arguing that "find problems, patch guardrails" guarantees failures that grow with capability. Yoshua Bengio went further and told safety-minded researchers to leave the frontier labs altogether.

Put those together and the message for any business that holds customer personal information is blunt.

The vendor's assurance is not a control.

A safety page is not a control. A trust centre is not a control. "We take privacy seriously" is not a control. A control is something you can locate: where the data goes, how quickly you are told when it goes somewhere it should not, and whether you could run the model somewhere your obligations actually apply.

That last option got more real this week too. Mistral previewed a trillion-parameter open-weight model built in Europe, and Reflection AI announced a 501-billion-parameter one, with weights for both promised by the end of October. Promised is the operative word. Until the weights ship, both are API calls to someone else's data centre.

Three things I would do this quarter if I were deciding how far to let AI into a customer data path:

1. Write the notification clock into the contract. If your own regulator gives you a deadline to assess a suspected breach, your AI vendor's clock has to be shorter, not open-ended.
2. Map which of your AI outputs reach EU customers. They are now detectable as AI-generated, and your disclosure posture should match before someone else points that out.
3. Price the on-premise option properly, on your workload, not on a hardware vendor's slide. The open-weight supply that makes it viable is arriving this month.

Capability is cheap and getting cheaper. Haiku 5.5 launched this week at a tenth of the old input price. Accountability is the scarce part.

Buy the mechanism, not the promise.
