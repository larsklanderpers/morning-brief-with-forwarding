The 48-hour rule has broken the security industry's foundational assumption.

CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report landed this week with a number that deserves more attention than it's getting: 88% of vulnerability exploitations with a working proof-of-concept occurred within 48 hours of public disclosure in the first half of this year.

That's not a trend. That's a structural collapse of the buffer that enterprise security was built on.

**The old model had a sequence:** researcher discovers flaw → CVE published → patch released → IT schedules maintenance window → patch deployed. Somewhere in that sequence — usually between step 3 and step 5 — the attacker used to run out of runway. Not anymore.

This week illustrates the problem across three separate incidents.

North Korea's Lazarus Group exploited a Windows kernel zero-day (a use-after-free in the WinSock driver) before Microsoft shipped its August Patch Tuesday fix. Check Point traced it to Operation Dream Job — the same campaign that's been quietly turning job-hunting engineers into inadvertent breach vectors. The patch is now available. The exploits were running first.

Meanwhile, Cl0p linked a suspected Oracle E-Business Suite zero-day to a simultaneous data theft operation across approximately 50 companies — Shell, Philips, GE, Fiserv, and dozens more. Eighty-nine gigabytes out of Shell alone. The companies being added to Cl0p's dark-web leak site hadn't finished their incident investigations before the ransom clock started.

And in a detail that should alarm every engineering leader: the Trivy supply chain attack compromised over 2,500 organisations through a container security scanner. Not through a gap in security tooling — through the security tooling itself.

**Here's the conclusion I keep coming back to:** patching is still necessary but it is no longer sufficient as a primary defence against initial access. The time between vulnerability disclosure and working exploit has compressed to the point where compensating controls — network segmentation, behavioural detection, data-layer monitoring, least-privilege access — have to carry the weight that patching cycles used to carry.

A SecurityWeek expert column this week asked bluntly: "Is Patching Dead?" The answer isn't yes, but the question itself is the right one to be asking in your next security leadership conversation. The teams still treating patching as their primary risk management lever are operating on a model that was accurate in 2021.

There's also a second thread worth following this week: AI-assisted attacks have officially moved from proof-of-concept to operational. The same CrowdStrike report flags AI as now embedded across adversary workflows — not as a novelty, not as an experiment, but as a force multiplier that lets smaller groups punch at the scale that nation-states used to require. The practical consequence is that your detection signatures, your SIEM rules, and your SOC playbooks were trained on human-paced attack patterns. AI-generated attacks create different telemetry at different speeds.

The organisations that are ahead right now are the ones that made that realisation in the last 12 months and started rebuilding their detection logic accordingly. The ones who haven't are the ones who will be explaining a breach to their board in language that still sounds like 2021.

The 48-hour rule isn't a technical detail. It's a strategic inflection point. Treat it like one.
