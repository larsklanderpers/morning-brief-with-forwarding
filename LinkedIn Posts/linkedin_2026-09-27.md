"Mitigated" is the most dangerous word in a risk register.

It usually means someone put a control in front of a problem, wrote down that the control handles it, and moved on. The control then becomes the thing nobody tests, because the risk is closed.

This weekend has been a masterclass in what that word hides.

Start with Oracle PeopleSoft. The flaw ShinyHunters is using, CVE-2026-35273, was patched in June. Plenty of organisations did not patch; they leaned on a web application firewall rule instead. Google's Mandiant has now shown how the attackers get past it: they swap the letter P in the vulnerable path for its percent-encoded form. The WAF sees a string it does not recognise. The server decodes it and routes the request exactly where the attacker wanted. One character. Dozens of victims across healthcare, higher education and government.

A WAF rule is a sign on the door. It is not a lock.

Then SharePoint. Microsoft shipped the fix for CVE-2026-65660 in August, but labelled it a medium-severity spoofing issue. Anyone triaging by severity put it behind the criticals. A month later it is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, with web shell attempts reportedly observed from 25 September. The control that failed here was not technical. It was a label.

Then GitHub. Two Actions compromised in May were disabled and everyone moved on. On 16 September they quietly came back online, still pointing at the malicious code, and for nine days any pipeline that referenced them by version tag ran a credential stealer again. An incident closed in May reopened itself in September without anyone touching their own code. A tag is a promise from someone else. A commit hash is a fact.

And then the one that should worry every board: two unpatched remote code execution flaws in Citrix NetScaler, exploited in the wild according to watchTowr, with no CVE, no advisory and no patch expected before next week. The gateway that decides who gets into the network is the thing being broken into. Some administrators have simply pulled their appliances offline.

That is where this gets uncomfortable, because pulling a VPN gateway on a Saturday is not a security decision. It is a business decision about revenue, staff access and customer service. Most organisations have never agreed in advance who is allowed to make it.

The one vendor that looked good this week did exactly that. Kiteworks asked its customers to shut their file-transfer servers down for a weekend window, not because of a breach, but because federal intelligence said an attack might be coming. Switching your own product off on a warning rather than on evidence takes nerve. It is also the right call for a category that extortion gangs have already turned into a mass data-theft channel.

So here is the Monday question for every executive team:

Which of your "mitigated" risks rest on a compensating control nobody has tested since the day it was accepted?

And who in your organisation is authorised to take a critical system offline, on a weekend, before the vendor tells you to?

If you cannot name that person, you do not have an incident response plan. You have a hope.
