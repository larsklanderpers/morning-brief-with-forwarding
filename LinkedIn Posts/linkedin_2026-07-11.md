This week, a ransomware operation ran its entire intrusion — reconnaissance, credential theft, encryption — with almost no human at the keyboard.

The malware, dubbed JadePuffer, exploited a flaw in Langflow, an AI workflow platform, and then let automation do the rest. One operator. End-to-end compromise. That used to take a crew.

Three other things happened this week that belong in the same picture.

First, the Five Eyes intelligence alliance issued a joint warning that AI's impact on offensive cyber operations is arriving "in months, not years." When five national intelligence agencies coordinate on a timeline statement, that's not commentary — it's a signal that regulatory obligations will follow.

Second, researchers documented attackers exploiting legitimate, sanctioned AI tools inside more than 90 organizations — using the enterprise's own AI to generate malicious commands and move data out. Your insider threat now includes your software.

Third, a former ransomware negotiator was sentenced to nearly six years in prison for secretly working with the BlackCat crew — extorting the very victims who hired him. Even the breach-response supply chain has a trust problem.

Here's the pattern I'd want every board and executive team to see: attack capacity is decoupling from attacker headcount.

For thirty years, our defensive assumptions have quietly depended on a human bottleneck. Intrusions took time because people take time. Detection windows, patch cycles, incident response SLAs — all calibrated to human-speed adversaries. Dwell time was our friend.

That bottleneck is dissolving. When one operator can run what previously took a team, the constraint on ransomware stops being skilled labor and becomes target supply. And the target supply is enormous — this week alone brought a critical CVSS 10.0 flaw in a widely deployed remote management tool, an actively exploited Gitea authentication bypass, and a sustained campaign against Fortinet firewalls with 74,000 stolen credentials already circulating.

So the question I'd put to decision-makers isn't "are we patched?" It's this: which of your response assumptions still hold when the intrusion is automated end-to-end?

Three places to start:

Your edge. Firewalls, VPN gateways, load balancers, RMM tools — the appliances you trust implicitly are the most reliably exploited assets in the enterprise. They need the shortest patch SLAs you have, not the longest.

Your non-human identities. AI agents with legitimate credentials and broad permissions are the new privileged users. Almost nobody has extended identity governance to cover them. If you can't inventory your AI agents today, that's the gap.

Your response stack. The negotiator case is a warning: the people and firms you'll grant intimate access during your worst week deserve the same vetting as your software vendors.

The attackers have automated. The window to re-baseline your defenses against machine-speed adversaries is measured in months — the Five Eyes just said so, in writing.

What assumption in your security program still depends on the attacker being human? I'd genuinely like to hear how others are re-testing theirs.
