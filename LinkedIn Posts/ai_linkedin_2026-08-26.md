AI wrote the minutes of a meeting last week and invented a person.

Not hallucinated a fact. Not misattributed a quote. It fabricated a name, a surname, and a company — neither of which exist — and filed them as the agreed action item.

The person who would have caught it caught it by accident, because they happened to be writing an email to the real person that same evening. If the introduction had been scheduled for next month, they would have opened the minutes in September, trusted them the way you trust minutes, and sent a warm introduction to a misspelled stranger.

That is the story from The Slow AI newsletter this week. The underlying research is grimmer: Whisper, the speech-to-text model running inside most of these meeting platforms, hallucinates fabricated phrases in about 1% of transcriptions, and 38% of those inventions carry explicit harms. A Whisper-based product was deployed to 30,000 clinicians across 40 health systems. Fabrications appeared in nearly all of the 26,000 transcripts one researcher checked. The platform deletes the original audio for what it calls "data safety reasons."

The record that could prove what was actually said is erased by design. What the machine wrote is the only version that survives.

This is not a hypothetical risk. It is a records management problem operating at scale right now in every industry that uses AI meeting notes — which is most of them.

And regulators are starting to price it in.

Australia's automated decision-making transparency obligations under the Privacy Act kick in on December 10. From that date, your privacy policy must describe the kinds of personal information used in automated processes and broadly how those processes work. "We use an AI platform" is not a description. The OAIC has been explicit.

In Europe, the EU AI Act's high-risk compliance requirements for AI systems involved in decisions that affect individuals came into full force on August 2. Penalties run to 7% of global turnover for prohibited practices. The Uber case — a reported $1 billion fine for automated driver suspensions that offered no appeal or explanation — gives you a sense of what enforcement looks like when governance is absent.

The pattern is consistent: organizations that built efficiency on automated decisions and skipped the governance layer are now discovering that regulators count the people affected, not the cost savings.

The organizations I talk to in financial services, health, and government are asking the right question: what is our AI actually deciding, and could we explain it if we had to?

The harder question is the one underneath it: does our AI run somewhere we can audit?

If your meeting notes, client summaries, or automated decisions are routed through a cloud platform, the audit trail lives in that platform's infrastructure. If that platform is US-based, the Australia-US CLOUD Act Agreement — in force since January — means US legal process can reach data stored on Australian servers. "Australian hosting" and "Australian data sovereignty" are two different things, and the gap between them just got larger.

The organizations building on local and on-premise models have a different answer to that question. They control what the model sees, what it writes, and what the record says. When a regulator asks for the transcript, they have one.

The AI record layer — meeting notes, decision summaries, automated outputs — is becoming the compliance surface that nobody audited because it didn't exist three years ago.

It exists now. December 10 is 106 days away.
