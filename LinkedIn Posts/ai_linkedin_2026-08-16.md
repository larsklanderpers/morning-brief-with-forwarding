Your workflow records are now a product. You're just not the one selling them.

Mercor offered a company called Warmly $300,000 this week for its codebase and workflow records. Warmly turned it down. But most businesses aren't being offered a cheque — the transfer is happening through the default settings of the tools already running inside your organisation.

Meeting assistants. CRM copilots. Email intelligence. Task-mining tools. Every time an AI system watches how your team handles an exception, navigates a legacy system, or fixes a recurring problem, it's capturing operational know-how that AI labs have decided is worth paying for. The default contract posture is usually in their favour.

This landed the same week two other things happened.

OpenAI shipped "Computer History" for ChatGPT — ambient workflow observation that logs your clicks, typing, and app-switching without screenshots. xAI shipped "Teach a Task" for Grok Bot — record a workflow once, the agent repeats it indefinitely. Both are genuinely useful. Both build detailed intelligence about how your organisation works and store it in someone else's infrastructure.

The question isn't whether AI should help your team work. It obviously should. The question is whether you've mapped where that intelligence goes once the session ends.

For regulated businesses — financial services, health, government — this isn't a philosophy question. APRA's CPS 230 operational risk obligations now extend to material AI service providers. Australia's Privacy Act amendments land December 10, requiring disclosure of which systems make automated decisions affecting individual rights. The EU's Article 50 enforcement is already live, with 78% of organisations non-compliant.

The compliance frame and the sovereignty frame are converging on the same practical requirement: know where your inference runs, know what rights your vendors have to your workflow data, and have a plan that doesn't assume your US cloud provider's terms won't change with a geopolitical headwind.

The good news from this week: the local inference option has never been more accessible. Meta open-sourced Muse Glimmer — multimodal, 131K context, runs on a single consumer GPU. OpenAI released open-weight models under Apache 2.0. Qwen3 is the reliable workhorse. Mistral's Paris data centre is online with SLA-backed sovereignty and self-hosting on four GPUs.

The harness — the proprietary workflows, context, and institutional knowledge you've built around AI — is now your most valuable AI asset. It's also the thing most at risk if you haven't governed where it goes.

Three questions worth answering before Q4:

1. Which of your workflows contain proprietary know-how or PII, and which AI tools are touching them?
2. Do your vendor contracts explicitly restrict use of your workflow data for model training?
3. If your primary AI vendor changed its terms tomorrow, what's your fallback inference path?

The organisations that will be best positioned in 2027 are not the ones that used AI the most in 2026. They're the ones that used it with the clearest picture of what they were trading.
