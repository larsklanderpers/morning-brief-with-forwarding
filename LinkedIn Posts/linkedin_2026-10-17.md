**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

CISA published an alert this week whose most important line was not "patch".

It was "rotate every credential the appliance stored or injected."

The appliance is BeyondTrust Remote Support, the tool IT teams use to log into other people's machines to fix them. A China-nexus espionage crew had been exploiting an authentication bypass in it since late August. The patch is out. But CISA's advice to self-hosted customers goes much further: forensics, rebuild from a known-good state, revoke every active session, check every new MFA registration on privileged accounts, and rotate every credential the tool ever handed out.

That is not a patch instruction. That is an admission that a remote support tool is a key ring, and when the key ring is stolen, every door it opens is stolen too.

Put that next to three other stories from the same 48 hours.

In Moscow, a court put a 19-year-old under house arrest over the Dodo Pizza breach. According to Russian press reports, the intrusion began with corporate credentials exposed on GitHub. One secret in one repository, and reportedly fifteen years of customer orders, sold for $100,000.

On the Arch Linux community package index, someone uploaded "yyay", a typo of a popular helper tool. Real code, plus an install script that ran a remote payload as root. One typo, and root on a developer's laptop, which is usually one hop from production.

And in Japan, Burger King's app lost 11.18 million member records: names, birth dates, phone numbers and order history. No card numbers. Just everything a criminal needs to make a phishing call sound like it comes from someone who knows you.

The pattern is not "attackers are getting smarter". It is that we keep building single things that unlock many things, and then measuring our security by how well we guard the single thing.

We count vulnerabilities. We count patches. We count critical CVEs. What almost nobody counts is blast radius: for each tool, key, secret and service account, what does it open?

That question is about to get much louder, because of AI agents.

The same week, NIST, the NSA and CISA published voluntary guidance on incidents where a highly capable AI agent takes actions nobody instructed. Strip away the frontier-lab framing and the first control is the same one CISA is now asking of BeyondTrust customers: know exactly what each agent can reach. Meanwhile Proofpoint agreed to pay $6.3 billion for Varonis, pitched on "controlling what people and AI can access". When a question becomes a multi-billion-dollar category, it is no longer a niche concern.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Most organisations I talk to could not produce, today, a list of every system their remote support tool can log into. Fewer still could do it for the AI agents they deployed this year. That list is the most useful security document most businesses do not have.

So my ask for decision-makers this week is simple. Pick your three most privileged tools: remote support, your CI pipeline, and whichever AI agent has the widest access. For each, write down what it can open. Then ask what you would have to rotate, rebuild and notify if it were stolen tomorrow.

If that answer takes more than an afternoon to produce, you have found your real risk. It is not the next CVE. It is the reach you never wrote down.
