# AI LinkedIn Post - 16 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Your AI vendor is now under investigation in three jurisdictions. Your own servers are not.

That is the quiet shift of this week, and most boards have not noticed it yet.

Look at what landed in five days. The US Federal Trade Commission sent civil investigative demands to OpenAI and Anthropic, reportedly more than 50 pages each, asking for incident records, safeguards, marketing claims and privacy policies. The European Commission told a summit in Dublin that its information requests to the same two labs are its first enforcement actions under the AI Act. And the US Department of Justice charged an OpenAI employee with planting a recording device in a boardroom, after allegedly asking an AI model how to build it.

None of that proves wrongdoing. A demand for documents is not a finding. But it changes what you are buying.

When you send customer data to a frontier model, you are no longer just buying capability. You are entering a supply chain that regulators on two continents are actively examining. Your data processing agreement probably references the vendor's privacy policy. The FTC now wants to know whether that policy described reality. So should you.

Here is the other half of the week, and it is the more interesting half.

Meta released Muse Spark Open: an 816-billion-parameter model with open weights, built for coding and agent work. Meta and Apple say it runs on two Mac Studios. Other reporting puts the real memory floor closer to a terabyte, so treat the desktop claim with care. Either way, the ceiling for what you can run inside your own walls just moved by an order of magnitude.

And in Singapore, Google DeepMind and the government's technology agency built a scam detector that reads conversations on the phone and does not send them anywhere. A government chose on-device inference because reading citizens' messages in someone else's cloud was never going to be acceptable.

Put those together and the decision for any business holding sensitive customer data gets clearer.

The old trade-off was capability or control. You sent data to the frontier because nothing you could host came close. That gap is narrowing fast, and the cost of the frontier is now rising in a currency that does not show up on the invoice: regulatory exposure you inherit from your supplier.

This is not an argument to rip out cloud AI. For most work it is still the right call. It is an argument to draw the line deliberately.

Three things I would do this quarter:

1. List which data must never leave your environment. Customer identifiers, health and financial records, anything covered by privacy law in your markets. Draw that line first, then pick models to fit it, not the other way round.

2. Reread your AI vendor contracts against the questions regulators are now asking. Incident notification, evaluation access, and what the privacy policy actually commits to. If your vendor cannot answer, a regulator may soon answer for them.

3. Price a local option for one sensitive workload. Not a pilot for show. A real cost comparison, including the hardware, the licence conditions on "open" weights, and the compliance work you no longer outsource.

In Australia the clock is concrete. From 10 December, privacy policies must describe decisions made or substantially assisted by computer programs that significantly affect individuals. You cannot disclose a decision you have not found, and you cannot control data you have already sent away.

The frontier is still where the capability is. It is also, this week, where the subpoenas are. Choose which of those you want your customers' data sitting next to.
