Your MFA is no longer protecting you. Let me explain why that's a precise technical claim, not hyperbole.

Bluekit, a phishing kit that began selling on cybercrime forums in April, uses a technique called Browser-in-the-Middle. It doesn't intercept your login — it streams the actual, legitimate login page to you in real time, records your keystrokes and MFA approval, then inherits your fully authenticated session the moment you complete the flow. TOTP codes. Push approvals. SMS. All rendered irrelevant. And it comes with an AI assistant, 40+ brand templates, voice cloning, and automated domain purchasing. Subscription model. No technical expertise required.

This lands in the same week that CISA confirmed active exploitation of Splunk Enterprise — the tool most large organisations use to monitor for exactly this kind of attack — and that Google patched the fifth Chrome zero-day of 2026, exploited before most enterprise environments had deployed the last one.

The pattern here isn't a run of bad luck. It's something more structural.

We are watching sophisticated, expensive attack capabilities become commodity services. What required nation-state resources and specialist tradecraft two years ago now ships with a UI, a customer support channel, and a pricing page. The DirtyClone Linux kernel exploit — published this week with a working walkthrough — follows the same trajectory. Researchers found it, documented it, and handed it to anyone with a local foothold.

Meanwhile, governments are finally starting to legislate the architecture, not just the response. The White House AI EO signed on 2 June creates a dedicated AI cybersecurity clearinghouse and directs agencies to restructure defences within 30 days. CISA's Binding Operational Directive 26-04 shifts federal agencies from calendar-based patching to risk-prioritised remediation. These are structural moves — they signal that the policy layer is catching up to the threat reality.

The implication for boards and executives is this: your security programme's threat model needs to reflect 2026, not 2019.

MFA is necessary but not sufficient — phishing-resistant authentication (FIDO2, passkeys) is the only credential-layer defence that holds against Browser-in-the-Middle. Your perimeter appliances — VPNs, firewalls — are active exploitation targets, not passive protection; Check Point and Fortinet both have critical vulnerabilities being actively abused right now. And any AI tool your workforce is adopting is a potential social engineering vector — this week saw threat actors creating fake OpenAI tenants to harvest corporate information from employees who thought they were joining an authorised workspace.

The security teams that will hold the line in this environment are the ones that treat these as interconnected systemic risks, not isolated incidents to be patched and filed. The teams that won't are the ones still measuring maturity by whether they have MFA turned on.

If your security strategy could have been written in 2020, it's already outdated.
