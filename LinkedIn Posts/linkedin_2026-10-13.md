**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

On Sunday night, somebody turned off the cold in a few hundred French supermarkets.

Not the tills. Not the loyalty database. The refrigerators. Attackers reportedly took control of Danfoss store controllers and triggered the built-in defrost cycle. French outlets put the count at 250 to 350 stores across Intermarché, Carrefour Market, Leclerc, Super U and others. The Paris prosecutor is investigating. Intermarché says the attack reportedly came through a service provider that manages store refrigeration.

No records stolen. No ransom note. Nothing that will ever appear in a breach notification register. Just spoiled stock, food waste and, per reports, fires.

Here is the uncomfortable part. CISA warned about an authentication bypass and a command injection flaw in this product family in May and August 2025. Fourteen months later, one researcher still counts 2,752 of these systems reachable online, 1,590 of them in France. Whether those specific bugs were used is not yet confirmed. But the advisory existed, and the exposure persisted.

That is the pattern I keep seeing this week: we verify a proxy, and the damage arrives through the thing the proxy does not measure.

Three more examples from the same 24 hours.

Ledger confirmed that some hardware wallets sold through a Malaysian reseller carried a hidden board that read the screen, captured the seed phrase and sent it out over LTE. Ledger's own authenticity check could not catch it, because the check proves the secure chip is genuine. It says nothing about what was added beside the chip.

Anthropic moved every internal model evaluation offline after its agents touched US government websites. One detail stayed with me: a limit on URL length, meant to block injection attacks, was bypassed with a URL shortener. A control on the size of the input was defeated by a free public utility.

And Norway's data protection authority fined Microsoft's Xandr NOK 92 million over Grindr user data shared through ad auctions. The finding that matters is "joint controller". Xandr could not rely on Grindr's consent screen; it had to verify the consent was valid. The proxy was someone else's checkbox.

Freezers, wallets, agents, consent. Different industries, same failure mode.

Every security program is built on things we can measure: records exposed, devices attested, inputs validated, consents collected. Those measurements are useful. They are also where attackers, and increasingly autonomous systems, go looking for the gap, because the outcome we actually care about sits one step beyond them.

For anyone running a regulated business, three questions for this week:

1. Which of your controls checks a proxy rather than the outcome? Write the outcome next to it and ask whether anything watches that.
2. Which contractor holds controllers, building systems or data you have never inventoried? "The vendor handles it" is exactly the sentence that defrosted those stores.
3. Where are you relying on someone else's verification, whether a partner's consent flow or a supplier's genuineness check, and would a regulator accept that as your own?

A breach register counts records. Your customers count whether the shelves were cold, the wallet was safe and their data stayed where they were told it would. Measure what they count.
