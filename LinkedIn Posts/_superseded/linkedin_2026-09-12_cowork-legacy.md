974 vulnerabilities. One patch cycle. Two already being exploited.

That's Microsoft's September Patch Tuesday — the largest single security release in the company's history. 20 of those flaws are wormable, meaning they can move from system to system without a user doing anything. Two are already in the wild. And as a bonus, applying the patch breaks Remote Desktop Services on Windows Server 2019, 2022, and 2025.

Welcome to the volume problem.

The number isn't the story. The trend is. Microsoft's September release is 5.6 times the pre-2026 monthly average. Adobe just patched a CVSS 10.0 zero-day in Magento three days after attackers started deploying Rust backdoors through it. CISA added four vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog this week — Cisco, Citrix, Fortinet, Chrome — with a federal patch deadline of today.

Meanwhile, the NSA, CISA and FBI published a joint advisory this week naming six Chinese AI companies — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, Z.AI — for conducting what they called "industrial-scale" extraction of US frontier AI models through distillation. Billions of tokens. Automated agents. Anthropic confirmed it dismantled Alibaba-linked infrastructure targeting Claude.

China called it normal commercial practice. They're not entirely wrong about the technical description. But "normal" is doing a lot of work in that sentence when the scale is billions of tokens routed through obfuscated accounts to extract proprietary capability from systems you don't own.

And then there's this: a likely Russian-speaking threat actor used hundreds of AI agents to exploit PaperCut across 440 systems at 395 organisations in 48 countries. Not a nation-state team. Not a large criminal organisation. One operator, scaled by AI.

That's the actual news this week. Not any single vulnerability. Not any single threat actor. The news is that the volume is now too high for security programmes that were designed for 2022.

The question I'd be asking if I were sitting in a board meeting this month is not which of these to prioritise. It's whether the security function is sized and structured to operate in a world where every week looks like this week.

Most aren't. Most were built for the old tempo.

The gap between the threat volume that now exists and the organisational capacity most enterprises carry is where the next serious incidents are being written. The organisations closing that gap are doing it through consolidation, automation, and hard decisions about what they're actually going to defend. The ones that aren't are doing it through hope.

Hope is not a patch management strategy.
