# AI LinkedIn Post — 13 September 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Your staff are already using Claude and ChatGPT on client work. The question is whether your compliance team knows what that means.

A newsletter circulating this morning — "Life of a Prompt" by Ruben — is the clearest explanation yet of what actually happens when someone uploads a client contract to an AI assistant. The short version: it becomes a database record, it may be read by a human safety reviewer, shared links can be — and have been — indexed by Google (Claude artifacts, July 2026, including medical records), and de-identification before training does not remove the intellectual content of the work.

Meanwhile, this week three frontier labs simultaneously launched AI models specifically designed for cybersecurity work. Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI each announced a defender-access program. They also co-signed a letter with 100+ companies warning that AI-enabled cyberattacks are about to become "far more widespread and sophisticated."

Read those two things together: the tools to attack your systems are becoming easier to access and more capable, while the tools to defend them are being gated behind curated access programs your threat team may not qualify for.

At the same time, ChatGPT Work quietly shipped integrations with Gmail, Google Drive, SharePoint, and Slack — with a feature that learns users' writing styles from their connected email and document history. For any organisation under the Australian Privacy Act, APRA's CPS 230, or the EU AI Act, that is a material third-party data relationship. It needs a DPIA and a contract review, not an IT ticket.

The Dec 10 Australian Privacy Act ADM obligation is 89 days away. From that date, organisations must disclose in their privacy policies which decisions about customers are substantially made by AI — with penalties up to AUD 50 million. Eighty-nine days is not long if you haven't yet mapped which of your customer-facing processes qualify.

Here is what I would be doing this week if I ran compliance or risk for a regulated business:

First, find out which personal-plan AI accounts your staff are using on corporate devices. A personal plan account used with client data is not covered by any enterprise data processing agreement. It is an unmanaged exposure.

Second, audit any shared Claude or ChatGPT links your organisation has created. Google has demonstrated it will index these. Your clients' data may already be public.

Third, if you have been treating "Australian data residency" as equivalent to sovereignty, re-read the AU-US CLOUD Act Agreement that came into force in January. Residency tells you where data sits. Sovereignty tells you who can legally compel it. Those are no longer the same answer.

The window for "we'll sort out the AI governance later" is closing in weeks, not months. Every week there is a new integration, a new capability, and a new enforcement event. The organisations that move early are not just managing risk — they are building a compliance story that their competitors cannot match in a sales conversation.
