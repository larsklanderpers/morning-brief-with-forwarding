Your security tools are now the attack surface.

This week, three tools organisations rely on to *find* vulnerabilities — Aqua Security's Trivy, Bitwarden, and Checkmarx — were compromised in a supply chain attack. Credentials stolen from those tools were used to breach OpenAI and Vercel. Attackers have worked out that compromising the vulnerability scanner is more efficient than finding the vulnerability.

At the same time, North Korea poisoned 131 AI framework packages, embedding themselves inside the development pipelines building the next generation of enterprise software. SafeDep found 353 poisoned versions across 79 npm package names. The command-and-control channel runs over public blockchain infrastructure — difficult to block because the infrastructure is otherwise legitimate.

And then N-central. N-able's remote monitoring and management platform — the tool MSPs use to manage every device in every customer network — had a zero-day exploited starting July 31. Attackers gained administrative access, pivoted to managed endpoints via N-central's own remote access feature, and installed Cloudflare tunnels for persistence. Patching N-central doesn't close the tunnel. Every customer of every affected MSP is a downstream victim until they check the published indicators of compromise themselves.

The pattern is the same in each case. Attackers aren't breaking down doors. They're walking through trusted channels that were pre-compromised.

This is the supply chain threat model that security teams have been warned about for five years, now running at operational scale. And the defensive posture most organisations have in place — treating supply chain security as a procurement compliance checkbox — was designed for a different era.

What the actual posture needs to look like:

Every vendor with privileged access to your environment should be subject to continuous monitoring, not point-in-time assessment. The MSP managing your endpoints, the CI/CD pipeline fetching packages automatically, the security scanner with read access to your source code — each of these is a lateral movement opportunity if the vendor is compromised.

CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report, released this week, adds another dimension: China-nexus actors are now exploiting critical CVEs within 24 hours of public proof-of-concept release. The patch window that security teams have operated around — 7 days, 30 days — is a fiction. For internet-facing infrastructure, it's hours.

The perimeter isn't a firewall. It isn't even an identity provider. It's the set of vendors and dependencies you've granted elevated access to without applying commensurate scrutiny.

Decision-makers who haven't asked their security teams "what does our vendor compromise playbook look like?" this quarter should make it the first question in their next briefing.
