**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The U.S. government just quietly admitted that perimeter security is over.

Not in a congressional hearing. Not in a strategy document. In a 17-page guidance paper on deploying fake credentials, honeypot databases, and decoy admin accounts inside your own network.

CISA's new "Cyber Decoys" guidance, published this week, is framed as a detection enhancement. Read it differently: it is a government acknowledgment that attackers are already inside, they're using your own tools to move around, and your current monitoring almost certainly won't catch them.

That framing matters because of what else dropped this week.

Researchers documented the first confirmed agentic ransomware campaign. A single threat actor deployed hundreds of AI agents — not a human, not a team, hundreds of autonomous agents — to exploit PaperCut systems across 395 organisations in 48 countries. The AI orchestrated reconnaissance, exploitation, and lateral movement simultaneously, at a speed and scale no human-operated campaign has matched. This is not a proof of concept. It happened.

Meanwhile, Microsoft's September Patch Tuesday addressed a record 964 CVEs in a single release — including a 9.8 CVSS RCE in Windows DNS Server that allows unauthenticated remote code execution, and a researcher claiming one of the zero-day patches is incomplete, with a working proof-of-concept still available.

And CISA added two more entries to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, following four additions earlier this month that included auth bypasses in Citrix NetScaler and Cisco's Firewall Management Center. Those two products sit at network perimeters. Auth bypasses in perimeter products are how you get onto the inside.

The pattern connecting all of this is one that security teams need to be able to articulate to their boards:

**The attacker now operates inside the trusted environment, using valid credentials and legitimate tools, with AI removing the human operator bottleneck that previously limited attack scale.** The IBM security team put it plainly this year: the biggest breaches of 2026 will come from the tools companies willingly connected to their own systems.

CISA's response — deploy decoys, create tripwires, build detection for when someone touches a fake database that no legitimate user should ever touch — is the right operational answer for an environment where you cannot trust your own perimeter.

The strategic answer is harder: organisations need to model what a fully autonomous, AI-orchestrated attack looks like against their specific environment and ask whether their monitoring was designed to catch someone with valid credentials and no malware. Most monitoring wasn't designed for that. Most monitoring was designed to catch things that look like attacks.

Attackers who look like legitimate users, assisted by AI agents that never sleep, are the 2026 threat. The government has told you they're already inside. The question is what you've planted for them to step on.
