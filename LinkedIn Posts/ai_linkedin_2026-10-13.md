# AI LinkedIn Post - 13 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Your customer data has a longer life than your vendor contract.

Three stories from the last 48 hours make the point better than any policy paper.

First: AI labs are buying the leftovers of dead startups. Slack archives, email, code, Notion and Drive. One wind-down firm says it has brokered more than 150 of these deals since April. In August, during an airline's bankruptcy, a reported 100 million emails and 500 million Teams messages went to the highest bidder for about $10 million. The sellers say personal information is scrubbed first. A lawyer quoted in the coverage calls the material "legally radioactive." There is no US federal law stopping the sale.

Ask yourself: which of your vendors holds your customers' data, and what happens to it if that vendor folds and a liquidator starts taking bids?

Second: Meta released new models this week with a feature called Active Memory. It is designed to remember important personal context across weeks or months of conversations. That is useful. It is also a brand new store of personal information, and I have not yet seen anyone publish where it lives, how long it is kept, or how a customer gets it deleted.

If your staff use it with customer detail in the prompt, you now have a processor you have not assessed.

Third: an agent product used by more than 400,000 people a week has reportedly changed the model underneath it twice in six weeks, and is moving to another lab's model now. The users did not sign anything new. For a business with privacy obligations, that is a change of sub-processor, whether or not anyone called it one.

Put those together and the pattern is clear. The risk is not only where your data sits today. It is who can end up holding it tomorrow: through a memory feature, through an insolvency, or through a silent model swap.

And concentration does not save you either. This week's reported strikes on three Russian data centres reportedly took payments, maps and mail down with them. Onshore is not the same as resilient.

None of the fixes are glamorous. They are contractual, and they are cheap compared with a breach notification:

1. Read the insolvency and termination clauses in every SaaS and AI contract that touches customer data. Deletion on exit should be an obligation with evidence, not a promise.
2. Keep a sub-processor register that names the model, not just the vendor. Require notice when it changes.
3. Treat any vendor memory feature as a new data store. Assess it before staff use it.
4. Run the most sensitive steps, embeddings and retrieval especially, on infrastructure you control. Small open models now make that practical.
5. Have a second site. Residency and resilience are different controls.

In Australia, the Privacy Act's automated decision-making disclosure duties start on 10 December. In Europe, the AI Act's high-risk data governance duties are already live. Both assume you know where your data goes and who decides with it.

If you cannot answer "who holds our customers' data if this vendor disappears tomorrow?" in one sentence, that is this week's job.

Capability is rentable. Custody is not.
