**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

This weekend a bank confirmed it handed customers' passports to a criminal.

Nobody broke in. The request arrived from a real government email domain, and the bank did what banks are supposed to do with government requests: it answered.

That is Revolut's disclosure from Saturday. An unauthorised party used a legitimate government agency domain to send fraudulent information requests. Revolut treated them as genuine and sent back identity details, passport and licence copies, and possibly verification selfies and transaction histories. It says "a limited number" of customers were affected and will not name the agency.

Put it beside three other stories from the same few days and a pattern appears.

Florida says the breach of its DAVID driver database came through the login of a single police officer, stored on that officer's personal device. The extortion crew claims more than 200,000 records. The state has not confirmed a number, but it has confirmed the door: a real government credential.

Microsoft says crews linked to the same extortion brand have spent months phoning staff as the IT help desk, with an urgent story about "updating your passkey". The passkey is the pretext. The goal is to walk the victim through a phishing page or a device-code sign-in, then register the attacker's own MFA method and download the mailbox, SharePoint and OneDrive at volume.

And Microsoft separately documented a million-email invoice scam, sent over three days in August, impersonating each target's own CEO alongside a fake ServiceNow invoice chain, asking accounts payable for nearly $50,000. It found signs of generative AI in the templates, and was careful to say it could not measure how much AI wrote. That caveat deserves to travel with the number.

Four stories. No zero-day among them. Each one borrows a trusted identity (a government, a police officer, your own help desk, your own CEO) and then lets the organisation's own process finish the job.

We have spent a decade hardening the technical perimeter. The attackers noticed, and moved to the procedural one. The weak point is no longer the firewall. It is the moment an employee decides a request is legitimate because of who it appears to come from.

Domain authentication does not solve this. It proves where an email came from, not who wrote it or whether the request is real. Revolut's email passed that test. So will the next one.

Meanwhile the regulators are shortening the other clock. Since Friday, the EU's Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers of connected products to give an early warning within 24 hours of learning a vulnerability in their product is being actively exploited. The time you are allowed to not know is shrinking, while the time attackers need to be believed is shrinking too.

So here is the exercise I would put in front of any executive team this week.

List every request your organisation obeys because of who it appears to come from. Lawful-access and regulator requests. Help-desk password and MFA resets. New or changed payees. Partner and agency logins into your data.

For each one, write down the check that does not travel through the channel the request arrived on. A call-back to a contact you already hold. A help desk that never starts an authentication change by phone. A payment change that waits for a second person.

If a line on that list has no answer, you have found your next breach. It will not need an exploit. It will need a convincing email and one helpful employee.

The attackers are not getting better at breaking things. They are getting better at being believed.
