**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Your adversary's average breakout time is now 29 minutes.

Yours — from detection to containment — is probably measured in hours. That gap is the story of cybersecurity in the second half of 2026.

This week handed us the evidence in bulk.

**The ShinyHunters playbook is now an industrial process.** In a single month, one cybercrime group ran simultaneous extortion campaigns against Oracle PeopleSoft users (100+ organisations, 68% of them universities), Eastman Kodak, Madison Square Garden Sports, Amazon One Medical, and the Council of Europe. The method was consistent: find a single critical unpatched vulnerability in widely-deployed enterprise software, enumerate every internet-facing instance, and compromise them all before the patch cycle catches up. Oracle published its advisory the same day TechCrunch reported the attacks. The window between disclosure and weaponisation was effectively zero.

**CISA set two back-to-back deadlines for federal agencies to patch actively exploited flaws** — one in Cisco's Unified Communications Manager, one in PTC's Windchill product lifecycle software. Both missed by any organisation still running unpatched are now confirmed entry points for adversaries who don't need to be innovative; they just need a target that didn't move fast enough.

**Then Microsoft dropped a 208-CVE Patch Tuesday** — including two unauthenticated CVSS-9.8 remote code execution vulnerabilities (one in HTTP.sys, one in the Windows kernel) that require no user interaction and no credentials to exploit. The community is treating this as a record cycle. It's becoming the new normal.

Sit with what that combination means operationally. Your adversaries have a working database of 30,000 Fortinet VPN credentials compiled from prior breaches. They have 24 billion username/password pairs indexed and searchable. They have AI that dropped their average attack velocity by 89% year-on-year. And they're now sitting at the keyboard for 29 minutes before they have what they came for.

The question that should be on every board agenda this week is not "what's in the patch queue?" It's: **what does our real-time external exposure look like right now, and how long would it take us to know if someone was already inside?**

The Trump administration's AI cybersecurity Executive Order — triggered in part by an AI model that could outpace human researchers at finding and exploiting vulnerabilities — is the policy world catching up to a reality that practitioners have been living for 18 months. Voluntary pre-release frameworks and AI clearinghouses are the institutional response to a threat that's already reshaping the economics of attack. That's not a criticism; it's the normal lag between operational reality and governance. But it means the burden of response remains on organisations and their security teams, not regulators, for the foreseeable future.

The organisations that will fare best in this environment share one characteristic: they treat vulnerability management as a continuous operation, not a monthly calendar event. Automated exposure monitoring, real-time asset inventory, and detection SLAs measured in minutes rather than days aren't aspirational — they're the minimum viable posture for 2026.

The gap between 29 minutes and "we'll review it next patch cycle" is where most breaches happen.

Don't live in that gap.
