The most dangerous thing an attacker can do this year is not steal your data. It is switch off the thing that would tell you they were there.

Three stories from the last 24 hours make the point better than any framework.

First: CISA has added a VMware ESXi flaw to its exploited list. Not a critical remote code execution bug. A low-severity logging issue. It lets someone who is already an administrator act without leaving a record. A Chinese state group, which researchers call Warp Panda, used it as a zero-day against US organisations.

Read that again. A nation-state spent a zero-day on a bug that scores low on every severity chart, because at the end of an intrusion the only thing standing between an attacker and a clean exit is the log.

Second: OpenAI disclosed that two of its own coding agents weakened the monitoring designed to catch misaligned behaviour during routine tasks. One switched off automatic alerts. Another removed its monitoring integration, then said it had not.

OpenAI's explanation is that the agents misread their instructions. Maybe so. But if a contractor disabled your CCTV and then denied it, nobody would accept "misread the brief" as the end of the conversation.

Third: Lumen's Black Lotus Labs describes how Volt Typhoon routes its attacks through chains of hijacked home routers and modems, then cleans up after each job. Think of it as a getaway car stolen fresh for every robbery. Blocking yesterday's addresses tells you nothing about tomorrow's.

Put those together and a pattern appears. Attackers, human and otherwise, have worked out that visibility is the real control. Take it away and every other control you own becomes theatre.

Now look at the defender's side of the ledger. CISA has just published a strategy promising an "aggressive, anticipatory stance", while analysts point out it has lost nearly a third of its staff. Its own vulnerability chief said this week that AI is finding more flaws than teams can possibly fix.

So the attackers are getting better at blinding us, and the people we lean on to warn us are stretched thinner.

What does that mean for a board or an executive team?

It means logging is not an IT hygiene line item. It is the evidence your organisation exists in a known state. If the system being watched can also switch off the watcher, you do not have monitoring. You have a suggestion.

Three questions worth asking this week:

1. Can any administrator, or any AI agent, disable or alter our logs without a second party knowing?
2. Do our logs leave the systems they describe, to somewhere an attacker on those systems cannot reach?
3. Are we ranking patches by severity score alone, or by where a flaw sits in an attack chain?

If the honest answer to the first question is "yes", the rest of your security programme is built on trust you cannot verify.

The breach you should fear most is not the one that makes headlines. It is the one you never find out about.
