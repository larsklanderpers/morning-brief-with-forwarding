# AI LinkedIn Post — 28 June 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

Last week, the US government banned export of two Anthropic AI models overnight.

No warning. No transition period. Organisations that had built workflows around Fable 5 and Mythos 5 lost access the same day the executive order landed. If your AI strategy depends on a US cloud vendor maintaining uninterrupted access to their own models, that assumption just broke in public.

And it's not isolated.

In the same week, APRA released its April industry letter — one of the sharpest pieces of regulatory writing I've seen from an Australian prudential regulator. The language isn't ambiguous: "governance, risk management, assurance and operational resilience practices are not keeping pace with the scale, speed, and complexity of AI adoption." Four specific failures named. Every regulated entity in scope. CPS 230 goes live July 1.

Meanwhile, in Europe, Mistral just signed a framework agreement to run AI models for the French military — on French-controlled infrastructure, by design. France didn't ask the US for permission. They built a sovereign stack.

Here's the pattern worth reading across all three: **the era of unrestricted, jurisdiction-free AI access is ending**, and the organisations that treated data sovereignty as a future problem are finding it's a present one.

For businesses that hold customer PII — which, if you're in financial services, health, legal, or retail, is most of you — this week's news is a forcing function.

The December 2026 Privacy Act amendments already require you to disclose which AI systems are making substantially automated decisions that affect your customers. AUD 50 million penalties. OAIC compliance sweeps already underway. APRA is about to audit whether your AI vendors are formally registered as material service providers with tested exit strategies.

The question I keep getting asked is: "What's the alternative to cloud AI?"

In June 2026, the honest answer is: **better than you think**.

NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra (550B parameters, permissive license) shipped June 4 — runs locally, commercially usable, no vendor dependency. MiniMax M3 offers a 1-million-token context window on open weights. GLM-5.2, MIT-licensed, runs via Ollama. The developer community's headline this week was literally "the open model nobody can ban."

We are past the point where local AI means accepting inferior capability. We are at the point where a well-designed on-premise deployment can process your entire customer contract archive, run decision-support on sensitive PII, and produce an audit trail — without a single byte leaving your network.

That's not a technical preference. Under the Privacy Act and CPS 230, it's becoming the most defensible architecture.

The businesses building that capability now — rather than waiting for a regulator letter or an overnight export control — will be the ones that can say yes to AI opportunities their competitors have to turn down.

The frontier is still moving. But the most important AI infrastructure decision for an Australian business in 2026 isn't which frontier model to use. It's whether you control where your data goes when you use any of them.
