Microsoft patched a critical SharePoint vulnerability in May. They just forgot to tell anyone.

CVE-2026-45659 — a remote code execution flaw sitting in SharePoint's guts — was quietly fixed two months ago with no CVE, no advisory, no announcement. This week CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and gave federal agencies until July 4 to patch. Translation: attackers found it before defenders knew it existed.

Here's why that one detail should keep executives up at night.

Almost simultaneously, DHS confirmed that hackers breached HSIN — the Homeland Security Information Network, the unclassified platform that federal, state, local and private-sector partners use to coordinate and share threat intelligence. The vector? A SharePoint system used for inter-agency collaboration. All of this while the United States is hosting World Cup matches and leaning on that exact network to coordinate security across dozens of agencies.

A silent patch and a breach of the government's coordination backbone, connected by the same technology, in the same fortnight. That's not a coincidence. That's a pattern.

The pattern is this: **our worst exposures are no longer in the exploits. They're in the layers of trust we've stopped inspecting.**

Look at the rest of this week. A maximum-severity flaw in SimpleHelp — a remote monitoring tool used by managed service providers — put hundreds of downstream companies at risk through a console none of them chose or manage. Booby-trapped proof-of-concept exploits on GitHub were caught delivering malware to the very security researchers who pull that code to study attacks. An 81-million-attempt password-spray campaign quietly enumerated Microsoft 365 tenants for their weakest credentials.

Different stories, one thread. Vendor changelogs. Government coordination networks. Managed-service tooling. Public code repositories. Every one of these is a trust relationship we've outsourced and stopped scrutinizing — and every one is now an attack surface.

We spent a decade hardening the endpoint. Meanwhile the real risk migrated to the seams between organizations: the patch you'll never hear about, the platform that aggregates everyone's sensitive data, the vendor holding an admin key into your network, the repo you trust because it's popular.

So here's the uncomfortable question for anyone with a risk register: **which of your patches would you never even know about?**

If your answer depends entirely on a vendor choosing to disclose, you don't have a vulnerability management program — you have a subscription to someone else's honesty. And this week proved that honesty is optional.

Three moves worth making before your next board meeting:

Map your outsourced trust. Not your assets — your dependencies. Which platforms aggregate your most sensitive coordination data? Which third parties hold privileged access? Those are your crown jewels now, and half of them aren't on your asset inventory.

Assume silent failure. Build detection that doesn't wait for a CVE. If your SOC can only act on vulnerabilities that got a press release, it's blind to exactly the class of attack that just hit DHS.

Put supply-chain trust on the board risk map. Not the patch queue — the risk map. When a tool you've never heard of can reach your whole client base, that's an enterprise risk, not an IT ticket.

The attackers this week didn't break sophisticated defenses. They walked through trust relationships nobody was watching. The organizations that stay off the leak sites will be the ones that started watching first.

What trusted dependency in your environment would you be most afraid to have breached? That's probably where to start.
