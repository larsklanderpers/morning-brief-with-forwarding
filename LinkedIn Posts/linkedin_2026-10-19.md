$40,000 to break it. Hundreds of millions to fix it.

Two numbers from the past week tell you more about security budgets in 2026 than any analyst forecast.

The first: XBOW, an AI pentesting firm, built a full-chain Chrome exploit in six days using a publicly available OpenAI model. Three bugs, chained from a web page to code execution on Windows. Compute bill: about $40,000. Google paid them $250,000, its top reward, because the chain was real.

The second: Bank of America's CEO told CNBC the bank brought in 1,000 contractors to work alongside 3,000 staff, patching flaws that frontier AI models had surfaced. Combined with anti-money laundering work, it added "a couple hundred million plus" to one quarter's costs.

Put them side by side. Discovery now scales with compute. Remediation still scales with people.

That asymmetry is the story of this year, and it does not care whether the person running the model is a researcher chasing a bounty or a crew chasing a ransom. XBOW sells AI pentesting, so treat its economics as a vendor's claim. But Google's cheque is an independent verdict, and Bank of America's cost line is not marketing.

Now look at what attackers are actually doing with their cheap discovery.

Over the weekend, attackers were confirmed exploiting a flaw in SiteGuard, a Japanese web application firewall, to run commands as root. The firewall you buy so the web server does not have to be perfect became the way in.

Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 published how a suspected state actor got into a telecom's central FortiManager, walked off with the configuration of more than 500 managed firewalls, and then planted an implant that lives only in memory, opens no ports and borrows legitimate traffic to talk. Compromise the console, inherit the fleet.

And the fix side has its own tax. Microsoft confirmed this month's Windows Server updates are breaking Exchange sign-in and mail flow on some servers. Every patch is also a change, and changes break things.

So what should a decision-maker do with this?

First, stop budgeting for security as if finding and fixing cost the same. They no longer do. If your plan assumes your team can keep pace with AI-speed discovery by working harder, it is a plan to fall behind.

Second, treat management consoles as crown jewels. Firewall managers, backup apps with tenant-wide access, remote support tools. These are the single points where one compromise becomes five hundred. They deserve the same protection as your domain controllers, and usually get far less.

Third, make rollback a first-class capability. The faster you patch, the more often a patch will hurt you. A tested way back is what lets you move quickly without gambling the business on every update.

Fourth, push remediation cost back up the supply chain. Most organisations do not have 4,000 people to throw at patching. If a vendor's code is the source of the flaw, the contract should say who pays to fix it, and how fast.

The attackers' cost curve has already bent. The question for every board is whether the defender's curve bends with it, or whether we keep paying human prices for machine-speed problems.

Which side of that equation is your budget built for?
