CISA gave federal agencies until Friday to patch a framework for building AI agents.

Read that again. Not a model. Not a chatbot. The plumbing underneath the agents — Langflow — is now sitting in the same Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog as Adobe ColdFusion, being actively exploited in the wild. That single line item tells you more about where security is heading in 2026 than any threat report.

Here's what today's headlines have in common, and it isn't obvious at first glance.

Accenture confirmed a breach after an attacker advertised 35GB of stolen source code. Everyone's talking about the code. The part that should keep CISOs up at night is what came with it: RSA keys, SSH keys, Azure access tokens. A source-code leak is embarrassing. A leaked access token is a running foothold — an unlocked door someone is already walking through.

Deutsche Bank confirmed a breach that entered through a third-party vendor. Ubiquiti shipped patches for seven critical flaws in gear that lives in thousands of branch-office closets with no named patch owner. ACSC flagged active campaigns against Fortinet VPNs, WordPress-hosted malware, and cPanel exploitation. And a new ransomware family, GodDamn, ships a vulnerable kernel driver specifically to switch off your endpoint protection before it encrypts anything.

Notice where none of these attacks landed: the places you actually watch.

They landed in the seams. The VPN concentrator. The self-hosted Git server. The vendor two steps removed from your brand. The developer's access token. The kernel below your EDR agent. For a decade we've poured budget into instrumenting endpoints and clouds — and attackers have quietly relocated to the one part of the estate that has no telemetry at all.

Now layer AI on top. This year saw the first fully autonomous, LLM-orchestrated post-exploitation attack documented in the wild. The World Economic Forum says 94% of organizations now name AI the defining cybersecurity force of 2026. What that actually means in operational terms: the skill ceiling that used to throttle attacker volume is gone. Hyper-personalized phishing, exploit-chaining, and adaptive malware now run at machine speed. The constraint on your adversary is no longer talent. It's compute — and compute is cheap.

So if you lead a security program, here's the uncomfortable question for your next staff meeting. Not "do we have EDR" — you do. The question is: **where do we have no visibility whatsoever?**

Which VPN appliance hasn't been patched since install? Which self-hosted developer platform holds your source and your secrets with last year's version running? Which vendor could lose your data and put *your* name in the headline? Which developer access token, if leaked tomorrow, hands someone a live session inside your cloud?

The government's Friday patch deadlines are, effectively, a free early-warning siren for the private sector. If federal agencies are sprinting to close a flaw, yours is already being scanned. The real test is whether your patch cadence for edge and developer infrastructure moves as fast as your endpoint cadence does.

For almost every organization I talk to, it doesn't. That gap — not the next zero-day — is the risk worth owning this quarter.

Instrument the seams. That's the whole game now.
