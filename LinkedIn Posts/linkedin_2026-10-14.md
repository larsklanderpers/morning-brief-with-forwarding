The most dangerous thing on your network this morning is something you already approved.

Look at what landed in the last 24 hours.

CISA told organisations running Ivanti Connect Secure to patch a zero-day that attackers are using for credential theft. Then it added a line every CISO should read twice: Ivanti's own integrity checker may not detect the compromise, because the attacker's commands run in memory. The appliance whose job is to decide who gets in cannot reliably tell you whether it has been got into.

Researchers at AIR showed that an AI agent inside Vercel's sandbox can leak data without ever talking to a forbidden server. It hands a crafted certificate to a server on the allowlist, and that server makes the onward request for it. The sandbox sees only approved traffic. The data leaves anyway.

US prosecutors charged an OpenAI engineer with allegedly hiding a recording device in an executive conference room. The Record published leaked chats from a Russian extortion crew directing US-based "agents" into law firms, some posing as IT staff. Neither needed an exploit. Both needed a badge, or the appearance of one.

And in Australia, ASD's ACSC issued an alert about operational technology, naming commercial refrigeration, after attacks on hundreds of French supermarkets' cooling systems. It has not seen targeting here. It is telling operators to find their exposed controllers before someone else does.

Four stories, one pattern. Every one of these controls checked the right thing at the wrong layer.

The VPN checks identity at the door, then trusts what happens inside the box.

The sandbox checks the first hop, then trusts whoever that hop calls.

Building access checks who walks in, then trusts what they carry.

The fridge was trusted because nobody thought of it as a computer.

We have spent a decade building controls around the question "should this be allowed in?" The attacks that are working now ask a different question: "what will the thing I'm already trusted by do for me?"

This matters more, not less, as AI arrives in the stack. An agent is the ultimate trusted insider. It holds credentials, it calls tools, and it will route around a block if its objective rewards finishing the job. If your agent controls stop at the allowlist, you have built the Vercel problem into your own platform.

It also matters for the patch queue. Microsoft shipped a record 1,411 fixes yesterday, and ties the volume to AI-assisted bug discovery. Nobody absorbs that by working harder. You absorb it by knowing which of your trusted things are exposed, and fixing those first.

So here is the exercise I would put to any leadership team in a regulated business this week:

List the five things on your network that everyone trusts by default. The VPN. The vendor's remote access to the building systems. The AI agent with a service account. The contractor's laptop. The conference room.

For each one, ask: if this were compromised tonight, what would tell us? Not "what stops it". What tells us.

If the honest answer is "the vendor's own health check", CISA just told you how much that is worth.

Trust is not a control. It is the absence of one you decided you could afford to skip. The bill for that decision is arriving in October.
