# LinkedIn Post — 30 June 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

You don't need to break encryption to read encrypted messages.

You just need to steal the key. And right now, Russian intelligence is doing exactly that — at scale.

This week, the FBI and CISA issued a joint advisory warning that Russian-linked actors (FSB-embedded, operating under the UNC5792 and UNC4221 designations) have shifted their Signal phishing campaign. They're no longer trying to break the encryption. They're tricking users into handing over their Backup Recovery Keys — which grants complete, retroactive access to the victim's full message history, on a device the attacker controls.

The target list? Government officials. Military personnel. Journalists. Ukrainian military and civil servants.

Here's the part that should give every security leader pause: creating a new Signal account with the same phone number does **not** invalidate a stolen key. Once it's gone, it's gone.

---

We've spent years telling organisations to adopt encrypted communications. That's still the right advice. But this attack reveals a gap in how we think about secure comms: we've focused almost entirely on the encryption layer, and almost nothing on the key management and user education layer.

Encryption without key hygiene is a vault with a glass window.

---

At the same time this Signal advisory landed, we saw June's Microsoft Patch Tuesday break a record — 198 CVEs, the largest in the program's history. Thirty-two rated critical. Two Windows components with CVSS 9.8 scores and unauthenticated remote code execution. HTTP.sys. The Windows Kernel itself.

And 28.3% of new CVEs disclosed in 2026 are being exploited within 24 hours of public disclosure.

The patch window — that comfortable 30-60 day assumption built into most enterprise vulnerability management programs — is functionally closed. We've built entire operating models around a timeline that no longer exists.

---

Meanwhile, prompt injection attacks on AI agents surged 340% in the first half of 2026. OWASP lists it as the #1 AI security threat. This week, CrowdStrike confirmed that threat actors are now using prompt injection to turn AI systems into on-demand malware generators — dynamically producing scripts that adapt to their environment and evade static detection.

Here's the architectural reality: large language models cannot reliably distinguish trusted instructions from attacker-controlled input, because both arrive as the same stream of tokens. This is not a bug that will be patched in the next model release. It's a fundamental property of how these systems work.

Every board asking "are we using AI?" also needs to ask: "what can our AI be tricked into doing — and by whom?"

---

Three stories. One pattern.

Attackers are operating systematically against our trust models — not just our technical controls. They're exploiting the trust users place in secure apps. The trust organisations place in patch timelines. The trust we've placed in AI systems to follow instructions.

The organisations that navigate 2026 well won't be the ones with the most tools. They'll be the ones that ruthlessly audit their assumptions — about what "secure" means, about how long they have to respond, and about what happens when the tools they deploy to defend themselves become the attack surface.

That audit is overdue.
