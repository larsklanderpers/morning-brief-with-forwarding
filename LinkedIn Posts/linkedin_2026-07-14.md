An attacker exploited a critical Adobe ColdFusion flaw within minutes of the technical analysis going public.

Not days. Not hours. Minutes.

Sit with that for a second, because it breaks most of the assumptions your security governance is built on.

This week gave us three more data points on the same curve. Researchers documented what looks like the first ransomware operation run almost entirely by an autonomous AI agent — it exploited a Langflow vulnerability, did its own reconnaissance, stole its own credentials, and encrypted the environment without a human driving. Sygnia published an investigation where AI-assisted attackers went from initial AWS access to full environmental compromise in roughly 72 hours. And the Five Eyes intelligence agencies issued a joint warning that AI is transforming cyber risk "in months, not years."

Meanwhile, most organisations are still defending on a calendar.

Quarterly patch cycles. Annual third-party questionnaires. Change advisory boards that meet on Thursdays. These processes were designed for an era when exploitation took weeks and attackers were constrained by headcount. Both constraints are gone.

The FortiBleed campaign makes the point from another angle: roughly 74,000 stolen FortiGate credentials are circulating, now directly feeding INC and Lynx ransomware deployments. The security appliance itself — the thing you bought to be the perimeter — has become the preferred way in. When the attacker logs in with valid credentials harvested from your firewall, your calendar-based review will find the breach at the next scheduled meeting. The attacker needed an afternoon.

And it's not just the big end of town. Australia's cyber agency issued a critical alert about mass exploitation of website CMS platforms — WordPress, Joomla, Craft — with many small and mid-sized businesses already compromised. The economics of AI-driven attacks mean targets that were once too small to bother with are now profitable at scale. If you've been assuming obscurity is a control, that assumption just expired.

So what actually changes? Three things I'd put in front of any board this quarter:

First, pre-approved emergency patching authority. If exploitation happens in minutes, the decision to patch cannot wait for a committee. Define the trigger conditions now, rehearse them, and give someone the authority to act.

Second, re-time third-party risk. The Conduent breach has now grown past 62 million affected individuals, and Deutsche Bank spent the week explaining a vendor's breach. Annual questionnaires audit history. Contractual notification SLAs and concentration-risk mapping shape the future.

Third, measure your clock speed. Not your maturity score — your speed. How long from advisory to patch on edge devices? How long from anomalous login to containment? Those two numbers tell you more about your survivability than any framework assessment.

The uncomfortable truth of this moment: the attacker's cycle time is now measured in minutes and the defender's in quarters. Every governance process running slower than the threat is no longer a control — it's the gap.

The organisations that come through the next two years intact won't be the ones with the biggest security budgets. They'll be the ones that re-timed their defences to machine speed while their competitors were still scheduling the meeting to discuss it.

What's your organisation's clock speed?
