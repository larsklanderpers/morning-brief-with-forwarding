**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The largest crypto theft on record appears to have happened without anyone stealing a key.

Binance lost about $6.3 billion on 29 October. TRM Labs traced roughly $6.36 billion leaving its wallets across ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes. Binance's early account points to a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, and access to a service container. It says it has found no evidence of a private key leak.

Sit with that for a moment. TRM's assessment is that the attacker gained the ability to make the hot wallet signing system sign transactions of its choosing.

The vault was never opened by force. Somebody just got the clerk to say yes.

These are early findings, and Binance says it is still gathering facts. But the shape of it matches two other stories from the same 24 hours.

On the same day, Polymarket halted trading after a third-party script on its site began draining users. Researchers tie the code to DebugBear, a website performance monitoring firm. Hudson Rock's analysis is the interesting part: the script did not appear to send passwords or keys anywhere. It simply reused the authority of sessions customers had already opened.

A tool installed to measure page load times was sitting inside the logged-in page, with the same powers as Polymarket's own code. Polymarket disclosed a similar third-party script compromise in June.

Then there is the PHP2Shell chain. CISA says a Linux kernel bug called Fragnesia can be chained with the PHP flaws to run commands as root. Security firm Dream describes a campaign that used that chain to reach a firewall management server, which held the configurations and credentials of every firewall the organisation ran. One foothold, and the attacker was holding the console. Dream is a security vendor and that account is its own, but CISA's listing says the same flaws can be chained.

Put the three together.

We have spent twenty years teaching organisations to protect credentials: passwords, keys, tokens. That work mattered, and it should continue. But the attacker's question has shifted. Instead of "how do I steal the secret?" it is now "which system already holds the secret, and will do what it is told?"

Signing services. Management consoles. Logged-in browser sessions. CI runners. AI agents acting on someone's behalf.

Each of them is a machine built to act on trust. Each of them produces perfectly valid logs when it is abused, because every action it takes is one it was authorised to take. A signed transaction is signed. A session is a session. Your monitoring sees business as usual right up until the money has moved.

This is why I think credential theft is becoming the wrong frame for boards. The better question is about delegated authority: what can each of our systems do on request, who is allowed to ask, and how would we know if the asker had changed?

Three things worth doing this month:

1. List every service that can move value or change configuration on a request. Treat that list as your crown jewels, ahead of the secrets it holds.
2. Put a limit or a second approval in front of those services, enforced somewhere the caller cannot reach. Twenty minutes is not long enough for a human to notice, so the brake has to be automatic.
3. Inventory every third-party script on any page where customers hold value. If you cannot name them all, you do not control that page.

The next big loss may well involve no stolen password at all. The system will have done exactly what it was asked.

The only question is who was asking.
