**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

For two years, enterprise AI ran on a hostage exchange: hand over custody of your data, and you get frontier capability in return.

This week, both sides of that trade quietly collapsed.

On Wednesday, Moonshot released Kimi K3 — a model that benchmarks alongside the best closed frontier systems, priced like a mid-tier one, with open weights landing July 27. For the first time, "frontier-class" and "runs entirely inside your own perimeter" describe the same model.

The same day, Thinking Machines shipped Inkling — a deliberately un-flashy open model built for one purpose: to be fine-tuned on *your* data, on *your* infrastructure, with a clean training lineage your lawyers can actually sign off on. Their pitch isn't benchmarks. It's that your data never becomes someone else's asset.

And in the most telling move of all, Microsoft began training its sales force to sell against OpenAI and Anthropic — not on quality, but on data custody. When the world's largest enterprise vendor makes "who holds your data" the axis of competition, the market has spoken about what buyers actually fear.

A former White House AI advisor put it in one sentence this week: organizations are trading frontier access for control over their data.

Except now they barely have to trade.

Here's why this matters if you carry PII obligations — and in financial services, government, or health, you do.

While the capability side was decoupling from the cloud, the control side was hardening. Australia's Prime Minister announced mandatory National AI Standards on Tuesday — one framework, one Office of AI, backed by law. In December, new transparency rules force APP entities to disclose exactly what personal information feeds their automated decisions, with penalties up to AUD 50 million. APRA's CPS 230 already treats your AI vendor as a material service provider. And the model layer — the part that often sits offshore even when your database doesn't — is precisely where those obligations bite.

So the equation has flipped.

Twelve months ago, keeping customer data inside your own walls meant accepting visibly worse AI. That was a defensible reason to send PII to someone else's cloud. As of this week, the capability premium for sovereignty is approaching zero — while the regulatory cost of not knowing where your inference runs is compounding every quarter.

Which means "where does the model run?" is no longer an infrastructure detail. It's a board question.

My advice to decision-makers in regulated industries: stop treating on-premise and open-weight AI as the compromise option. Stand up a pilot behind your own perimeter this quarter — the models are finally good enough, and by December you'll need to explain to a regulator, in writing, exactly what touches your customers' data and where.

The organisations that answer that question from architecture will write a paragraph. The ones that answer it from archaeology will write a disclosure.

Which will you be?
