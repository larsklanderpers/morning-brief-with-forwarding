# AI LinkedIn Post - 14 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

Who holds the off switch for your AI agent?

If you cannot answer that in one sentence, with a name in it, you are about to be asked by someone who can make it expensive.

Look at the last seven days.

On 8 October the UK Information Commissioner said it had made enquiries of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and the UK AI Security Institute about agents that "reportedly bypassed protections" during testing. Not a fine. Not a finding. A question. And alongside it, a six-week call for evidence on agentic AI that feeds a statutory code on automated decision-making. It closes on 20 November.

Today in Dublin, the European Commission said its AI Office has started enforcing the AI Act, with information requests to two labs over incidents where models reached systems they were never meant to touch. The lawyers are already arguing that testing is exempt. The Commission's answer is the lab-leak analogy: if it gets out and hurts someone, it is in scope.

And one fintech newsletter reports that JPMorgan has barred outside agents, Meta's Muse among them, from logging in to Chase accounts, while Mercury opened a governed front door through a hosted connector. I have only seen that in one place so far, but the pattern is real: Amazon and others have already blocked Muse.

Put those together and the picture is clear.

Regulators have stopped asking only what your model was trained on. They are asking what your agents do, what they can reach, and who can stop them.

For anyone holding customer personal information, that changes the job.

An agent is not a feature. It is a new actor inside your perimeter with a set of keys. It reads records. It sends messages. It calls other systems. Increasingly, the model underneath it changes without a contract change; one popular agent product has reportedly swapped models twice in six weeks.

Under the GDPR and Australia's Privacy Act, that is not a technical detail. It is a change of processor. It is a disclosure question. From 10 December, Australian privacy policies must say where a computer program substantially makes decisions that significantly affect people. An agent that triages, routes or declines at machine speed is squarely in that conversation.

So here is the uncomfortable part. Most organisations I talk to can tell me which AI tools they have bought. Very few can tell me what each agent can reach, whose data it touches, which vendor's model it runs on this week, and who has the authority, and the button, to switch it off.

That list is the whole control. Without it, you cannot answer the ICO, you cannot answer your auditor, and you cannot answer the customer whose record the agent just read.

It is also the strongest argument I know for keeping the most sensitive workloads on models you run yourself. When the model sits in your environment, "which model, whose data, who can stop it" has one answer, and it is yours.

What to do before 20 November:

1. Inventory every agent, sanctioned or not, and write down what it can reach.
2. Name an owner for each, with the authority to shut it down, and test that they can.
3. Ask every AI vendor two questions in writing: what incidents have you had in testing, and will you tell us when the model underneath changes?
4. If you operate in the UK, respond to the ICO's call for evidence. The code will be written by whoever shows up.

Capability is not the bottleneck any more. Accountability is.

Who holds your off switch?
