The watchtower just became the target.

CISA confirmed this week that CVE-2026-20253 — a critical unauthenticated file-write vulnerability in Splunk Enterprise — is being actively exploited in the wild. Days after public disclosure. Federal agencies have until Sunday to patch.

Splunk is the SIEM. It's the platform security teams use to detect everything else. When attackers go for your logging infrastructure first, they're not just stealing data — they're blinding your detection capability before the real operation begins.

This is the new playbook, and it's playing out across multiple fronts simultaneously.

GitHub confirmed that TeamPCP, a supply-chain threat actor, compromised an employee's developer device via a malicious Visual Studio Code extension and exfiltrated 3,800+ internal repositories. Not a phishing email. Not a malware payload. A VS Code extension — the kind developers install without a second thought dozens of times a year.

Meanwhile, Kimsuky (North Korean state-sponsored) is now conducting persistent access operations using legitimate VS Code tunnels as covert command-and-control infrastructure. Traffic that looks identical to normal developer activity. Network-based detection will miss it entirely.

The pattern is deliberate: attack the defender's tools, blind the defender's visibility, then move at will.

And the velocity is accelerating in ways that should concern every board and executive team.

FIRST's 2026 CVE forecast now projects 66,000 published vulnerabilities by year-end — driven primarily by AI-assisted automated bug discovery. For context: that's roughly one new vulnerability every eight minutes, around the clock, all year. No human patch management process can keep pace with that cadence. Microsoft's June Patch Tuesday alone dropped 206 vulnerabilities, including a CVSS 9.8 Windows Kernel RCE that requires no authentication and no user interaction. The June release was the largest since 2017.

We are at an inflection point. The organisations that survive this environment are not the ones that patch faster — although that matters. They're the organisations that have fundamentally rethought how they process threat intelligence, how they instrument their own toolchain for anomalies, and how they make the distinction between "AI deployed" and "AI effective."

60% of organisations will be running AI-augmented security platforms by end of this year, according to Gartner. That number sounds reassuring. It isn't. Deploying an AI security tool and having a mature, calibrated, human-in-the-loop AI security posture are very different things. Most organisations will land somewhere in between — with a false sense of coverage that sophisticated attackers will learn to exploit.

Three things decision-makers should act on this week:

**Audit your developer toolchain.** VS Code extensions, IDE plugins, CI/CD integrations — these are now active attack surfaces. If you don't have a vetting and monitoring policy for developer tools, you have an uncontrolled entry point into your source code.

**Treat logging infrastructure as Tier 1 critical.** If your SIEM, your log aggregation, or your threat detection platform gets compromised first, every other security control becomes unreliable. Patch Splunk. Audit access to your detection stack.

**Close the gap between disclosure and remediation.** The Splunk CVE was weaponised in days. That window will keep shrinking as AI accelerates exploit development. Your patch cycle needs to be measured in hours for critical infrastructure, not weeks.

The threat landscape in mid-2026 is not harder — it is fundamentally different. The tools attackers use, the targets they choose, and the speed at which they operate have shifted. Strategy needs to shift with it.
