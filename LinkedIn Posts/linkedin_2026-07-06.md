Your most dangerous system isn't the one holding the most data. It's the one that knows the most about everything else.

I went through this morning's security news looking for the exploit of the day. I found something more useful: a pattern.

Progress Kemp's LoadMaster is being actively exploited through a pre-auth flaw that hands attackers code execution on the appliance. That box is a load balancer. It terminates your encryption and sees every request that crosses it — and it almost certainly isn't running the endpoint agent you spent your budget on.

ServiceNow disclosed a June breach through an unauthenticated access flaw. ServiceNow isn't a database. It's the map of how your company actually runs — who has access to what, which changes got approved, how incidents move. An attacker in there doesn't need to steal much. They've been handed the blueprint.

Adobe pushed maximum-severity patches for ColdFusion again — the platform nobody wants to touch and everybody quietly depends on.

And in the AI column, OWASP now calls prompt injection the fastest-growing attack category on the planet, up 340% year over year, with 88% of organizations reporting AI-agent security incidents. Microsoft demonstrated a path where a single crafted prompt escalates to remote code execution on the host running the agent. An AI agent reads every instruction it encounters as if you sent it. It cannot tell your memo from a stranger's.

Four different stories. One shape.

The highest-value target in a modern organization is no longer the biggest pile of records. It's the layer that aggregates operational context — the balancer that sees all the traffic, the platform that maps all the work, the agent that reads all the inputs and can act on them. Each one concentrates enormous leverage. And each one tends to sit in the blind spot of the controls we built for laptops and user accounts.

The attack economy has already noticed. This week also brought Avalon — a single malware kit that fuses credential theft, lateral movement, backup destruction, and ransomware into one operator workflow. And NetNut, a service caught renting out millions of compromised devices so attackers can launder their origin through ordinary residential IPs. The kill chain is collapsing into one afternoon, and attribution is getting cheaper to erase.

So here's the uncomfortable question for anyone accountable for risk:

Are you budgeting security attention by device count, or by concentration of context?

Because those two numbers point at completely different systems. Device count sends your money to the endpoints. Concentration of context sends it to the handful of platforms that, if compromised, hand an adversary the map of everything else — and those are usually filed on the risk register under the most boring word in security: "infrastructure."

Three things worth doing this quarter:

Inventory your aggregators. Not your servers — your context-concentrators. Which single systems, if breached, expose how everything else works? Name them.

Extend real detection to the appliance layer. If your load balancers, firewalls, and hosting panels can't be watched the way your workstations are, that gap is a strategy, and right now it's the attacker's.

For every AI agent you deploy, define the blast radius before you connect it to a tool. Prompt injection is not a patch you're waiting on. It's the current nature of the technology. The control isn't cleaner prompts — it's constraining what a hijacked agent could actually do.

The exploit of the day will change tomorrow. The pattern won't. The organizations that stay resilient are the ones that learn to protect what a system *knows*, not just what it *stores*.

What's your most context-rich system — and when did it last get the security attention it actually warrants?
