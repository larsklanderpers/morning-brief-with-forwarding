Deleting a leaked key is housekeeping. Revoking it is security. One AI company just learned the difference took five months.

LiteLLM, the open-source gateway that sits between thousands of applications and their AI models, said on Friday that last week's supply-chain attack came through a master key it had exposed in May. The team spotted the leak at the time and removed the file. They did not revoke the key. Someone else kept a copy, and about 49,000 downloads of a poisoned release later, every user of that version has been told to assume their credentials are gone.

I keep coming back to that story because it is not really about AI. It is about the gap between knowing something is wrong and actually fixing it, and this week that gap showed up everywhere.

The University of Copenhagen disclosed that attackers reached a test environment holding data on 525,000 people, national ID numbers included. Nobody puts real identity data in a test system because they think it is a good idea. They do it because synthetic data is work, and the test system is "temporary". Then it gets a production-grade breach with development-grade controls.

CISA added both PHP2Shell vulnerabilities to its known-exploited list on Monday. The detail worth reading is not the listing itself. It is the federal directive behind it, which tells agencies to check whether they were compromised before the patch went on. Patching closes the door. It tells you nothing about who walked in while it was open.

And then the part that should change your planning assumptions. South Korean police say a former penetration tester and six associates breached more than 90 organisations, including schools, hospitals and large companies, using ARTEX, an agentic AI attack framework. Days earlier, Microsoft had tied the same framework to a Chinese state group. In one week, a capability went from state budget to small criminal crew.

That is what AI is doing to the attacker's side of the ledger. It is not inventing new attacks. It is collapsing the time and skill between "a weakness exists" and "a weakness is exploited". The unrevoked key, the real data in the test box, the unpatched server, the unexamined patch window: these were always risks. They were tolerable because exploiting them took effort, and effort was scarce.

Effort is no longer scarce.

So here is my challenge for leadership teams this week. Do not ask "what do we know is wrong?" Most organisations have that list, and it is long. Ask a harder question: of the things we already knew about, which ones did we tidy up instead of fixing?

The leaked credential someone "removed from the repo". The production copy someone loaded into staging. The system someone patched without checking whether it had already been compromised. Those are not hypothetical. They are in your history, and an automated attacker can now find them faster than your audit cycle will.

Rotation is cheaper than the incident. It always was. The difference now is that the incident arrives sooner.
