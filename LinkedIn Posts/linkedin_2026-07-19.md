Adobe ColdFusion was exploited in the wild within two hours of its advisory going public last week.

Two hours.

That is not a statistic about one company's poor patch hygiene. It is a statement about the structural limits of human-speed security operations in 2026.

Here's where we are right now. Microsoft patched 622 vulnerabilities in its July Patch Tuesday cycle — five times the May volume, largely driven by AI-accelerated vulnerability discovery. CISA data shows 28.3% of tracked CVEs are now exploited within 24 hours of public disclosure. And a May 2026 incident documented the first fully autonomous post-exploitation attack conducted entirely by an LLM-driven agent, with no human in the loop on the offensive side.

Meanwhile, defenders are still running monthly patch cycles and reading CVE feeds.

Three things happening simultaneously this week clarify what this inflection point looks like in practice.

First: the Fairlife/Coca-Cola ransomware attack halted US dairy production across an entire national supply chain. A $4B revenue brand, completely offline. No ransomware group has claimed responsibility — which either means active negotiations are underway, or we're watching a threat actor with operational security discipline that outpaces public attribution timelines. Either way, a physical manufacturing operation is now a direct casualty of cyber operations moving faster than the defenders who are supposed to protect it.

Second: CISA, NSA, FBI, ACSC and international partners issued a joint advisory confirming Russian state-sponsored actors are actively targeting network perimeter devices across critical infrastructure — communications, energy, financial services, government, healthcare. This is not threat intelligence speculation. This is agencies telling you they have observed active access, likely pre-positioned. The attack is not coming. For some networks, it is already in.

Third: Adobe ColdFusion, Windows VMSwitch (CVSS 9.9), SharePoint (CVSS 9.8, unauthenticated RCE) and Fortinet FortiSandbox all required emergency remediation this week. A researcher dropped a working PoC for an unpatched Windows vulnerability within hours of Patch Tuesday. And Anthropic publicly confirmed its Claude Code AI tool was weaponised in real cyber campaigns.

The throughline across all of this is not complexity. It is speed.

AI has fundamentally changed the time constant of offensive operations. Vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and post-exploitation automation are now running at machine speed. Detection models trained on human-speed attack patterns are structurally obsolete. Monthly patch cycles cannot respond to a 2-hour exploitation window. CVE triaging by human analysts cannot keep pace with 622 new vulnerabilities per month.

The organisations that will survive this environment are not the ones that patch faster. They are the ones that have made an honest architectural decision about which systems can be defended at modern attack velocity — and have built automated detection, isolation, and response capability around the ones that cannot.

That is the conversation boards and executives need to be having right now. Not "are we patching on time?" but "are we even operating in a model that assumes defenders have time?"

The answer, for most organisations, is still yes. That assumption is now wrong.
