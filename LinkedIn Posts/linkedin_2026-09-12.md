Nobody breaks into the vault any more. They steal the key ring.

Look at what attackers went after this week, and notice what is missing. No crown jewels. No core banking platform, no customer database behind three layers of defence. Instead:

The console that manages your firewalls. Cisco Talos says three separate groups exploited flaws in Cisco's Firewall Management Center. One was Qilin ransomware affiliates. Another uses tooling that overlaps with Russia's Sandworm.

The remote-support tool your IT provider uses to reach your machines. CISA added a ConnectWise ScreenConnect flaw to its exploited list on Friday, days after N-able's N-central went the same way.

The code repository where your secrets live. A maximum-severity GitLab file-read flaw drew probes within hours of disclosure and was on CISA's exploited list the same day.

The marketing platform that emails your customers. Attackers got into 120 accounts at Brevo and used Trezor's own newsletter list to send a fake security warning to roughly 347,000 people.

The ID scanner at the front door. IDScan.net has confirmed unauthorised access to names and licence numbers. A dark-web seller claims 153 million scanned licences. That number is the seller's, not the company's, and should be read that way.

Every one of these is an intermediary. None of them is the thing you think you are protecting. Each one already holds permission to act on your behalf, which is exactly why it is worth more to an attacker than the asset sitting behind it.

The club and gaming sector knows this one by heart. In 2024 the Outabox leak exposed licence sign-in data from patrons of at least 17 NSW and ACT venues. The venues did nothing exotic. They trusted a vendor with a copy of what they scanned.

Now add the accelerant. Blackpoint Cyber and GreyNoise describe one crew running hundreds of AI agents, built on OpenAI Codex and a DeepSeek model, against PaperCut print servers: 440-plus instances, 395 organisations, 48 countries, mostly schools and universities. Those are two detection vendors' own counts, and they say they cannot yet confirm the goal. The direction is still clear. Finding the forgotten intermediary used to take a skilled team. Now it takes a budget.

So here is the uncomfortable question for boards: can you list every system, vendor and agent that can sign in, send, pay or change configuration in your organisation's name?

Most cannot. The list is spread across IT, marketing, facilities and procurement, and nobody owns all of it. That gap is exactly where the attackers are working.

My view: stop ranking systems by what they contain and start ranking them by what they can do. Anything that can act in your name is tier zero. It gets MFA, logging, a named owner, a contract clause on breach notice and data deletion, and a line on the risk register next to the core platforms.

If a system would let a stranger impersonate you, it deserves the same protection as the thing a stranger would steal.

The vault is fine. Check who holds the keys.
