Nobody broke into the Azure accounts. They logged in.

A threat actor is currently selling 3.6 million employee records from Fortune 500 companies' Azure infrastructure — not because Microsoft had a vulnerability, not because there was a sophisticated zero-day. Because someone had valid credentials. The attacker authenticated. The system said welcome.

That story dropped yesterday. Today we learned that 1.2 million Heights Finance customers had their data exposed via a compromised third-party cloud platform. And that the French tax authority lost 678,000 records to an attacker who got into their systems through methods that, once again, didn't require breaking anything.

This is the pattern. And we need to stop treating it as a run of bad luck.

**The perimeter assumption is dead. Most security spend doesn't reflect that yet.**

The mental model that shaped a decade of security investment goes roughly like this: build a strong wall, keep the bad guys out, and what's inside the wall is safe. Firewalls. VPNs. Email gateways. Endpoint AV. All of it oriented toward the outside threat.

Attackers figured out years ago that the wall isn't the interesting problem. The interesting problem is the keys. Credential theft, phishing, compromised third parties, OAuth grants to shadow AI apps that nobody in IT approved — these aren't "getting past the wall." They're coming through the front door.

The Clop ransomware group underlines the point from a different angle. Their new tooling isn't spray-and-pray. They built a custom Java web shell specifically for PTC Windchill and FlexPLM — industrial product lifecycle management software used across manufacturing, aerospace and defence supply chains. Philips and GE are now investigating whether they were hit. This is targeted, patient, industrially-specific. And the entry vector was a software platform those companies trusted.

**Three things that actually change the calculus:**

Credential hygiene isn't a compliance checkbox — it's your primary control against the majority of attacks happening right now. MFA enforcement, conditional access policies, and privileged access workstations aren't optional infrastructure. They are the wall.

Vendor security posture needs teeth. The Heights Finance incident isn't an anomaly — it's the third-party cloud risk that every CISO has been warning their board about for three years. Questionnaires aren't assessments. Contractual security SLAs with audit rights are the minimum.

Assume the attacker is already inside. Not because you've failed — because the boundary between "outside" and "inside" no longer exists in a way that's operationally meaningful. Detection and response investment, behavioural monitoring, and privilege minimisation matter more than adding another layer to the perimeter stack.

One more thing worth watching: DeadLock ransomware is now routing victim communications through Polygon smart contracts. When law enforcement takes down their infrastructure, they restore contact through the blockchain. The takedown playbook doesn't reach this. The implication is that the only tools that work against these groups at scale are financial disruption — crypto tracing, sanctions, cutting off the affiliate payment rails — not infrastructure seizure.

The threat actors have adapted. The question is whether our governance, our investment priorities, and our board-level conversations have caught up.
