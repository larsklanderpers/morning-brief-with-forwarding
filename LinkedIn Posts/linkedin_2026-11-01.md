A professional poker player asked an AI agent to review some statistics. It found remote-access software on his machine that he had never installed.

WIRED's account of what followed should be read by anyone who runs a business on someone else's software.

An attacker reportedly compromised the update system of a popular poker helper app. Through that foothold, they reached the developer of a second app, and got malicious code into a signed release. The malware checked usernames against a target list. If you were not on the list, it behaved like normal software. If you were, it opened your screen to the person across the virtual table.

WIRED's sources estimate $1.5 million to $4 million was taken. That range is unverified, and the accused player has not been charged. But look at the shape of it. Nobody hacked a poker site. Nobody broke the game. The attacker went one vendor to the side.

I build technology for club and casino gaming, so that story landed close to home. It would be a mistake to treat it as a gambling story, though, because it arrived in the same week as three others with the same shape.

Northflank, a cloud hosting platform, told customers on 30 October: "Assume all environment variables have been decrypted." Customers then reported their AI API keys being used by someone else. Wiz, which sells cloud security and raised the alert, counts nine AI developer platforms breached in October. In two cases it studied, stolen keys were tested within 30 minutes and later ran up more than an estimated $100,000 in usage, with the keys traced to resellers offering discount AI access.

Amadeus, the reservation technology behind a long list of airlines, has now produced notices from Japan Airlines, Cathay Pacific and Malaysia Airlines, covering access reported from August 2023 to September 2026. Amadeus has not said how many people are affected. So each airline reports its slice, in its own units, and nobody outside can add it up.

And in Pennsylvania, Lehigh Valley Health Network paid $800,000 to the US health regulator over a 2023 attack that reportedly reached systems of a practice it had acquired. The class action reportedly settled for $65 million. You buy the business, and on the same day you buy its IT.

Four sectors. One pattern. Each organisation outsourced a function and quietly assumed the risk had moved with it.

It did not move. The customer gave their data to the brand. The name on the breach notice is yours, and so is the regulator's letter.

What has changed in 2026 is the economics on the other side. Holders of other people's secrets are now the target in their own right, because a single platform pays out thousands of times over, and there is now a market that turns stolen keys into cash within days. Attackers do not need to get past your controls if your supplier's environment variables hold the answer.

So here is the exercise I would give any leadership team this week. List what your suppliers hold on your behalf: keys, tokens, customer records, update rights on your machines. Then ask one question of each entry.

If they called tomorrow and said "assume it is gone", how long would it take you to make it worthless?

Keys you can rotate in an hour are an inconvenience. Keys nobody can find are a crisis. Customer data you can never rotate is a reason to hold less of it, and to think hard about where it sits.

Vendor questionnaires will not answer that. A rehearsal will.
