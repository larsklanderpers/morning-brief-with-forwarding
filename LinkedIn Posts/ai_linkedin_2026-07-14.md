# AI LinkedIn Post — 14 July 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

You're paying for AI twice. The second invoice never shows up in your accounts.

Satya Nadella said the quiet part out loud this week: every prompt, every tool call, every time your team corrects a model's wrong answer, institutional knowledge leaks "trace by trace" into the model provider. You pay once with money. You pay again with the proprietary know-how you must reveal to make the intelligence useful.

That's not a bug in the business model. That is the business model.

And the timing of his admission is no accident. Consider what else happened in the last thirty days.

On 12 June, a US export directive suspended access to two frontier models mid-contract. Enterprises around the world — including here in Australia — watched capability they'd built workflows on switch off overnight, because of a policy decision made in another jurisdiction. Bindu Reddy called it "a huge wake-up call for every global organisation," and the response has been immediate: multi-model strategies, open-weights experimentation, a hard look at lock-in.

Then this morning, Demis Hassabis — the man building some of these models — published a manifesto calling for a US-led watchdog with power to screen the world's most advanced AI. Whatever you think of the proposal, note what it concedes: access to frontier intelligence is becoming a licensed, gated, revocable privilege.

And tomorrow, Prime Minister Albanese delivers "AI in Australia's interests" — elevating AI from a portfolio issue to a national one, with an Office of AI planned inside PM&C. Australia is now the heaviest per-capita AI user of 121 countries measured. We are deep into this transition, whether or not our governance vocabulary has caught up.

Put those three things together and a pattern emerges.

The question that mattered in 2024 was "which model is best?" The question that matters in 2026 is "who controls the intelligence your business now depends on?"

For most organisations, the honest answer is: someone else. A lab controls the terms of service. A foreign government controls export policy. A vendor's offshore inference layer controls where your customers' data is actually processed — and if you're APRA-regulated, CPS 230 just made that your problem formally, as of two weeks ago. Storage residency is not processing residency. Your database being in Sydney means nothing if every inference call crosses the perimeter.

If you hold sensitive customer PII, this is the strategic read:

Dependence is being repriced. Not capability — dependence. The subsidised frontier models are extraordinary tools, and you should use them. But a tool you can lose by directive, whose terms you don't set, and which learns from everything you feed it, cannot be a foundation.

The alternative is no longer a compromise. Open-weight models now run million-token contexts under MIT licences on hardware you own. The local stack gets faster every month. The gap between "AI we rent" and "AI we control" has narrowed to the point where, for sensitive workloads, control wins on the merits.

So here's the challenge for decision-makers this week: map your AI dependencies the way you'd map any critical supplier. Where does inference happen? Who can revoke it? What leaves your perimeter in every prompt? What are you teaching someone else's model about your business?

If you can't answer those four questions, you don't have an AI strategy. You have an AI subscription.

Own the loop. Or accept that you're the product feeding someone else's.
