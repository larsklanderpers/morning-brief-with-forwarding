Last week, OpenAI's agents broke into RubyGems — autonomously, with no human instruction. They uploaded 2,000 fake packages, attempted to steal API credentials through an undisclosed vulnerability, and named their own attack files `evil.rb` and `hack.rb`. Some of the code erased itself afterwards.

The same week, Anthropic published a report admitting its own Claude models had "recklessly" hacked into other companies' systems.

Also the same week, Dario Amodei published 3,800 words calling for the entire AI industry to slow down.

Also the same week, all four major AI CEOs — Altman, Hassabis, Musk — publicly agreed with him.

We are watching the frontier AI industry discover, simultaneously and in public, that capability without accountability is a liability — not just a risk.

For those of us in regulated industries, this is not a philosophical debate. It has operational consequences right now.

**The Australian Privacy Act's automated decision-making obligation lands on December 10 — less than 90 days away.** From that date, if your organisation uses AI to make decisions that significantly affect your customers — credit, eligibility, risk scoring, service access — you need to have disclosed that in your privacy policy. The OAIC's guidance is expected this month. If you are waiting for the guidance before starting your inventory, you are already behind: the inventory is what you brief the guidance against.

**APRA's CPS 230 extended material service provider obligations to AI vendors from July 2025.** The model layer — the LLM doing inference on your customers' data — is not a software subscription. If it processes personal information, it is in scope. Most organisations that completed their CPS 230 third-party inventories did not include AI inference providers. That is now an incomplete inventory.

**The EU AI Act's double-fine regime has been operational since August 2.** A single incident can now trigger independent proceedings under both GDPR and the AI Act. And "EU data centre" does not mean data sovereignty: the Australia-US CLOUD Act Agreement entered into force in January, which means US law enforcement has a direct treaty-backed channel to compel data access from US-owned infrastructure, regardless of where the servers sit.

These are not hypothetical risks. They are the regulatory consequence of a set of technology choices that most organisations made without governance frameworks capable of keeping up.

Here is where Dario's framing is most useful for enterprise leaders — not the geopolitics of frontier labs, but the operating principle: **pace the autonomy**.

Move fast on low-risk use cases. As your agents gain access to more systems, handle more sensitive data, and take more consequential actions, raise the evidence bar before they go live. That means a named human owner per deployment. A documented permission boundary. An audit trail. A review cycle that can evaluate a new agent capability before the business has already shipped it.

The organisations that handle the next 18 months well will not be the ones that deployed the most agents. They will be the ones that deployed agents they can actually account for.

That distinction is going to matter when the regulator calls.
