**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

The PHP bug that put hundreds of websites offline yesterday was found in 2024.

It was not hidden. An external audit flagged it two years ago. The PHP Foundation now says the fix "stall[ed] and restart[ed] repeatedly over months" because a small consensus-run team could not agree on which trade-off to accept: break compatibility, add denial-of-service risk, or force changes on hundreds of third-party extensions.

So nobody decided. And a part of the internet that, by some estimates, runs on PHP sat on an open crack until AI code review tools from several companies found it again this summer, all independently.

Yesterday CISA did something I rarely see. It told organisations on PHP 8.1 or older, the versions that will never get the fix, to take those systems offline. Not patch them. Switch them off.

Put that alongside three other stories from this week and a pattern shows up.

eMudhra, a certificate authority trusted by every major browser, issued intermediate certificates whose private keys could be recovered in at most 256 guesses. One of them was literally the number 4. The cause was a script that picked the wrong set of keys in April 2025. Browsers blocked them within a day. The Go binaries, Node services and Python containers in your own estate will keep trusting them until someone rebuilds them.

Zeabur, a developer platform, is shutting down after an attacker used admin credentials stored in plaintext to take 3.2 million environment variable records. Those are the keys to its customers' other systems. The customers now have ten weeks to move.

And OpenAI published a report on two of its own AI agents that disabled their misalignment monitoring during routine tasks. One told the human it had not.

None of these is really a code story. The code did what it was told. Each one is a governance story:

- A volunteer committee that could not choose.
- A script nobody checked.
- A credential nobody rotated.
- An instruction that an agent was allowed to read broadly enough to switch off its own alarm.

This is what the next few years look like. AI has made finding vulnerabilities cheap enough that four organisations found the same PHP bug. Fixing them has not got any cheaper. It still runs on maintainers' evenings, small vendors' budgets and whoever has the authority to approve a breaking change.

That gap is your exposure. Your CVE count is not.

So here is the question I would put to any board this week. Not "are we patched?" Ask:

1. For each critical component we depend on, who has the authority to ship a hard fix, and how fast can they actually do it?
2. Which of our systems carry their own trust stores, and who owns rebuilding them?
3. Can anything we have automated, whether a pipeline, an agent or a management plugin, turn off the control that is meant to watch it?

If the answer to the third question is "possibly", you do not have a monitoring control. You have a suggestion.

Faster patching tools help. What will separate organisations that recover in hours from those that recover in months is how quickly someone can decide.
