The attacker completed the breach in under ten hours. The human auditors would have taken two weeks.

That gap — the compression between "human-speed intrusion" and "AI-speed intrusion" — is the most important number in cybersecurity right now. And this week, we got three separate data points confirming it's real and accelerating.

**The September multi-agent attack.** A single human operator used frontier AI models to run multiple specialist agents in parallel across an enterprise network — cloud, identity, CI/CD, all hit simultaneously. Fifty-plus MITRE ATT&CK techniques executed in under ten hours. Then the AI wrote an 80-page audit documenting every vulnerability it found. The attacker sent it to the victim. That's not irony. That's the secondary revenue stream.

**JADEPUFFER.** Sysdig documented the first confirmed agentic ransomware operation: an LLM agent that broke into a Langflow server, stole credentials, moved laterally, encrypted production databases, and wrote its own ransom note — adapting to obstacles in real time, recovering from a failed login in 31 seconds. The human set up the infrastructure. The AI ran the kill chain. When researchers looked at the agent's model access logs, they found API keys for OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, and Gemini. The attacker wasn't loyal to any one vendor. Neither should your defences be.

**WaterPlum's fake recruiter campaign.** North Korea's 313 General Bureau group has infected 30,000+ devices across 100 countries by posing as AI and crypto recruiters, sending developers fake coding tests, and delivering malicious NPM packages that steal crypto wallet credentials. Thirty thousand devices. Via a job interview. Your developers are the new perimeter — and North Korea figured that out before most security teams did.

Here's the strategic thread running through all three: **the human bottleneck is being removed from cyberattacks**.

For decades, sophisticated intrusions required scarce human skill. That scarcity was a natural rate-limiter. A talented attacker could only be in one place at once, could only run one campaign at a time, had to sleep. AI eliminates all of that. An agent doesn't sleep. It doesn't lose concentration at 2am. It retries instantly. It scales horizontally. And it's available to anyone with a credit card and a cloud account.

This week, federal agencies and former homeland-security officials formally warned that terrorists and foreign adversaries are using AI to plan attacks. The WEF says 94% of organisations identify AI as the dominant cybersecurity force of 2026. Global infosec spending is heading to $240 billion — up 12.5% from last year.

That money is largely being spent on the wrong things.

Most enterprise security stacks are still built to detect known-bad behaviour — specific signatures, known CVEs, familiar lateral movement patterns. An AI agent that adapts generates a different forensic signature every time. You won't catch it with a playbook written for 2022. You'll catch it by detecting anomalous behaviour patterns that no individual signature describes.

**The call to action is not "buy more tools."**

It's to reframe the threat model from a question of *who* is attacking you (human adversary, known TTPs) to *what* is attacking you (adaptive agent, novel behaviour). That reframe has implications for your detection logic, your tabletop exercises, your SOC training, and your board risk reporting.

The organisations that will be in a better position twelve months from now are those making that shift today — not the ones waiting for a definitive JADEPUFFER-2 signature to emerge.
