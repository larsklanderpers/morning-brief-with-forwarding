# LinkedIn Post - 11 October 2026
**Status:** Draft (review before posting)

---

The most dangerous sentence in security this week was four words long: "no evidence of exploitation."

SonicWall put it in an advisory on 6 October for a CVSS 10.0 flaw in its SMA 1000 remote access appliances. Within 24 hours, honeypot data from one threat intelligence firm showed attackers trying it. More than 400 of those appliances sit on the open internet.

I don't think SonicWall lied. I think the sentence meant exactly what it said: the vendor had not seen exploitation yet. The problem is how the rest of us read it. We heard "you have time."

That gap, between what a trusted party actually told us and what we chose to hear, ran through almost every story I read this week.

Take Palo Alto GlobalProtect. Ransomware affiliates are reportedly using an authentication bypass to log in as ordinary VPN users. Once someone arrives as a valid user, every control downstream is making a judgement call on a lie. Patching closes the door. It does not tell you who already walked through it.

Then the one that should make every CISO pause. The FBI arrested a founder of a ransomware negotiation firm on a federal extortion charge tied to the ShinyHunters investigation. It is an allegation, not a verdict. But think about where a negotiator sits: in the room during your worst week, holding your board's risk appetite, your insurer's limits and your attacker's demands at the same time. There is no licensing regime for that role. Most of us inherit the negotiator from an insurance panel and never ask a question.

And in South Korea, where an operator used an open-source agentic pentest tool, and reportedly Claude agents, to breach banks and expose tens of thousands of customers, local press reports that the sector's own security institute had briefed bank staff on AI hacking techniques weeks earlier. A lawmaker now says the audit will focus on why known internet-facing flaws were left open. The warning was given. The trust was placed in "someone will act on it."

Four stories, one pattern. None of these is a new technique. Each is a party we relied on to tell us the truth, or to act on it: a vendor advisory, an identity gateway, a crisis adviser, a sector warning.

Here is the shift I'd push for in any regulated business, and it is cheap.

Write down which third-party statements you are treating as controls. "Vendor says not exploited." "Insurer picks the negotiator." "The VPN authenticated them." "We attended the briefing." Then, next to each, write how you would know if it was wrong, and how fast.

If the answer is "we wouldn't," you have found your real exposure. It will not be on any threat feed.

The attackers have automated the boring parts of hacking. The least we can do is stop outsourcing our judgement to sentences we never read closely.
