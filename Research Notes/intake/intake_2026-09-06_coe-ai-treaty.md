# The AI Treaty: A framework for global AI governance (Techplomacy Magazine, 3 Sep 2026)

Author: Marc Rotenberg, founder and executive director, Center for AI and Digital Policy (CAIDP).
**Commercial or institutional interest, named:** CAIDP "has supported the development, adoption,
ratification, and implementation" of this convention (author bio, same page). This is an advocacy
piece by the treaty's most active civil-society backer, not a neutral status report. Its factual
claims were checked against the Council of Europe Treaty Office chart (primary, below) and hold.

## Extracted

- Instrument: Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights,
  Democracy and the Rule of Law, CETS No. 225. Opened for signature Vilnius, 5 Sep 2024. "The
  first legally binding international treaty for artificial intelligence."
- Rotenberg's count: CAIDP counts "more than 45 countries" supporting it when the EU and member
  states are included; the formal record shows 20 state signatures plus the EU, which formally
  approved in May 2026.
- Entry into force needs five ratifications including three CoE member states. "As of August 30,
  2026 ... the threshold for entry into force has not yet been reached."
- Non-European participants in the negotiation: Argentina, Australia, Canada, Costa Rica, Israel,
  Japan, Mexico, Peru, United States, Uruguay, Holy See. Signed: US (Sep 2024), Canada, Japan
  (Feb 2025), Israel, Uruguay (Sep 2025). PACE encouraged non-member accession in June 2026.
- Core obligations: lifecycle coverage; transparency and oversight; accountability; equality and
  non-discrimination; privacy and data protection; remedies and procedural safeguards; risk and
  impact assessment; recognition that some uses may require bans or moratoria ("red lines are now
  the front lines").
- The treaty regulates consequences and responsibilities, not technical architectures. Rotenberg
  argues that is why it may prove durable.
- **Admitted limits.** Applies clearly to public authorities and private actors acting for them;
  for other private actors, governments choose whether to apply it directly or "use other
  appropriate measures." The EU declared it will apply the principles to private actors via the
  EU AI Act. National defence is excluded; national security is qualified. The monitoring
  mechanism "will depend heavily on the seriousness with which governments approach
  implementation."
- Sovereignty argument: "sovereignty is strengthened, not diminished, by international rules";
  the alternative is "private governance by a small number of multinational technology companies
  whose technical decisions become global policy by default."
- Precedents claimed: Convention 108 (data protection) and the 2006 Cybercrime Convention drew
  parties well beyond Europe.
- Endorsements cited: Bengio, Stuart Russell, Virginia Dignum, Kasparov.

## Primary check, 6 Sep 2026 (CoE Treaty Office chart, status as of 06/09/2026)

- Signatures not followed by ratification: **20**. Ratifications or accessions: **1** (European
  Union, signed 05/09/2024, ratified 15/05/2026).
- Not in force. Threshold is five ratifications including three CoE members; there are zero
  member-state ratifications.
- **Australia: neither signed nor ratified**, despite participating in the negotiation. Same for
  Argentina, Costa Rica, Holy See, Mexico, Peru.
- Signatories among CoE members include the UK, Norway, Switzerland, Ukraine, Georgia, Moldova,
  Iceland, Andorra, San Marino, Montenegro, Liechtenstein, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Armenia, North
  Macedonia, Albania. **Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Netherlands and Poland have not signed
  individually**; they sit under the EU's ratification.
- Rotenberg's "20 state signatures in addition to the European Union" matches the chart exactly.
