The cheapest head on your org chart is a junior. The most expensive thing you can lose from that same chart is a manager who has actually done the work -- and you only ever get the second one by keeping and improving the first.

That is the whole piece, and because it's a Saturday I'll tell you up front that it ends somewhere good: the fix costs almost nothing, it's a redesign and not a hiring spree, and you can start it the week you decide to. But we have to walk through the false economy first, because it's the kind that hides in a spreadsheet looking exactly like a win. Also because it's a Saturday, it's probably going to take me a few thousand words to get there.

Here is how it hides. A junior analyst, a junior developer, a junior anything -- the least expensive person doing work an agent now does in a fraction of the time, without a lunch break or a bad Monday. So you don't backfill the seat. You book the saving this quarter, and the P&L applauds. What the P&L can't show you is the invoice that comes due in five years, in a currency it doesn't track: the senior who was supposed to grow out of that seat, and didn't, because the seat was gone.

## The numbers, both directions at once

I want to be honest about the evidence, because this is a topic where people grab the number that flatters their priorities and run full speed. Slowing down to really consider it changes the frame.

The alarming side is real and it is measured. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from ADP payroll records, looked at workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations and found a 16 percent *relative* decline in their employment since late 2022 -- relative meaning against older colleagues doing the same jobs, and controlling for the usual firm-level shocks, so it isn't just "a few companies had a bad year." The qualifier is doing real work: it is not "16 percent lost their jobs." It is that the youngest cohort fell that far behind the people one desk over. Software developers are the sharpest version of it -- 22-to-25-year-old devs are down nearly a fifth from their late-2022 peak, while developers 35 and over kept growing straight through the same stretch. The lab's live dashboard, refreshed July 22, now breaks that same exposed cohort out by gender rather than reporting one pooled number: over the past twelve months, employment in the most AI-exposed occupations contracted at 3.1 percent a year for early-career men and 5.4 percent for early-career women, against continued gains in the least-exposed group. I pulled the current numbers before this went out, as promised -- the pattern hasn't reversed. If anything, the gendered cut reads sharper than the blended ~3.8 percent figure did back in April.

Now the other direction, held just as openly, because it's just as real. The Yale Budget Lab, tracking the aggregate since ChatGPT shipped, finds no meaningful change in unemployment for workers in the most AI-exposed jobs through early 2026. Ramp and Revelio Labs went through more than 21,000 firms and found the heaviest AI adopters *grew* overall headcount about 10 percent in the two years after adopting, and grew entry-level headcount 12 percent -- the companies leaning hardest into the tools were hiring more juniors, not fewer. And Sam Altman, of all people, told the CEO of Commonwealth Bank, at the bank's own AI event in May, that his earlier forecast was wrong: he'd expected far more entry-level white-collar jobs to be gone by now, and they aren't. A chief executive walking back a prediction that pointed toward his own product's inevitability is about as good as evidence gets, precisely because it costs him something to say it.

So which is it? Both, and the fact that both are true is the actual finding. The aggregate holds; the on-ramp narrows. Total employment isn't collapsing -- but the specific rung where a 24-year-old used to climb in is getting thinner and steeper. And that is exactly the situation where deleting the rung is the wrong move and redesigning it is the right one, because the problem was never headcount. It was the ladder.

Which is why the sharpest line on this came from someone with no incentive to be sentimental about junior staff. Matt Garman runs AWS -- and Amazon, its parent, has shed something like 30,000 corporate jobs since last October. Asked about replacing junior developers with AI, he called it "one of the dumbest things I've ever heard" -- and pointed out that juniors are the least expensive people you employ and the ones most fluent in the new tools, so cutting them is bad on cost and bad on capability at the same time. In the same breath, Amazon says it's hiring 11,000 interns and new grads this year. Hold both of those numbers next to each other, because Amazon is holding them too: cutting hard and hiring young at once is not a contradiction. It's a company that has noticed the rung is load-bearing.[^1]

## What the rung actually teaches

What the org chart never captures is the reason this matters beyond nostalgia for paying your dues.

The visible half of a junior job -- draft the memo, reconcile the ledger, write the boilerplate function, summarize the call -- is exactly the half an agent now does well. Fine. Nobody should mourn the boilerplate. But there's an invisible half that only ever accrued *while* you were doing the visible half: the pattern-recognition, the "I've seen this go sideways before," the small dread that makes a good analyst reread a number that's technically valid but somehow wrong. That is tacit judgment, and it is not a document you can hand someone. It is a callus. It forms from contact, on the rung, over time.

PwC put the mechanism in one sentence better than I can: AI is removing the routine work that used to act as the apprenticeship. That is the trap. We're keeping the destination -- we still want seasoned people who can catch a confident machine being wrong -- while quietly demolishing the only staircase that ever led there. And while PwC is the reference for this piece, the truth is that *everybody in the tech side of the industry, at least, already knows this is a problem.* Podcast after podcast is discussing the fact that we are eating our young with our strategy here, as does X post after X post. And nearly every conversation I have with someone in the business is not only their worry about this systemic pull-up of the ladder behind the leaders (often driven by commercial or other non-technical elements) but that, at the same time, they can't find anyone to hire to fill the job. This is not an ivory tower problem; it is a real, on-the-ground issue, and the coming jobpocalypse is not going to be in the loss of these roles but rather in the inability to fill them in 36 months.

If you've been reading this thread, you know the shape of this already. Two weeks ago I argued that certain seats are load-bearing: the person who can explain the system, the one qualified to withhold an approval, the one who sees a boundary the dashboard doesn't draw. Last week's "The Loop Is Load-Bearing" made the same case from the other side -- your human-in-the-loop only works if the human still knows enough to catch the machine. What I skipped past then, and what this piece is for, is the uncomfortable supply-chain question underneath all of it: competence has a supply chain, and the rung being automated is the factory floor. In the archetypes, this is the Grower: the function by which an organization manufactures its own future seniors. Nobody applies for it. Starve it and you don't feel it this year. You feel it the year you go looking for someone who can stand on the wall, and find you stopped making them in 2026. And not just you -- everyone.

## The redesign, which is the actual good news

Keep the role. Change what fills it. That's the move, and it's cheaper than the layoff.

**Stop hiring juniors to do the task. Hire them to catch the machine doing it.** The cleanest template comes from accounting, of all the unglamorous places. Edmondson and Chamorro-Premuzic -- she's the Harvard professor who put "psychological safety" into the management vocabulary -- laid it out in HBR: let the AI reconcile the transactions, which it's good at, and move the junior staff onto anomaly detection, fraud investigation, and actually talking to the client. Same people, same seats, radically better job. The machine does the first draft; the human's job becomes deciding whether the first draft is any good. In the archetype language I've been using all month, that's the Editor in its natal form -- knowing what "right" looks like well enough to catch what's off -- and it's the exact skill every senior seat above them will eventually demand.

**Measure them on catches, not throughput.** This is the part that requires an actual decision from you, because every existing metric pushes the other way. If you grade a junior on volume, you've told them to trust the agent and move on, and you've trained precisely the wrong instinct. Grade them on what they caught -- the number that was wrong, the assumption that was buried, the output that looked clean and wasn't -- and you're paying them to develop the judgment you'll promote them for later. The metric is the curriculum. Pick it with attention, care, and the value it represents to your future. 

**Rotate them through the seats you already named load-bearing.** You spent the last two weeks, if you were reading, identifying the human seats that can't go empty -- the approver, the boundary-seer, the explainer. Those seats are also a rotation. Put a junior next to the qualified approver for a quarter, next to the person who reads model output for what it isn't saying, next to whoever has to explain the system to an auditor. Call it shadowing if you like, but it's the apprenticeship itself, rebuilt deliberately, in the one place the old one used to happen by accident.

I am a senior technical executive. As senior as it gets, in the context of my organization. No one who is in my role and no one who observes my role would argue that I should be troubleshooting problems. And yet, I still do; and not because the people who work for me aren't trained, but because they don't understand the shapes of problems well enough to solve them when they get really twisty. They haven't had the learned experience of being in a codebase, and all of its many iterations, for more than a decade, and because we're constantly running, we can only train them so fast. Changing the apprenticeship -- teaching them not *what* to do but *how* to do it, which is historically something that happens as people are moving from Level 2 to Level 3 -- so that they are learning the how and why not just the what, is the only thing that's keeping our rungs populated. Our Level 1s today know more about the shape of our stack in the first 90 days than they used to in the first two years. Because they need to, for so very many reasons, but mostly because the best way to keep the rungs populated is to teach people who haven't climbed it yet that the ladder has real value, and to make that stick: keep the value in the rungs because the people on them will know if you aren't attaching it to their work.

None of this is a hiring spree or a heroic budget ask. It's the same headcount, pointed at a different job description and a different scorecard. The redesign is close to free. The deletion is the expensive option; it just sends the bill to a version of your company that can't answer the invoice yet.

## The rung is where your load-bearing humans come from

On Monday I asked where the two-to-five humans in everyone's shiny agent-factory ratio were supposed to come from -- the survivors who supervise the fifty agents, correct them, and catch them when they're confidently wrong. This is the answer, and it isn't a mystery. You grow them. On purpose. *With* purpose. Starting on the rung you were about to automate away.

The single most valuable skill of the next decade is going to be catching a confident machine being wrong, and there is exactly one place a person learns it: doing the work close enough to feel when the work is off. Cut that rung and you save a rounding error this quarter and lose your whole bench by the time you need it. Keep it, redesign it, and you're not running a cost center -- you're running the only factory that makes the people we'll all depend on to navigate this mess. That's the good news, and on a Saturday I'll take it: the fix is already in your building, wearing a lanyard that says intern.
