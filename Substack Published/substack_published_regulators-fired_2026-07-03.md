On Monday this week, Commerce Secretary Lutnick signed a letter giving Mythos 5 back to roughly 100 approved companies. He can revoke it at any time. The criteria for being on the list are unpublished. Fable 5 is still dark (update: Fable 5 was made available in even-more-limited-fashion as of July 1).

That's what a resolution looks like when you don't have a regulatory framework.

On Sunday, the Supreme Court handed down a decision that makes it structurally harder for the US to ever build one.

That's not how the decision in *Trump v. Slaughter* was reported. Most coverage focused on whether the president can fire commissioners of the Federal Trade Commission. He can, the court said 6-3, overturning 91 years of settled law. But the AI story buried inside that ruling is the one your board needs to understand -- even if your board is 100% Australian and couldn't particularly care less about American case law.

## 1935 to 2026 in One Paragraph

In 1935, the Supreme Court decided *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*. The unanimous ruling established something that seems obvious in hindsight: a president can't fire a regulatory commissioner just because he dislikes their decisions. That protection — for cause removal only — was the entire point of independent agencies. It meant the FTC could regulate Standard Oil's successors without worrying about whether the Oval Office approved. It meant the FCC could set communications policy without being a political organ. It meant regulators could follow the evidence rather than the election cycle.

On June 29, 2026, the court's six-member conservative majority overturned that ruling. Chief Justice Roberts: *"Subordinates who exercise the President's power are subject to removal by him."*

Justice Sotomayor, in dissent: *"The Court gives the President a power unknown even to the English Crown against which the Founders revolted."*

The agencies affected include the FTC, the FCC, the NLRB, the EEOC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission. The Federal Reserve was carved out in a companion case — apparently the thing America decided it couldn't afford to politicise is the interest rate, not worker rights or consumer protection.

I am no finance bro...but it's pretty evident even to the uneducated like me that the Fed carve-out is worth an eyebrow raise — the court drew the line at the one agency that *directly affects* asset prices. Make of that what you will.

## What This Has to Do With AI

Everything.

The FTC was the agency that the Trump administration directed, via executive order, to issue an AI policy statement by March 2026. It did. The FCC is supposed to develop federal AI disclosure standards that would preempt conflicting state laws. The FTC is the primary consumer protection body for AI-related fraud, privacy violations, and deceptive practices.

After *Slaughter*, every one of those agencies serves at the pleasure of the president. Not as a matter of political convention — as a matter of constitutional law. A commissioner who declines to approve a major tech company's AI product because of antitrust concerns can be fired for that reason. One who enforces consumer protection rules the administration dislikes can be gone by Monday -- or Friday night at 5:47, this Administration's apparent preferred time for doing things that they hope will fly under the radar.

This isn't hypothetical. Trump already fired Slaughter for exactly this reason — because her views didn't align with his priorities. The court just said that's fine.

## The Connection No One Is Making

In yesterday's piece I described Charlie Bullock's framing from the AI Daily Brief: *"This ad hoc last-minute licensing regime is bad for companies, the administration, and the public — we desperately need to replace it with actual legislation."*

Here's the problem with Bullock's prescription: the agencies that would write that legislation are now captured.

The stable regulatory framework everyone says they want — transparent standards, published benchmarks, a formal process for model approvals, an appeals mechanism — would have to come from either Congress (which is so somnolent it moves at geological pace on technical topics when it moves at all) or independent agencies with genuine expertise and insulation from political pressure.

Congress has shown no signs of passing comprehensive AI legislation. Candidly, Congress hasn't even shown signs that they want to talk about it like serious people. And the independent agencies that might have developed it now answer directly to the president.

Dean Ball, who described the current AI regulatory situation as rules that are *"secret even to the administration itself,"* recently joined OpenAI after leaving his White House role. He understands better than most that this wasn't just a temporary chaos in a transition period. After *Slaughter*, it becomes structural. The president's ability to reshape any regulatory agency overnight — by firing commissioners who disagree with him — means no AI governance framework can be assumed to survive a change of administration, or even a change of mood.

What's it all mean: well, if I am being cynical, it means that everyone who doesn't have the direct ear of the president or his closest allies is now at the mercy of a capricious "structure" that has the capacity to take whole businesses offline in a limited amount of time, without recourse, without impact. And when I say everyone, I mean *everyone*. Decide a model has national security implications? Don't give it to anyone else. Figure out that a model is really good at hacking and incursions? Don't share it with anyone, even our closest allies, because the value of being able to break into every system they have far outweighs the little things like alliances and hundreds of years of history. This is not a highlighting of risk: this is the way it already is, and an emphasis on the fact that the one person with the capacity to make it change both has no interest in doing so and has proven to be less concerned with doing the right thing than he is the most profitable thing. It's the US as arms provider to the world but selling biplanes to everyone else and keeping the jets for themselves.

## Justice Gorsuch Saw the Problem

The most interesting piece of *Slaughter* is Gorsuch's concurrence. He voted with the majority — the president can fire agency heads — but he couldn't help raising the question the majority didn't want to answer:

*"Would Congress have delegated so much power — including legislative and judicial power — to independent agencies had it known that the President would come to control them? How will Congress respond now? And what, if anything, will this Court do about it?"*

He's asking: if Congress created these agencies on the assumption they'd be independent, and that assumption is now unconstitutional, did Congress really intend to create them at all?

Legal scholar Ilya Somin, writing in the immediate aftermath of the decision, took this further. He argued that at least some agencies whose independence was baked into their statutory design could now be challenged as "inseverable" — if the independence provision is unconstitutional, the entire agency might be too.

We may not just be looking at a politicised FTC. We may be looking at agencies that become legally contestable by any party that wants to challenge their authority.

For AI governance, that means: not only are the regulators now politically captured, but the regulatory frameworks they've built could be challenged in court as founded on an unconstitutional premise. Every rule the FTC has issued on AI, every FCC proceeding on disclosure standards — all of it now sits on shakier ground. Shaky ground that sits in contention to some of the world's richest men - so it's not a stretch to imagine that they will take advantage of the shakiness whenever it suits them.

## What Australia Should Take From This

Australia's equivalent institutions — the OAIC, APRA, the ACCC — operate with genuine independence from the executive. Their commissioners can't be removed because the government of the day dislikes a ruling they've made. That independence isn't bureaucratic inertia. It's the thing that makes their decisions credible and their frameworks stable.

The US just demonstrated what happens when you give that up.

APRA's April letter about frontier AI governance failures landed in a context where Australian regulated entities could at least assume the regulatory landscape wouldn't be remade by a single personnel decision. After *Slaughter*, that assumption is no longer available to US-headquartered entities, or to Australian companies whose AI governance depends on US regulatory stability.

The APRA-regulated entity that depends on offshore frontier AI — which is most of them — now has two unstable dependencies: the AI vendor (who can be turned off by a Friday-night Commerce letter) and the regulatory framework governing that vendor (which can be rewritten by a personnel decision at the FTC or the FCC).

**This is what APRA means by concentration risk.** It just got three layers deeper.
