Nobody gives a new hire the production database on their first morning. Not because you distrust them -- because you have not watched them work yet, and until you have, you are standing exactly where a former US President made famous: trust, but verify.

You give them a read-only login. They spend a week reading code they did not write, sitting in meetings where they understand about half of it (if they are lucky), and asking questions that are occasionally very good. Somewhere around the second week somebody says "give her commit access to the staging branch," and a few weeks after that she is shipping. Nobody wrote that ladder down. Everybody has climbed it, and at a certain point in their career, has probably climbed it multiple times. And if it is not self-evident, this movement up the trust ladder is foundational whether we are discussing software code or booking reservations. 

Then we started hiring agents, and we skipped the entire thing.

## The rung the demos are shot on

Here is what I think happened, and it is not anybody's fault exactly. Every demonstration of an agent you have ever watched is shot on the top rung. The agent reads the ticket, writes the code, runs the tests and opens the pull request. Or it takes the phone call, searches for available rooms, offers the potential guest their choices and books the room for the requested dates. Either way, it posts to the channel to say it is done. That is a good demo. It is also the most privileged configuration the software supports, and it is the first one most people try, because it is the one they were shown. "Privileged" in this context means that the agent executes with the most possible rights -- you are basically giving it free rein from the get-go to do whatever it deems necessary (I believe mostly because we assume it will do it just like we would).

So the question people ask first is *what can this thing do.* Which is the wrong first question, and I say that as somebody who asked it first, too, and only really began to understand the risk of that when it immediately started to go off the rails of what I expected. 

From experience, now, and the fact that there are a whole lot of "best practices" out there in social media land, the first decision is not what an agent is allowed to do. It is what it is allowed to **see**.

Last Monday -- the 17th, not yesterday -- I wrote about the harness at system scale: that the layer you actually own is the tools, the permissions, the sandbox and the deny rule, and that it is smaller than most people think. This is the same argument at desk scale, on a Tuesday, for one person with one agent and an hour to spare. Same idea, much smaller room. Much more achievable endpoint.

## Four rungs, and where each one's mistakes end up

**Read-only.** It can look at everything and change nothing. Almost all of the value of an agent's first week lives on this rung, and almost nobody spends a week here. Point it at a codebase you inherited and ask it what the deployment path is. Point it at a contract and ask what the termination provisions actually say. Point it at last quarter's incidents and ask what they have in common. Every one of those is genuinely useful, and the worst case is that it is wrong and you notice.

**Write, but to a scratch space.** Now it can produce something. In a place where wrong output costs nothing -- a branch nobody merges, a folder nobody reads, a copy of the spreadsheet rather than the spreadsheet. The value of this rung is that you find out what its output actually looks like at volume, which is a different question from whether one sample impressed you. It also, from a purely human perspective, gives you a final review point to make sure that it is right in all the cases you can easily think of, before it goes public in front of your peers or the world.

**Write to the real thing.** Now a mistake is a mistake. This is the rung where you need the habit I wrote about last week -- knowing in advance what would count as the work being right -- because from here on you are the only thing between its output and somebody else's Monday. And I would emphasize that if you can, even this step you should take carefully, and probably do only certain tasks right away, and make sure you are seeing a detailed audit log of what is happening prompted to you on a regular basis, so you do not "set it and forget it" until it becomes a problem.

**Act outward.** Send, pay, post, provision, deploy. The rung where a mistake stops being yours and becomes somebody else's problem, usually a customer's. Very little needs to live here. A surprising amount of software will put you here by default if you accept the setup screen without reading it.

The rungs are not a maturity model and I am not going to draw you a diagram. They are four different sizes of blast radius, and the only real question is whether you know which one you are standing on right now. Most people cannot answer that about the agent they used this morning. Honestly, in practice, they are more like the rings on a tree than they are four different sizes, because the right way to do this is incremental in every stage. You have to remember that while the agent can do and see whatever you tell it, at agent speed, while you can only do and see so much in a limited period to make sure it is right. You have to take it at a speed you are comfortable with in each sub-element of each stage so you do not create exposure for yourself and others just "trying to keep up."

I do this, still, even just with writing (let alone with the coding pipeline). And my colleague, whose work ethic and sensibility often exceed my own, I find, took months before he even allowed agents to *push* to his repos, and write PRs for him, and he still reads them all closely to make sure they are correct. The agent is able to get the whole way to "act outward" when it is appropriate, but each step has an authorization checkpoint. This comes with its own costs, as we have discussed in the past, but until you are comfortable to your satisfaction that it is doing what you say and *no more than what you say*, you should ensure it is working at "you" speed. No one gets to blame the agent later if something is wrong; the human is in the loop as much to take the blame as they are to provide surety, so if you are going to have to wear it, make sure you check the drape pretty carefully.

## Her other rule, the one that does not go on a slide

The clearest version of this practice I have found belongs to Allie K. Miller, who writes and speaks about applied AI for a very large audience and runs a lot of agents in her own work. Her rule, in her own words, is about as plain as advice gets:

> "You're not going to give it email access... start with read-only access and then earn trust, figure out what is working."

*Earn trust.* Not "assess risk," not "conduct an evaluation." The same phrase you would use about a person, and used deliberately.

Her second rule gets quoted far less often, and I think that is because it is not aspirational enough to put on a slide:

> "Sometimes these things don't work... you just kind of have to say, 'Okay, it's broken now. I'll come back to it in an hour.'"

No recovery architecture. No incident process. No framework. She tolerates the breakage, because she has arranged things so that breakage is affordable. That is the whole trick, and it is worth more than any diagram: the reason she can be relaxed about an agent falling over is that she never put it somewhere where falling over is expensive. Now, in the enterprise, I am pretty sure that will not be the case (ever), but you have to *start* there to earn the trust and learn about the problems so you can prepare for the breakage properly.

Which is the optimistic reading of this entire subject, and I think it is the correct one. The reason to start read-only is not that agents are dangerous. It is that starting read-only is what lets you stop worrying about them.

## Write down what has to be true

Here is the one piece of process, and it is a sentence, not a program.

**Before you promote an agent a rung, write down what had to be true for it to earn that.** Not a feeling. Not "it has been good lately." A specific thing you looked at: *it summarized eleven incident reports and I checked four of them line by line and it got all four right.* Then promote it. If you understand what you want well enough, I would go one step further -- write down what it takes to escalate *before you ever implement the agent.* That way you avoid the risk of being fooled by your own (partial) success.

**Then write down what would send it back down.** This is the half most people skip, and it is the half that matters when you are tired. Deciding in advance what a demotion looks like means you will actually do it, instead of explaining to yourself at 6pm on a Thursday why this particular failure was an exception. And demotion is, ultimately, *just* as important as promotion when dealing with agent governance -- and it does not require a performance improvement plan, either.

**And do it per task, not per agent.** The same agent can sit on the top rung for drafting and the bottom rung for anything that touches a customer. We do this with people without thinking about it -- the new analyst who is trusted completely with the model and not at all with the client call. There is no reason to be less nuanced with software than we are with each other.

Note what this is not. Yesterday's piece was about making sure your agents' *instructions* can all be true at once. This is a different axis entirely: not what you told it, but how much of the world it can reach. You can get the instructions perfect and still hand the thing far more reach than the job needs.

## What to do tonight

Pick one agent you actually use. Not a hypothetical -- one you used this week.

Work out what it can currently see and what it can currently change. That will take you about ten minutes and it is very likely to surprise you, because for most people the answer is "whatever the default was." Then ask whether the last three things you actually asked it to do needed that much reach. If they did not, turn something off and see whether you miss it.

That is the whole exercise. You will either discover that you are over-provisioned, which you can fix in about a minute, or you will discover that you are exactly where you should be, which is worth knowing too. Either way you will be able to answer the question that almost nobody can answer right now, which is a good place to be standing on a Tuesday.
