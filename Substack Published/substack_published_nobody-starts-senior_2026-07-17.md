# Nobody Starts Senior

Every airline captain you have ever flown with started as the person who was not allowed to land the plane. That's not a quirk of aviation culture. It's the design. The right seat of a commercial cockpit is one of the most deliberately engineered training environments humans have ever built: a junior pilot sits next to a scarred one for thousands of hours, watching, handling the radios, flying the easy legs, being talked through the hard ones -- accumulating the judgment that no simulator fully delivers. The industry pays an enormous ongoing cost for that seat. And it pays it because of a fact so obvious it sounds stupid when you say it out loud: there is no other way to make a captain.

Airlines have tested this the hard way. Every time the industry leaned on somebody else's pipeline -- waited for the military to train its pilots, assumed the regional carriers would keep feeding the majors -- the arithmetic held for years, right up until it didn't, and then there were airplanes with no one qualified to sit in the left seat and routes that simply stopped flying. The shortage never announced itself the day the pipeline was cut. It arrived a decade later, all at once, with compound interest.

Hold that picture, because the software industry -- and shortly after it, every industry adopting agents -- is currently walking into the cockpit and sawing off the right seat. The reasoning sounds impeccable: *the AI does the junior work now.*

## The fight that's actually about 2029

The strongest practitioner thread I've seen all cycle was a room full of senior engineers -- the people with the least personally at stake -- arguing, hard, that not hiring juniors is a mistake. Not a kindness. A *mistake.* And what struck me reading it was that the argument wasn't sentimental. Nobody was defending juniors as charity, or even as cheap labor. They were defending them as *inventory* -- the only feedstock that has ever turned into a senior engineer.

Because look at the shape of the reasoning on the other side. "AI does the entry-level work now, so we don't need entry-level people." Every clause of that sentence is true. The model writes the CRUD app, grinds the tickets, drafts the tests -- the work we used to hire juniors to do genuinely is being done by agents, faster and cheaper. If a junior is a unit of task-output, the junior is obsolete.

But a junior was never a unit of task-output. That was the *billing code* -- the line item that justified the seat. What the seat actually produced, the whole time, was something else: a senior, five years later. The tickets were never the product. The tickets were the flight hours.

## Now stack this week on top of that

Walk back through the seats I've spent four days describing, and ask one question of each: what kind of human does it take?

The person who can still explain the system -- trace it, defend it, hand it off -- after the agents built most of it. The qualified approver who can look at what an agent proposed and withhold the click, the pharmacist who actually knows pharmacology. The engineer with the gut instinct that a container is a convenience and not a security boundary, who can see the survey line where everyone else sees the fence. Every one of those is a *senior* judgment. Not senior as a title -- senior as a substance: pattern recognition accumulated across years of watching things break, of shipping the mistake and cleaning it up, of being the green person next to the scarred one when the boundary failed.

Now the question this whole week has been circling, the one I've teased three times and owe you an answer on: where do those people come from?

They get grown. There is no other source. Nobody starts senior. You cannot hire your way out of this at the industry level, because every senior you poach was grown in someone else's right seat -- poaching just moves the shortage around, the way airlines poaching from the regionals didn't create one additional captain, it just decided whose routes stopped flying. The only thing that creates the judgment is the pipeline: a green human, next to a scarred one, doing progressively harder work with someone watching. That's the on-ramp. And we are tearing it out at precisely the moment the market is screaming for what it produces.

And the market *is* screaming. Agentic-AI job postings are up something like 280 percent year over year -- tens of thousands of openings, averaging around $190K -- and nearly all of them are for the orchestrator-and-steward end of the spectrum: people qualified to define the work, judge the output, and supervise the agents. Senior judgment, in other words. We have never demanded more of the thing the pipeline produces while simultaneously defunding the pipeline. Meanwhile the new-grad market is the most brutal it has been in a generation. Those two facts are the same fact, ten years apart.

## The Grower is a production function, not a cost line

In the eleven shapes piece two Saturdays ago, I introduced the Grower mostly as one archetype among the others -- the person who builds people. I want to upgrade that claim today, because this week earned it.

The Grower is not a "people person" role you keep because culture. The Grower is the *production function for every other load-bearing seat I've named this week.* The explainer, the qualified approver, the boundary-seer -- those are outputs. The Grower runs the factory. Which means when an organization cuts the junior pipeline to save a cost line, it isn't trimming fat. It's decommissioning the factory that manufactures the one input the agent era provably cannot generate: qualified human judgment. The models are getting better at everything else. They are not getting better at *having been burned.*

This is Monday's wet winter, again, in its slowest and most expensive form. Knock out the wall and nothing happens -- this quarter's output doesn't dip, because the seniors you already have keep judging and the agents keep generating. The feedback is delayed by half a decade. Then the seniors age out, or leave, or get poached by the firm across town that also stopped growing its own -- and there is no bench, and no amount of budget fixes it quickly, because the thing you need takes years to grow *by its nature.* You will be able to buy compute in 2029. You will not be able to buy 2029 seniors in 2029. Those were hired in 2026, or they don't exist. Some things you simply cannot get by paying more money for them at the last minute, and grown humans top that list.

## What to actually do -- because the answer is not "hire juniors and feel virtuous"

The seniors in that thread were clear-eyed about the other half of this, and it's the constructive turn: the old junior role really is gone, and defending it as-was is a losing argument. The tickets went to the agents and they're not coming back. So the move isn't to preserve the old on-ramp. It's to build the new one, deliberately.

**Redesign the junior seat around judgment, not output.** The old apprenticeship worked by accident -- juniors built judgment as a *byproduct* of grinding tickets. Now that the grinding is gone, the judgment has to become the explicit curriculum. Put the junior in the loop the agents run through: have them review agent output before the senior does, predict what the senior will flag, and defend their call. Have them write the spec, watch the agent execute it, and account for the gap. That's the right seat, rebuilt -- flying the easy legs with someone checking.

**Make growing people a named, funded job.** If the Grower is a production function, staff it like one. Somebody senior in your organization should own the pipeline the way somebody owns uptime -- with time carved out, a bench plan, and accountability for whether this year's green hires can pass Wednesday's test in three years. If growing the next generation is everyone's job, it's nobody's.

**Audit your seniority supply chain like you'd audit any other single-source dependency.** You would never let a critical system depend on one vendor with no second source and a five-year lead time. Your qualified-approver bench is exactly that. Count the people who can actually staff the seats this week described, subtract expected attrition, and look at what your current hiring says the number will be in five years. For most organizations right now, that line goes to zero. Nobody decided that. It's just what the spreadsheet does when every individual quarter's decision is locally rational.

Tomorrow is Saturday, and I'll flip this to the lighter, personal version -- because if your organization won't grow you, the tools have never made it easier to grow yourself, and there's a real and cheerful argument that the best on-ramp left might be the one you build alone. Sunday I'll make the whole week add up.

For today, one uncomfortable question for your next leadership meeting: everyone nodding along that AI handles the junior work now -- ask them where the 2031 seniors are coming from. Sit in the silence. That silence is the sound of an industry eating its seed corn and calling it margin.

Nobody starts senior. Somebody has to start them.
