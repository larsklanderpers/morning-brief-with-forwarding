Every week someone asks me two questions (and often more than one someone): firstly, is AI going to take away MY job, as a technologist, and am I worried about the implications? The second question, hot on the heels of the first (sometimes without even waiting for my response) is whether AI is going to take THEIR job. I genuinely believe that it is the wrong question, and it is making smart people miserable.

The right question is quieter and a lot more useful. Not "will my job survive," but "what is my job actually made of -- and which of those pieces is the part worth keeping?" This is not an academic exercise for any of us: the ground is shifting fast, executives and senior managers are making decisions with limited or poor information, and the quiet 3am dread is real. So I might reframe the question even more specifically for each individual asking: "How do I get in front of this wave -- or at least ride it -- instead of being taken under by it?"

Here is the analogy I keep coming back to. Think about a restaurant kitchen before anyone wrote down the concept of a "line." There was just a cook, and the cook did everything: sourced the fish, prepped it, fired it, plated it, ran it out, took the compliment. Then kitchens got busy, and somebody realized the *work* had a shape independent of the *person*. Sourcing is one shape. Prep is another. Fire is another. Expediting -- the person calling the pass, keeping ten tickets coherent -- is a shape all its own. Nobody's job title changed on paper. The work just got unbundled into roles you could see, name, and get good at.

That is happening to every aspect of every business, whether recognized or not, right now. And it's happening at a speed that rivals the industrial revolution's impact on agrarian societies. Most materially for most people who would be reading this or thinking about this kind of thing, this is happening with knowledge work right now. Not in a decade. Not even in a year. This quarter. Running out the clock is really no longer an option because the clock time has compressed for this, too.

## The job title was always a lie we agreed to

My title in my business is CTO. Google summarizes this in two lines: "It is a high-level executive position responsible for managing the technological needs, research, and development of a company. The CTO ensures that the organization's technical strategy aligns perfectly with its broader business goals." For years, my actual work was generally less than the definition -- I spent far more time building product than I did solving future problems. In the last six months, that has flipped entirely on its head, and my job now is to pace what we ship to what customers are ready to absorb, while still ensuring we are delivering the best and brightest we can in that space. In several cases, in the space of days or weeks, I leapfrogged ten years of understanding to a whole new way of doing things. And it has been really, really tough.

Here is the thing the AI-jobs panic keeps missing. A job title was never a real description of work. It was a billing code. "Marketing Manager" tells you which budget line pays them and roughly where they sit in the org chart. It tells you almost nothing about what the work *is*.

On the Claude Code team, the story goes, the product manager writes code. The designer writes code. Even finance writes code. Everyone is nominally a "Member of Technical Staff," which is another way of saying the titles stopped meaning anything and the *dispositions* started meaning everything. What are you actually good at? What shape of work do you reach for when nobody's assigning it?

That is the shift underneath all the noise. When the cost of producing a first draft of anything -- code, a campaign, a contract, a support macro -- collapses toward zero, the value stops living in the doing. It moves to the judgment that surrounds the doing. Which draft was worth making. Which one ships. Who cleans up after. Who keeps the whole thing from flying apart.

So instead of defending a title, collect shapes. Every one of these shapes is a pivot from the way most of us defined work, in some cases (like mine) for decades. But here's the flip side of the compressing clock: as the cost of curiosity trends toward zero, the person who keeps asking questions gets the compounding returns, and gets them early. *Everyone* will build this reflex sooner or later. Starting now isn't about outrunning something -- it's about collecting the upside sooner.

Regardless, shapes. Whether these are all of them, or whether you see different ones, or whether you feel like a different mix applies to your workplace, here are eleven of them that I think cover a lot of ground here.

## The eleven shapes (and which one is you)

Don't read this as a personality quiz. Read it as a menu. Most people are already two or three of these and have never had the language for it. The families matter more than the labels.

**The ones who face the work.** These are the makers. The **Prototyper** invents -- churns out a dozen rough v1s knowing eleven will die, because now that ideas are cheap the value moved to *having* them. The **Builder** takes the one that lived and hardens it into something real: secure, reliable, ready for the public. The **Sweeper** simplifies and unships, keeping speed from turning into sprawl. The **Maintainer** owns the mature thing and keeps it humming at scale. Four dispositions, one instinct: put your hands on the artifact.

**The ones who face the world.** The **Scout** lives outside and drags signal back in -- what's shifting, what competitors are doing, what customers actually said. The **Editor** has taste, and taste is now the scarce resource: when you can generate a hundred prototypes, someone has to decide which three deserve a human's week. The **Grower** takes what's built and grinds it against reality -- conversion, retention, fit. The **Evangelist** gets the market to see the world the way the builder sees it. If you've ever been the person in the meeting who says "yes, but is this the *right* thing to build," congratulations, you're an Editor and you've been underpaid for it.

**The ones who make it cohere.** The **Orchestrator** manages the agents and the people, living at the seams where prototype becomes build becomes growth. The **Conductor** is the specialist version -- running a team of agents that behave like synthetic employees and keeping their handoffs clean. And the **Risk Steward**, who I'll defend harder than the rest in a second.

Read that list again and notice something. Nothing on it is "the AI." The AI is the leverage under every one of these shapes. The shapes are what's left that's *yours*. The shapes protect and define you, and that shape definition -- that skill set, that craftsmanship -- is not something any model release is going to take from you.

## The one move that future-proofs almost anyone

If you take one operational thing from this, take this: **become the maker for your own corner.**

Not a career change. Not a bootcamp. Just the reflex. The back-office person who used to file a ticket and wait three weeks for a one-off expense-report exception now builds the little tool herself in an afternoon, because building finally got cheap enough to be worth her time. Multiply that across service, sales, ops, and support, and the org quietly reshapes itself from the bottom up -- not because anyone announced a transformation, but because the people closest to the friction started removing it themselves.

**Here's the weekend version, if you want one.** Pick the single most annoying repetitive thing in your week -- the report you rebuild every Monday, the email you rewrite forty times, the data you copy between two systems that refuse to talk. Don't optimize it. Hand it to an agent and watch what breaks. What you learn in that hour is not "how to prompt." It's what the actual substance of your job is, once the mechanical part is gone. That leftover -- the judgment, the taste, the coherence -- is the thing worth building a career on.

## In defence of the Risk Steward

One more, because it matters more here than anywhere and it's the one people get wrong.

The **Risk Steward** is not the person who says no. That's the old model -- the gatekeeper, the bottleneck, the human speed bump every fast-moving team learns to route around. The new version is the opposite. When agents push every function's operating speed up sharply, they also shorten the time you have to notice a pile-up before it becomes a wreck. The Risk Steward is the one looking two steps down the track so the train never has to stop. In security we've been arguing for the enabling version of this role for years and losing. AI might finally win the argument for us, because at agent speed, the gatekeeper model simply isn't fast enough to survive.

The compression of the decision clock is exactly why this shape matters. The one looking ahead has always been the one protecting us -- we just didn't know it. The Risk Steward is the chess player looking three moves ahead across everything that touches their area of responsibility, and their job is not to win. It's to make sure the losing moves never get played, because at agent speed your response often needs to happen before anyone else has even recognized there's a problem. And when agents manage agents, one stumble cascades -- so the Risk Steward designs for the stumble before it happens, and makes sure there's a fallback for the inevitable day the orchestrator falls over. That is a fundamentally different job than the gatekeeper's. You still need the compliance function, but by the time a gatekeeper catches a failure, it's usually too late. The Risk Steward's craft is making sure risks are identified, managed, and handled before they become outcomes.

## So, back to the wrong question

Your job title is melting. That is not the threat the headlines make it. It's the billing code losing its grip on the work, which means the work is finally visible -- unbundled into shapes you can choose, name, and get deliberately good at. I know that this is a complete inversion for the typical white-collar worker who knows what job they're getting, gets the job that they're getting, and performs the job that they're getting. They know within a reasonable set of frameworks the definition, responsibilities, and accountability of that job. Changing the way you work, and more importantly changing the way you think about work, is how you make sure that when the billing code loses its grip for good, you're holding the work, not the title.

You are not competing with the agent. You're deciding which shape you want to be while the agent does the part that was never really you. Done right, by the time you are finished, the agent should be doing all the work that makes you feel dead at your job. You should be doing all the work that makes you happy you took that job in the first place. So start now. Pick two shapes. Get great at them. Let the title melt.

Don't ask whether AI takes your job. Ask which of the eleven shapes you'd build a career on if the title vanished tomorrow -- and then go be that on Monday, a week before anyone tells you to. And as importantly, if the shape that you feel you are or that you want to be doesn't map to any path that you can see from your current job, then maybe it's time to look for a new one where you can be that person.
