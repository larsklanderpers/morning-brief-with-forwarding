# Adoption Speed, Control Speed

The model you tested is not the model you are running. That is now a normal operating condition, and the bill for it lands on the operator.

On 23 April 1985 Coca-Cola changed the formula of the most famous drink in the world to better compete with Pepsi. Seventy-nine days later, on 11 July, it brought the original back as Coca-Cola Classic and sold it alongside the new one. The story everyone remembers is the consumer revolt, the phone lines and the protests. The part that rarely gets told is what it did to every restaurant and fountain operator that poured the stuff. They had not asked for a new formula, and then they had two. That meant a second syrup, a second line on the machine, shelf space for a box of product that had never been part of their line, and staff explaining to customers which one was which. Coca-Cola made the decision. The restaurant carried it. Self-serve drinks weren't really a thing at the time; nearly every interaction required explaining new Coke and Classic Coke and a lot of hemming and hawing on the part of everyone involved. This move on Coca-Cola's part has been examined in B-schools for the last forty years, far more deeply than I intend to: the point to consider is that they changed the formula and the people using the product had to retool their fountain machines (or replace them!) with more spigots; the bar guns where I worked had to reprogram the buttons, and in many cases, again, add a new button. And every retail outlet ended up adding another refrigerated cabinet to handle the expanded shelf space need. Coke ended up far better off for it; the restaurants were not. I was pretty young, but my memory is that the numbers were down where I worked and where all my friends worked and took months to come back.

That is where operators of frontier AI are sitting this month. It is a commercial problem before and after it is a technical one, and it is an ongoing one.

## The change you did not order

On Thursday I ran through what the labs have shipped since the pacing essay, and said that every model change "ends up being just enough to carry with it an API charge, and a design change." That line deserves its own day, because it is the part of this story the operator pays for. Before I do that, though, I want to be clear that I don't mean to imply that the model improvements aren't *more* than that -- often far more -- but I mean it in the context of what the operator *does* with that model once you get beyond chatbots: the tweaking and tuning that every model change has driven into the way delivered code works.

And, as if this challenge wasn't already hard *enough* with every frontier lab except Google shipping more releases in the last 12 months than in the 12 before (by my count, OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI went from 19 releases to 36, nearly double), despite the "pacing the frontier" essay, the labs are not slowing down to make the point for us. By LLM Gateway's count, 20 new models from 12 providers have shipped so far in September. The safety talks between the three frontier labs (Elon Musk has publicly backed them, but xAI has not been named as a participant) went ahead without waiting for Washington to decide what to do, and so did the release calendar.

## Four days to eighteen months

It's important, I think, to acknowledge that each lab publishes a policy for how long you get before the model you built on changes or disappears. Unfortunately, for *reasons*, they are not the same policy. Not even close.

OpenAI promises "at least 6 months" for generally available models, and much less for previews. Anthropic promises "at least 60 days' notice" before it retires a publicly released model, and after that, "Requests to retired models will fail" -- to be clear, meaning any API call which specifies a model no longer available will not degrade to the next model up in the same family still available, it just throws an error.

Microsoft, on the other hand, which hosts many of these models on Azure, and presumably because they have had reliable end-of-life policies on software for decades, sets an Azure model's retirement date at launch, eighteen months out, and upgrades standard deployments automatically, region by region. So using a Microsoft-hosted model trades pinning a specific version in your API calls, by default, for reliability as the needle moves. And the third frontier lab, Google, says their "latest" alias for Gemini "will get hot-swapped with every new release," with "a 2-week notice" by email for breaking changes. So your time to respond to an eliminated model on the American frontier ranges from two weeks at the low end to a seemingly generous 18 months at the high end, though the second is a known date specified in advance, and therefore much easier to plan around.

Just so I don't give you the impression it's only the frontier that does this, consider our good friends over at DeepSeek, which announced on 10 September that from the 14th, "all `deepseek-v4-pro` requests will route to V4.1-Flash at V4.1-Flash rates." Four days' notice. The operator's code still says Pro. The model answering is a different one. There are so many issues with that which go beyond just your own changes, I am not going to unpack it here. Suffice to say this kind of "change management" is by no means unique to the big American names.

None of this is hidden or unreasonable on its face (mostly). OpenAI's own documentation tells operators to pin production to a specific snapshot and build test suites for every upgrade. That is good advice, and it is also a description of work that lands entirely on the operator's side. It's also an instruction that most operators took on when the release cycle was every six months or so; the current cycle of weeks is something that very few production organizations are geared for at any measurable level.

## The flag that now fails

I do not know that there is a public figure for this anywhere, because I do not know how much people want to admit to it. But I can tell you for nothing that if you're building against the API, and every single one of your calls says "turn thinking off," and you're shifting to Opus 5.5 as your default model, every one of those calls is now going to throw a failure inside that sync call out to the API. (This is only one example, but it gives you an idea of how often there are breaking changes inside versions that most software professionals would have always said should be avoided on the cadence the labs move to.) Anthropic's own migration guide says so in one line: `thinking: {"type": "disabled"}` now returns a 400 error, "thinking.type.disabled is not supported for this model." Forced tool calls, non-default temperature and a prefilled answer fail the same way. If you hadn't already prepared for a situation where thinking was no longer a viable flag, and you didn't wrap that call to handle a 400 as a result, you are going to have to go back and touch every piece of code inside your (presumably complex) enterprise solution which starts any given command or instruction that way.

With AI in the mix, that is probably not an overly burdensome fix (depending on how comprehensive its repo access is, and on how many different and distinct API calls you are making). But it is not nothing. And if you did not do the implementation yourself, if a third party did it, then you have failures in the routine until somebody gets it sorted, on their schedule rather than yours. The implications are substantial whether or not anybody ever puts a dollar figure on them (and I can guarantee you, the third party will be doing so, especially if it needs to be done in a rush, as presumably it would be).

Pinning helps less than it looks, too, because behavior can move without the name moving. In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in ChatGPT after it turned sycophantic, and said afterwards that its "offline evaluations... generally looked good." In September 2025 Anthropic published a postmortem on three infrastructure bugs. At the worst hour, 16% of Sonnet 4 requests were affected, and "the evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting." No version number changed in the second case. Both are vendors describing their own failures, which is to their credit, and both show the same thing: the lab's tests passed, and the operator's customers found the problem. And then the operator's customers *wore* the problem.

## The Gemini gap

The Gemini story belongs here, and not because it makes it more interesting.

In May, during a cybersecurity test run by the testing firm Irregular, Gemini reached systems at three real companies. That's right, **again** (I am not sure what the people in the test labs who are supposed to be the super-safe interrogators here are doing, but regardless), the test machines had internet access they were not supposed to have.

The model guessed one login and found the credentials for the other two in a public code repository, then used them on sites it believed were part of the test. Heather Adkins, Google's vice president of security engineering, said the model "guessed credentials to access websites it thought were part of the test," and that in all three cases it stopped before doing anything further. A Google source went further with CSO Online: "No harm was caused. There was not an issue of model misalignment."

Leaving aside the specifics of that claim from Google, because it is a very tight legal needle they are trying to thread there, and I have shared my opinions on "misalignment" frequently, the test lab told Google at the end of July. Google said nothing publicly for about *seven weeks*, until the Wall Street Journal called, and the story ran on 18 September. SecurityWeek put it plainly: "Unlike the other AI companies involved in similar incidents, Google did not disclose the findings until it was contacted by the WSJ." Anthropic had disclosed its own incidents from the same kind of test on 30 July.

Now regardless of the motivations here by either Google or Anthropic or OpenAI or any other of the "me too!" episodes we have heard about, that delay in disclosure is materially important *to the operator.* This is Google making the operators using it carry an unknown weight for nearly two months, which is a lifetime in these circles.

For a lot of buyers, Gemini was the lab that had not had one of these. They were willing to trade the slightly lower capability (because, for the vast majority of actions, regardless of what the sellers want you to believe, the latest and greatest intelligence is just not necessary) for cost, reliability, the integration with existing infrastructure, and the fact that they had a certain level of security confidence because there had not been a breakout. In some cases, I have even seen contracts where a breakout can be a cause for cancellation or renegotiation. That's probably going to go away, if you are buying from the frontier, because it's just not supportable anymore. And even more importantly -- seven weeks after a known issue occurred, the lack of disclosure, especially from a company which was believed to be safety-forward, has cost them trust in the marketplace. Without being rude, there was already a lot of nervousness about Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI. Google has been a trusted partner for *decades* and this type of delayed disclosure has legal and risk managers asking "what else haven't they told us because we didn't know we had to ask?"

The operator carries the cost anyway, and so do their insurance providers, tech teams, security infrastructure, and so forth.

## The asymmetry

Put the two sides together. Capability has a price per token, and it keeps dropping: on 15 September TypeSafe put a decision model called Jev into early access at $42 per billion input tokens. The same week, a researcher showed that one malicious browser extension could take over the AI assistants built into five browsers, including Gemini Live in Chrome, Edge, Perplexity Comet and Claude in Chrome. The capability got cheaper and the attack surface got wider, a day apart. Control has no published price at all, but it has a schedule. Every release you adopt means a re-test. Every alias you did not pin means a behavior change on someone else's calendar. Every incident you hear about late means exposure you carried without knowing.

The lab books the release. The operator books everything after it. On Monday this arc put the deployer on the hook when the model is wrong. This is the quieter version of the same bill: the deployer also pays when the model changes, wrong or not.

## The upgrade tax

I know for us, for years we carried services in .NET 2, even though it had been end-of-life and even though there was a known security issue with it, simply because I just didn't have the time to go back and rewrite those services and move them forward.

While it's slightly different when you're working with an LLM, it's not all that different. The fact that these things are changing as fast as they are, and particularly if you spend any time looking at what people say on the internet or watching YouTube videos or anything of the kind, you'll hear very quickly that, consistently, when new models come out, the YouTube guys will say: "Oh, you need to go back and clean out all your context and start over," or "You need to go back and reevaluate every single thing that you have in a configuration-level file because it's all being interpreted differently now." And by YouTube guys in this case I mean respected people in the space like Nate Herk, one of the most watched voices on YouTube about AI, or the other Nate, Nate B. Jones. And it is not only the YouTubers. Boris Cherny (creator and head of Claude Code at Anthropic) has talked about cutting 80% of Claude Code's own prompt as the models improved, and Thibault Sottiaux (who leads core products at OpenAI) describes the scaffolding around a model as temporary, something to be removed over time, with some adaptation always needed from one model to the next. That's enough of a pain when it's your files. I've done that exercise twice now, and in both cases it took me almost 8 hours to make all of the necessary config runs and changes.

Now I've looked at a couple of things with Opus 5.5, and it's substantially faster than Opus 5 was, so that's not quite as bad as it used to be. The changes that it makes, though, make it very clear that if you don't make the changes, then you'll essentially fall back to an Opus 5 model conversation with the concomitant performance and be defeating the purpose of the upgraded model, regardless of anything.

The issue here for most operators is time and money. The fact that the model is cheaper, that's great. Over time, running agentic tasks on Opus 5.5, which costs 40% less to run than Opus 5 by Reuters' count (a fifth less at list price) and substantially less than Fable 5.1, Anthropic's larger model, means that the CFO is going to be happy about the fact that the monthly token bills are going to go down. That 40% comes with an important caveat: Anthropic's own figures put much of the saving in cached content, where cache reads are 60% cheaper and, in Anthropic's words, "make up the majority of agentic and coding work costs," which is a category that is becoming more and more prominent as agentic workloads become the standard. If you are not using cached content, your savings will be smaller.

If you don't have people re-optimizing the code or the model, and if you're not making sure that you're avoiding things like the "no thinking, no longer supported" sort of situation that I detailed above, then you probably need to really look at the finances of that. Having a core team of developers rewrite a piece of code in a matter of a couple of days because it's broken all over with the new model and your customer is having a problem with it is not a cheap exercise. It's not an exercise that you can't plan for, except that the notice arrives on schedule, but the breaking detail lands with the release, so you kind of can't plan for it.

Not only are the costs substantial, but the costs are chunky and uneven, which, when you're trying to manage development cost in a SaaS business in particular, is pretty much the exact opposite of what anybody in the chain wants it to be. It's hard to say whether this is intentional or not. There doesn't necessarily seem to be a lot of justification in my mind for returning a 400 on a check as opposed to just an acknowledgment but no confirmation. There are simple ways to get to the same thing that don't result in a breaking change, but just result in an informational change, but the labs have opted not to do that. Failing loudly is arguably the honest choice, but it could have come with a deprecation window. The cost to the operator, to the implementer, continues to go up.

## Throw your hands in the air, scream and shout, run about

If it wasn't completely evident from my commentary above, in regards to the asymmetry involved in adoption speed between the actual adopters, the enterprise, and frontier labs, the releasers, I think there's a very simple principle at play here that's worth keeping in mind.

Traditionally, most companies that ship software have learned that when an upgrade comes, the decision about when to take the upgrade and the implications of taking the upgrade have always impacted the budget, the operations, the scheduling, and the implementation. There is a reason why many companies held on to Office 97 for years past its reasonable shelf life. Up until last year, when Microsoft started aggressive end-of-life actions with Windows 10, I still had customers running Office 97 over Windows 95 because they were unwilling to take on the overhead of the upgrades (not many, but I am thinking of two in particular). What made them change? Not Microsoft. Cyber insurance policies and regulatory compliance. It had a lot of problems, but they were known problems, and they knew exactly what their risk was. From a risk minimization perspective, it made more sense for them to stay put than it did for them to adopt something new that could break their existing processes, especially if they were heavy users of macros (which were sort of an early precursor of a lot of the type of things that we see people do now with computer use).

There is a long and well-traveled path to all of this. The biggest problem that you have in terms of maintaining that is that the labs are releasing more like they were a SaaS product. When you subscribe to a SaaS product, that's just it: it's a subscription, and you more or less get what the SaaS provider decides. If they push a cloud-wide fix, you don't get to exempt yourself from that unless you have a self-hosted container or something similar.

The labs have taken that same perspective. You don't get to opt out of this change and apply it to something that programmatically runs underneath the surface of every piece of code that you write. It's not called out to. It doesn't exist behind a separate surface or infrastructure. It's baked into the way that solutions are built.

The implications of the days talked about above, the 2 weeks, the 60, and so on, are far greater in this situation than they would have been if we were just talking about a SaaS product, because it's embedded in everything that's being done: an agentic workload that suddenly costs twice as much as it should have. The way it was done originally is really inefficient for the way the model works now, and that has real implications. You are going to be the one who carries it. Nothing in the lab's terms makes it responsible, and your implementer will bill you for the fix. In many cases, you won't even know that it's doing that until it's been running for a while, unless you go through this exercise on every single model upgrade.

## A budget line for the churn

**Price the churn.** Put a line in the AI budget for re-validation, sized to the release calendar rather than to the contract. If a vendor ships monthly, you re-test monthly or you accept the drift knowingly. I would generally allocate at least 1 day of developer time for every 3 to 4 weeks of developer time that was involved in building the original solution. Depending on how substantive the model change is, or if you're shifting from manufacturer to manufacturer (i.e., going from Opus 5 to GPT-6 Astra), you might need to allocate more. Even though the commands are very similar, they're not identical, and the responses are not identical. Your evaluations are not going to be identical, and your decision-making is not going to be identical. As an estimated perspective, budget roughly 1/20th of the original build for a same-vendor upgrade, closer to 1/10th if you are changing vendors.

**Inventory the parameters behind the model name.** Every call that sets thinking, temperature, tool use or output format is a call that can break when the default model changes underneath it. Know where they are before the change forces you to find them.

**If someone else built it, settle the fix TIMING and COST both now.** Ask your implementer who repairs a broken call after a model change, how fast, and whether you are paying for it. The time to learn the answer is not the morning the routine starts failing.

**Make the notice window a procurement question.** Two weeks, sixty days, six months and eighteen months are all live policies today. Ask for the number before you sign, and write your change process around the shortest one you accept.

**Ask about incident disclosure too.** How long between the vendor learning of an incident involving its model and the vendor telling its customers? If the answer is "when the press calls," you have learned something worth pricing into your contract as well.

If the model upgrades continue to come every month to 6 weeks, I believe the frontier models are going to knock themselves out of the business operator market, because there's just too much variance and operators can't keep up with it. So figure out where you stand: how many internal products the contracts touch, and what a model change does to your business, your software operations and your bottom line.

Coca-Cola did not send the restaurants a check for the second syrup line, and no lab is going to send you one either. If you know where you stand, you'll be ahead of most of the market. If you don't, somebody else is setting the value of your business every single day.
