Five people. One hundred agents.

That is the staffing ratio being pitched into every boardroom in corporate America right now. It comes from McKinsey's "The agentic organization" report: a human team of two to five people can already supervise an agent factory of fifty to a hundred specialized agents running an end-to-end process -- onboarding a customer, launching a product, closing the books. It is a spectacular number. Boards have seen it. CEOs and CFOs love it. Consultants have built decks on it. Somewhere right now, a headcount plan is being drawn against it. In many ways, this kind of thinking is a big part of the story behind the tech layoff numbers for the first half of 2026 -- somewhere between roughly 120K and 139K depending on how you count.[^1]

Scary, right (or exciting, depending on whether you are feeling optimistic today)? But take a pause. Now go read the sentence it lives in, because it opens with three words that should stop you cold: *"In our experience."* **From McKinsey.**

Not "in our study." Not "across our benchmark of client deployments, measured over such-and-such a period." In our experience. The ratio that is redrawing org charts is an anecdote from client work, published back in September 2025, and it has been compounding through boardrooms ever since without anyone going back to check what was actually underneath it.

Think about how every other dangerous ratio in your life got set. Your local pool posts one lifeguard per however-many swimmers because somebody, somewhere, did the drowning math -- insurers, regulators, decades of grim actuarial homework. Nurse-to-patient ratios are fought over in legislatures precisely because everyone knows what happens on the wrong side of them. The ratios we trust with consequences are the ones somebody was forced to defend. And to defend, over and over, despite changing tools, technologies, and expectations.

The agent-factory ratio is not one of those. It is what is commonly called a "brochure number." And while many of us have cynically gotten very used to these kinds of numbers reported as fact, this week I want to pull on what is standing behind it -- because the answer, five days from now, is going to turn out to be the question of who actually controls *anything* in this new arrangement.

## The seat you fought for

Yesterday I closed out July arguing the model was never the hard part. Today starts the tour of what is.

Two weeks ago I spent five days arguing that certain human seats are load-bearing -- the explainer, the qualified approver, the boundary-seer, the Grower. Last week I argued that the account you can keep around a machine is the fence you build, not the path you can't trace, and that a qualified human has to stand on that wall -- that the loop is load-bearing. If you have been reading along, you already know who the two-to-five people in McKinsey's ratio have to be. They are exactly the people from "Now the Hard Part: The Seats That Can't Go Empty." We spent July establishing that they must exist and what they must be able to do.

Consider that argument won (because, you know, it's my newsletter, and I say so). Here is the harder question the winning bought us: *what does that seat actually control?*

Because a seat and control are not the same thing. A pilot's seat with no yoke in front of it is a chair with a view. This week -- the models, the exhaust, the regulators, the enforcement layer -- is one long walk through everything flowing through that seat that the person sitting in it does not currently govern. Today's job is just to show you the cockpit.

## Nobody will stand behind the number

Start with the ratio itself, because the story gets better the closer you look.

Back in April, Deloitte looked at the same question -- how many agents can a human actually supervise -- and did something consultancies almost never do: it declined to answer. Its operating-model work poses "How many digital agents can a human manage and be accountable for?" as an open question. Read that again. One consultancy asserts a span of control from anecdote; the other, staring at the same market, says out loud that nobody knows. And to be clear, as far as I am concerned, the key part of this sentence which is carrying a LOT of water is the "be accountable for" -- and that's the piece that caused Deloitte to defer.

The rest of the industry isn't converging either. McKinsey itself now says it runs some 25,000 agents alongside roughly 40,000 humans. Do the arithmetic: the firm selling you a fifty-agent factory per five-person crew is running well under one agent per human in its own building. Rivals at EY and PwC have publicly pushed back on agent-count as a metric at all. Sensibly -- a thousand trivial agents are worth less than five good ones.

The most honest number anyone has produced so far comes from Replit, which instrumented its own organization and reported 2.9x code output per engineer with quality metrics flat -- and even that comes with caveats, which, credit to Replit, they printed themselves: the headline metric is lines of code (a thing agents inflate happily), and Replit sells agent tooling, so this is a company reporting favorable numbers about its own product category. It is the best data point available. It is also, notice, *not a span-of-control number* at all. Measured, instrumented, and caveated is a different animal from "in our experience," and I'm not pretending otherwise.

So here is where we actually are. The one number reorganizing work in 2026 is the one number nobody who produced it will stand behind as measurement. Every profession that supervises dangerous things at scale -- aviation, nursing, air traffic control -- earned its ratios through bodies and lawsuits and decades. We are setting ours from a slide.

## Whose humans are they, anyway

It gets one turn stranger, and this is the detail I can't stop thinking about, and I hope you won't either.

While everyone argues about how many humans the agent factory needs, the hyperscalers have quietly started paying to *be* those humans. Microsoft has reportedly committed $2.5 billion and AWS a further $1 billion to programs that embed their own AI engineers inside customer organizations -- not selling to your team, sitting *on* your team, building your agent factory from a desk in your building. One source so far on those particular decimals, so hold them loosely; where this is heading, though, is not subtle -- and it is not only the hyperscalers.[^2]

On one reading, that's generosity: the talent barrier is real and someone is removing it. On the reader-of-this-newsletter reading: walk back through the two-to-five seats from last week's argument, and now imagine a couple of them wearing a vendor's lanyard. The person defining what your agents do, judging their output, and correcting their mistakes -- the person in the seat we spent two weeks fortifying -- is on someone else's payroll, building on someone else's platform, and going home with everything they learned about how your business actually runs. Your data governance, your model provenance, your exit planning: all of it now has a third party standing in the middle of it, and he brought his own chair.

## The part Mr. Nadella already told you

Which brings me to the thing the ratio conversation never mentions, and the reason this is a week-long argument rather than a Monday howl-at-the-moon moment.

Those two-to-five humans have one core job: correcting the machine. Catching the agent's mistake, fixing the draft, refusing the bad output, steering the process back. That is the load-bearing work. And in mid-July, Microsoft's own CEO, Satya Nadella, published a warning about exactly that work: every correction your people make to an AI system teaches the provider's model something about how your business operates. He called it the Reverse Information Paradox -- you pay for the intelligence with money, and then pay again with the institutional know-how you must reveal to make the intelligence useful.

Now follow the incentive. The fewer humans in the factory, the more concentrated their judgment -- and the more valuable each correction they make becomes, to somebody who is not you. Your five survivors are simultaneously the control layer *and* the leak. That is not a side effect. That is the architecture. And as with any type of operational architecture, it's really, really important to understand who created it, who benefits, and whether their goals actually align with yours. Tomorrow I am going to take that apart properly, because the CEO who issued the warning happens to run the company selling the product.

And there's one more line item for the cockpit inventory, because it decides whether any of this is sustainable: where do the five humans come from? The rung that has always grown them is the one being cut. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from ADP payroll data, finds workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen a 16 percent relative decline in employment since late 2022, controlling for firm-level shocks -- while older colleagues in the same jobs held steady or grew. Young software developers alone are down about a fifth from their late-2022 peak. The survivors in your ratio came from somewhere, and we are dismantling the somewhere. Saturday's piece is about how to rebuild it on purpose.

## What you control from the seat

So, Monday inventory. The ratio governing your headcount plan: an anecdote nobody will defend as measurement. The humans in the ratio: increasingly, on "loan" from your vendor at a total net cost that is virtually unpredictable. Their daily work: exhaust flowing to a model provider under an agreement you probably haven't read with that question in mind. Their replacements: not being grown. And all of it sitting under governance documents that -- Friday's piece, and I'll prove it -- mostly can't stop any of this at runtime.

That is the week. Tuesday, the exhaust. Wednesday, what happens when an entire country adopts at record speed with a framework that is still, effectively, a press release (I will give you a hint: disorganized work at 50x speed is just 50x more disorganized and harder to wrangle back under control). Thursday, the fight over who even gets to decide which models exist. Friday, what a control actually is, technically, when someone finally builds one. Sunday, what it adds up to.

Some acquaintances of mine have built a really interesting startup that is doing well, has a VC round, the valuation is high, and the model ("the Uber of X") is very well-proven. A couple of weeks ago, they told all their developers that within 90 days all of their coding needed to be done by agents, that none of the coders should be writing any of their own code, any more. They simultaneously, because they are Elon fans, rolled out Grok's own coding agent -- Grok Build, xAI's command-line tool -- enterprise wide as the root coding agent. Last week, it came out that the tool had been quietly uploading entire codebases -- full commit history, files the agent was never asked to open, even the secrets sitting in developers' `.env` files -- to xAI's own cloud storage. And here is the part that should make your teeth itch: it kept doing it *even with the model-training setting switched off*. This wasn't a training feature you could opt out of. It was a retention behavior that sailed straight past the one toggle everyone assumed was the off switch. It was on by default (and you know how I feel about default settings) -- which means my friends, who rolled it out on the default configuration, at speed, enterprise wide, were exactly the population that got exposed. To xAI's credit, once a researcher proved it, they killed the upload within hours and Elon posted that everything already uploaded would be "completely and utterly deleted." But read that in order: the codebases went up first, quietly, by default; the promise to delete them arrived only after a stranger on the internet caught it.[^3]

This is a slightly different anecdote from the root argument but it sits right on this wall -- the bosses told the guys who sit on the wall to move back to a different wall with more space, the agents took over the entire playground, and the third party with access to the building siphoned everything in the playground. Even had the guys on the wall KNOWN that it was happening, they wouldn't have been able to stop it -- and the likelihood they would have known, at the distance they were instructed to adopt, is pretty minimal even with the most diligent engineers.

**Today's assignment:** one honest look at your own building. Two questions, and most organizations have answered neither. Last week's question: who sits in the seats, and are they qualified to be there? This week's: what do those seats actually control -- the learning loop, the vendor dependency, the enforcement layer, or none of the above? And the bonus questions: Where are the leaks that you haven't even considered yet likely to be found? How can you begin to fill them?

Five people, a hundred agents, and a ratio written on a slide.

Does knowing what's actually underneath it change your calculus about how fast you build the factory? Can it? Or is the math complex enough -- and the decisions governed by enough outside factors -- that it doesn't matter for the design, and the number just scrolls past with everything else, in a news cycle that never even seems to pause, let alone stop?

[^1]: The two figures bracket two different ways of counting, which is really the whole point. Layoffs.fyi tallies roughly 120,000 people laid off at *tech companies* worldwide in the first half of 2026 -- nearly matching its entire 2025 total in six months. Challenger, Gray & Christmas counts 139,156 announced *U.S. tech-sector* job cuts over the same stretch, up 83% year over year, with AI the single most-cited reason for four straight months (though "cited reason" measures the press release, not the P&L). We're not going to debate these numbers here -- reconciling the trackers is a week's work in itself. What's interesting about them is the negative space. Over the very same six months, employment in tech *occupations* across the whole economy actually *grew* -- up 47,000 in June alone, with tech-occupation unemployment sitting at 2.9% against 4.2% nationally (CompTIA's read of the BLS data). Record gross cuts at tech firms and rising net tech employment, both true at once -- because the people let go get rehired, often outside the tech sector, and the cuts fall hardest on the non-technical roles. Hold onto that paradox; Saturday's piece lives inside it. (Layoffs.fyi via TechCrunch, 6 Jul 2026; Challenger June report, 2 Jul; CompTIA/BLS, 2 Jul.)

[^2]: And it is not only rumor, either. Every frontier lab is now standing up the same motion. OpenAI launched a *Deployment Company* in May -- roughly 150 forward-deployed engineers, more than $4 billion in backing from nineteen partners, its bench seeded by buying a UK applied-AI firm outright. Anthropic followed in July with *Ode*, a Blackstone-backed implementation firm (reported at $1.5 billion and 100 engineers). Google, Mistral, Cohere and Databricks all run the same play. The economics are the tell: the labs decided model quality no longer wins the deal -- deployment does -- so they are racing to become your "system of work" before a consultancy or a hyperscaler gets there first. As the venture firm a16z put it, "enterprises buying AI are like your grandma getting an iPhone: they want to use it, but they need you to set it up." Worth the mirror image, too, because it is the whole sovereignty question in one contrast: China's open-weight labs mostly do the *opposite* -- DeepSeek runs no deployment arm at all, by design, and the model tends to arrive as open weights on an all-in-one appliance with nobody from the lab in the building. Two opposite answers to "who implements this?" -- and both of them still end with someone who isn't you learning how your business runs. (OpenAI newsroom, 11 May 2026; Ode via Business Wire + TechCrunch, 15 Jul; a16z, Jun 2025.)

[^3]: The incident is real and reproducible. A security researcher publishing as *cereblab* demonstrated (~14 July 2026), with a reproducible wire capture, that xAI's Grok Build CLI uploaded users' entire git repositories -- including files the agent never opened and unredacted `.env` secrets -- to an xAI cloud bucket, and that turning off the "improve the model" setting did *not* stop it. The researcher was careful to note he proved upload and storage, not that xAI trained on the data. xAI disabled the upload within hours, turned retention off by default, added a `/privacy` command, and open-sourced the tool; Elon Musk posted that all previously uploaded data would be "completely and utterly deleted." xAI said enterprise zero-data-retention customers were never affected -- which is exactly why running the *default* configuration was the whole exposure. (Sources: cereblab repro + The Hacker News and Simon Willison, 15 Jul 2026; Musk on X via Neowin, ~13 Jul. The combined entity is "SpaceXAI" after SpaceX's reported Feb 2026 acquisition of xAI.)
