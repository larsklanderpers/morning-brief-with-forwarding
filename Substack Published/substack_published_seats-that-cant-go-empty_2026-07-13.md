# Now the Hard Part: The Seats That Can't Go Empty

**Status: Draft (v1) -- target Monday, 13 July 2026**
**Series: Weekday feed**
**Thread: Shape of Work-to-Come × AI as Attack Surface (transition piece)**
**Builds on: Sunday's capstone "The Actor Is No Longer Human" (12 Jul). Opens the week of 13–19 Jul, "The Load-Bearing Human."**
**Source spark: Friday's AI Work signal ("the agent era rewards the manager, not the fastest typist"); the r/cscareerquestions "workforce splitting in two" thread; last week's five-empty-chairs argument.**

---

Yesterday I closed out a week by walking you through five empty chairs. The gatekeeper who turned out to be a vendor. The insider who turned out to be a configuration. The identity with no person behind it, the attacker with no human at the keyboard, the ground the whole thing runs on that belongs to someone you can't reach. Five roles a person used to fill, each one quietly vacated, and a governance stack still politely addressed to a human who isn't coming back.

Believe it or not, that was the comforting half of the story. Easily enough resolvable now that the problem has been identified, structured enough to be knowable, and promising to be consistent enough that B-schools will teach it soon. I need to spend this week on the other half, because it's the one that is not so clear, not so identifiable, and is exactly the kind of problem that most organizations, flush with success from their automated efforts, will miss until it's far too late to do much about it.

If some seats emptied out without anyone noticing, then the obvious, uncomfortable question is: *which seats didn't?* Which chairs look every bit as automatable as the five I described, right up until you pull the human out of them -- and then hold, and hold, and hold, and then fail all at once? Because those exist too. And they are much harder to see, precisely because removing the human from them doesn't break anything on Monday.

## The wall that was holding up the house

Anyone who has ever renovated an old house (or at least watched it on TV) has met, or been, the person who looks at an inconvenient wall and says: it's just a wall. The ceiling's still up. Knock it out, open up the kitchen, get the light in. And they're right for a while. You take the wall out and nothing happens. The ceiling stays where it is. For months. Sometimes years. Then one wet winter the floor above starts to sag, and a very expensive person in a hard hat explains to you that the wall you removed was the one holding up the house, and the ceiling stayed up this long only because everything else was quietly working harder to cover for it.

You cannot tell a load-bearing wall from a decorative one by looking at it. They're the same wall. The only way to know is to understand the structure -- to know what's resting on what. And the tragedy of the load-bearing wall is that the feedback is delayed. The moment you remove it feels like a win. The bill arrives later, and by then the person who swung the hammer is long gone.

This is the exact shape of the mistake the whole economy is making with human roles right now. When you automate a task and nothing breaks, you conclude the human was decorative. Sometimes that's true, and good -- that's last week's story, and there's real freedom in it. But sometimes the human was load-bearing, the structure is just working harder to cover for the gap, and the sag is coming. The difference between those two cases is the most important call a leader will make this decade, and almost nobody has the language for it.

## The reassurance was actually an assignment

There was a line in Friday's work brief that I underlined and then couldn't stop thinking about: the agent era doesn't reward the fastest typist or the cleverest prompter -- it rewards the manager. The one who can define the job, judge the result, and grow the next person who can.

When I first read it, I read it as comfort. *See, there's still a place for us.* Sit with it a day longer and it stops sounding like reassurance and starts sounding like an assignment. Because "the roles that survive are the ones that hold a boundary" is not a promise that you'll be fine. It's a description of *work* -- specific, effortful, unglamorous work -- that a lot of people are currently assuming will take care of itself. Judging the result is a skill. Defining the job is a skill. Being the person who can look at what the machine produced and say "no, and here's why" is a skill, and it is not the one most of us spent the last twenty years being paid to have.

So the survivors aren't the people with the safe titles. They're the people standing at a boundary doing the boundary's work. That's a much narrower and more demanding claim, and it's the through-line for everything I want to write this week.

## Why the evidence is coming from code

Here's the part that surprised me, and the reason this week is going to lean somewhere I don't usually spend five days in a row: the clearest evidence for which human roles are load-bearing is coming out of software engineering. Not because coding matters more than your work -- because coding got automated *first and deepest.* The agents came for code earlier and harder than almost anywhere else, which means software teams are simply further into the experiment than the rest of us. They're the ones who already knocked out the walls, waited two years, and are now standing in the results telling everyone what sagged.

And what they're reporting is fascinating, because it isn't "the robots took our jobs." It's stranger and more useful than that. It's a set of very specific, very human capabilities that turned out to be structural -- the ability to actually explain what got built, the qualified judgment to approve or refuse something, the trained eye that can see a danger the tooling doesn't flag -- and a set of others that really were just habit. The practitioners are living in the delayed feedback right now, and they're generous enough to be posting the results.

You can already see the split forming, and it's worth naming because people keep drawing the line in the wrong place. The workforce is bifurcating, yes -- but not into senior and junior, and not into safe and doomed. The real fault line is between the people who picked up the leverage while keeping a load-bearing human skill, and the people who let the machine's output outrun their own understanding and quietly became the person who can no longer explain what they ship. One of those groups is compounding. The other is about to meet a wet winter.

## The week ahead

So this week is a tour of the seats that can't go empty, mostly told through the people furthest into finding out.

Tomorrow: the human who can still explain the thing -- and why "it works, ship it" is how you accumulate a debt that comes due all at once. Wednesday: the qualified approver, and why a human in the loop who can't read what they're approving is not a safeguard but a liability wearing a safeguard's badge. Thursday: the trust boundary itself, made brutally concrete by a fifteen-year-old flaw, and the person whose whole value is being able to see a line no dashboard draws. Friday: where those people actually come from, because none of them arrive fully formed and we are busy dismantling the on-ramp. Saturday, a lighter turn toward what all this leverage means if you'd rather point it at your own ambitions. And Sunday I'll try to make it add up.

Last week's question was *which chair did the human leave?* This week's is harder, and I think more urgent: **which chairs did we leave that we were never supposed to -- and how long until the ceiling tells us?**

The honest answer is that you usually find out at the worst possible moment. So the work, starting tomorrow, is to find out on purpose instead. Now, before the ceiling falls down.
