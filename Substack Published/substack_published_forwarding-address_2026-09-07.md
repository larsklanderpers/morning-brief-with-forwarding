New South Wales now requires every one of its agencies to register every AI system it uses, before it uses it. It is the most serious piece of AI governance I have seen any Australian government actually ship.

It also contains one sentence, about forty words long, that keeps a fast-growing category of government AI off that register. I wrote about that sentence a month ago and did not publish it. Two versions of the policy have now been published, a version number apart, and that sentence is in both of them word for word. That is the part I want your Monday for, because a clause that survives an edit is not an oversight. Somebody looked at it and left it.

A forwarding address is a real instrument. You move out, you file the card, and it works. The post office honors it, the mail goes where the card says, and nobody in the chain is doing anything wrong. The whole arrangement rests on one assumption that nobody checks, which is that somebody is still at the other end. If the address on the card is an office that got renamed, or a suite that was subdivided, or a company that was folded into another one two years ago, the redirect still works perfectly. The mail arrives exactly where you told it to go. It just does not arrive anywhere useful, and you will not find out until something in it mattered.

That is the shape of it, and it is not really a New South Wales story. Some version of this sentence is sitting in the AI inventory your own organization is standing up right now. Before we start, I will warn you in advance that there are *a lot* of document references in this piece, because tracing what it all means ends up being really, really specific, and it's important to understand that. You don't really much need to hold onto all the document names -- what you need to do is understand that the diagram of how these things all tie together is very, very complex. Stay with me though -- I promise the address at the end of this one is real.

## Four obligations and three good clauses

Give them the credit first, because they have earned more of it than they are going to get from most readers.

The NSW AI Operational Policy is issued under Circular DCS-2026-02, which is active and carries an issue date of 30 July 2026, and it replaces both the 2024 circular and the NSW AI Ethics Policy. It binds every agency in the NSW Government sector. Four hard obligations sit at the center of it. Designate an Accountable Official at senior executive level and notify NSW GovAI within three months. Register AI use cases before use. Run the assessment framework where registration tells you to. File an annual attestation by the thirty-first of October each year, with the first reporting date in 2027.

And then there are three clauses in that document which are actually quite well thought out and speak highly to the working committee which wrote the circular:

The first: *Agencies must ensure AI dependencies supporting critical services have tested business continuity plans that can be activated when required.* To be clear, that is a government refusing, in writing, to become dependent on a vendor it cannot replace. However, it is also a government side-stepping key issues, because unless the business continuity plans include numerous situations, like the provider or model goes down, or there is a determination that data sovereignty or inference sovereignty is not being observed, or if they are using a frontier model from the US, if the US government deploys a kill-switch. All of these items are material to this language and since they are all inside the "business continuity" plan on a department by department basis, there is little or no transparency here into just how hard that clause bites. 

The second: *Records must be managed as State records in accordance with the **State Records Act 1998***. You cannot rent that audit trail. It cannot live only inside the provider's platform, and if it goes missing that is a reportable event. And this clause is going to get harder to satisfy, not easier. If a model discloses little or nothing about how it got from the input to the output, then the audit trail is basically "where you started" and "where you ended," and I am fairly sure that is not what the Act has in mind by a record. There is a live version of that argument running right now over one of OpenAI's own models, on the strength of what OpenAI put in its own system card. That deserves considerably more space than a clause inside a piece about something else, so I am taking it properly on Friday.

The third is the one I would print out and pin up, and put on the virtual desk of every Service NSW employee and force every internal application developer building solutions for the government to always answer to, every PR, every design: *Treat all inputs to and outputs of AI systems as potentially shared with the provider.* Stated flat, as policy, by a government. Everything I have been writing about inference sovereignty arriving not as infrastructure spend but as an acceptable-use clause that costs nothing and bites the week it is written and is categorically unavoidable as a result (very similar to the AML policy decisions we have discussed in the past). And its important to note -- zero-data retention (ZDR), which many enterprise customers require from the inference providers, does not obviate this statement nor its implications for PII: because you are still passing the data to the provider, regardless of how ephemeral you believe it to be.

This is not a box-ticking document. Somebody thought hard about it. And that has weight when it comes to both what it *says* and what it *doesn't say.*

## So, the forty words promised you above

Here is the sentence, verbatim:

*"The requirement to register AI use cases does not apply to third-party suppliers providing services to the NSW Government where they use AI as part of those services. These arrangements are managed through procurement, contracting, and supplier assurance processes."*

Read the second half again, because the second half is where this whole thing turns into something very different from where it started. The clause does not say supplier AI is out of scope. It says supplier AI is somebody else's filing. It is a forwarding address to another department that is responsible for the supplier, and may or may not say a whole lot of anything about what is actually *supplied.*

And the register is the artifact the entire regime is built on. It is what the annual attestation to the Office for AI certifies. Draw the scope of the register and you have drawn the scope of everything downstream of it. That's also important beyond just its existence as an artifact. It's important as a *roadmap.* We're barely 20 lines into this piece and hopefully you can already see how twisty this is ultimately going to get. 

Two versions of this policy have now been published. The first carries the version line *"Final version 1: July 2026."* The one currently linked from the live policy page carries *"Final version 1.1 - 30 July 2026,"* which is the date the circular took effect, and it is served under a September filename. I have not seen a change log and I am not going to tell you what moved between the two, because I do not know, and no change log has been published that would let anybody outside the department find out. What I can tell you is that I checked the forty words in both files and they are identical, down to the punctuation.

So this is not a first-draft artifact that a second pass would have caught. It went past a second pass. That makes it intentional.

## Now follow the forwarding address

Here is where I stopped writing this piece in August and where it got interesting in September.

The clause says these arrangements are managed through procurement, contracting and supplier assurance. Fine. So I went and read procurement (well, I read *enough* of procurement to speak layman-intelligent about it).

**The AI procurement essentials guidance is the document that actually tells a NSW buying team how to handle a supplier delivering an outcome with AI inside it.** It names that exact procurement type: *"Platform, service or outcome-based procurement involving AI: Contracting a supplier to deliver a specific outcome or deliverable, where the supplier utilises generative AI technologies as part of the service provision."* That is the excluded category, described precisely, in the place the exclusion sends it. The guidance is real and it is not empty.

It also carries a date stamp on the page that reads **updated 6 May 2026**. That is four months ago and it is before the Operational Policy existed. Under its own heading of related NSW policies and frameworks it lists the AI Ethics Policy, which is the policy the Operational Policy replaced in terms. It also says, twice, that further guidance is coming: guidance *"will be developed for responsible AI officers, and buyers,"* and it refers to *"the upcoming AI Procurement Guidance."* Four months on, still upcoming. So right now, even though the other piece says that procurement is responsible, procurement hasn't yet established just what it is responsible *for.* 

**Behind that sits the Procurement Policy Framework**, which is the instrument with real force here, mandatory under the *Public Works and Procurement Act 1912*. Its published update history ends on 13 December 2024. The AI entry in that update history names Circular DCS-2024-04, which is the circular that was archived and replaced this July. That entry was written five months after the circular it names, and it is the last entry there is. So the framework still points at a document that no longer exists -- which means the statutory weight of it rests on a set of rules and protocols that are de facto no longer accurate.

**And then the one that genuinely surprised me.** The landing page for the AI Assessment Framework, which is the **mandatory assessment tool** the whole regime runs on, still describes its own mandate this way: all NSW government use of AI *"must apply the NSW AI Assessment Framework (AIAF) in line with the NSW mandate under Circular DCS-2024-04 to use both the AI Ethics Policy and the AIAF."* The archived circular and the replaced policy, cited as live authority, on the page hosting the tool. I first checked that page on 6 August. I checked it again on 5 September and it says the same thing. At the ground level, without changes to this framework, the new guidance is effectively immaterial. And that is, needless to say, a problem.

Four properties, one government. Exactly one of them is current, and it is the one with the exclusion in it.

## The claim I will defend

In my opinion, the obvious version of this argument is wrong.

Supplier AI is not unregulated in New South Wales. It is *unregistered,* and the paperwork it is redirected into is dated. Those are different sentences from "nobody covers vendor AI," and the difference is what I just spent a couple of thousand words talking about.

Vendor AI does get looked at. It gets looked at **once,** at the moment of purchase, by a track whose own governing documents were written for the previous regime, and then it never enters the central inventory or the annual attestation. The defensible claim is about visibility and the reporting line built on it, not about coverage, and I want to be held to the narrow one.

If you recall, we have been here before with Australian legal instruments. Earlier this year I ran two corrections in this newsletter on a different Australian instrument, APRA's CPS 230, because I wrote about what a standard required before I had read the standard properly. The argument I made after those corrections, that an instrument which never mentions AI can reach further than one written for it, is an argument I have made and will continue to make. I am, however, not going to make it today. I raise it only to be clear about which kind of claim this is. Everything above is a description of what four documents say today, with the dates they say it on, and every one of them is a page you can open yourself in less time than it took to read this section. That does not mean those things won't change in a month, but right now today, there is a clear gap in governance.

## OK, so that seems wrong. However, what should *I* do about it?

Three things, all of which are worth the exercise even if you don't need to take steps.

**Find your own forty words and read them as a routing instruction, not as an exclusion.** Every AI inventory has a scope sentence. It was written in a working group, usually by one person, usually in a single line nobody escalated. Do not ask whether the scope is broad enough. Ask where the sentence *sends* the things it leaves out, because it almost always names a destination rather than a void.

**Then go to that destination and check the date on it.** This is the step the scope sentence quietly assumes somebody already took, and it is the cheapest thing in this piece. Whatever your scope sentence redirects to, third-party risk, procurement, vendor assurance, the standard contract terms, open the actual document and look at when it was last revised and what it cites as its authority. A redirect into a process that has not been updated since before your AI policy existed is not coverage. It is a forwarding address to an office that moved.

**Then reconcile the two yourself, and schedule the next reconciliation before you finish the first one.** The register has an owner. The procurement framework has an owner. The scope sentence connecting them has no owner at all, because it is one line inside a document that belongs to the first of those two. Nobody is hiding that gap. It is just genuinely nobody's job, and things that are nobody's job do not get done in the year they stop being true.

You may not know this, but the vast majority of our regulatory obligations in the gaming space are not "one and done." They come back every two years, sometimes every year. And every time they come back, the questions have changed slightly. This puts us in a radically different frame than a government supplier here, because it's not just at the start of the contract that we have to prove ourselves out (procurement) it's also in ongoing governance on a recurring basis. And every one of those actions is an opportunity for the government or contracting entity to make sure that the nature of the agreement hasn't drifted. It's hard yards for everyone -- regulators and vendors -- but in a fast moving environment like this, I would argue it's a requirement -- the reconciliation itself is not enough. You have to keep reconciling and proving that it worked then and it works now. 

## The strongest argument against this piece

**"You are describing normal government website lag, and dressing it up as a governance finding."**

Perhaps. That's a mostly fair objection but I think that in this case it's clearly not lag. I checked the assessment framework page on 6 August and again on 5 September and it had not moved, and the procurement framework sitting behind it has not been revised since December 2024. Lag is a page that catches up. Twenty-one months is a decision. Web properties trail policy everywhere, in every jurisdiction, and a stale citation on a landing page is not evidence that anyone is doing their job badly. The people who wrote the Operational Policy do not own the procurement pages and cannot edit them.

Regardless, here is why I still think it matters. In an ordinary case, a stale page is an inconvenience: somebody reads the wrong version, notices, and finds the right one. This is not the ordinary case, because a specific clause in the current policy performs a redirect *into* those pages, and it does so for the entire category of AI that agencies buy rather than build. The staleness stops being background lag at the moment an operative instrument routes a live obligation through it. And the tell is the annual attestation. On 31 October 2027 an Accountable Official signs that their agency has complied. Everything in the register is in scope of that signature. Everything the forty words redirected is in scope of a framework last revised in December 2024. Both of those things will be true on the same page, over the same signature, and that makes them problematic for accountability.

## What this is really about

Every organization standing up an AI inventory this year has to make the same scoping choice, and almost all of them are making it the same way. List the models we run. List the tools we licensed. Vendor AI arriving inside a service we bought goes to third-party risk, where it becomes a questionnaire at onboarding and then, functionally, nothing. The choice does not feel like a governance decision when it is made, because it reads as an administrative tidy-up: this belongs over there, with the people who handle that.

New South Wales did the hard thinking, wrote it all down, and then wrote forty more words deciding how much of the problem the register would ever be able to see. Those forty words were the cheapest thing in the document to draft. They were cheap because they did not decide anything, they only pointed somewhere, and pointing somewhere always feels free. It is free right up until somebody walks to the address on the card and finds nothing.
