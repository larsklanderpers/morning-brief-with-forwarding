One of the biggest challenges adopters -- from my dad to enterprises everywhere -- is deciding what things to delegate to an agent and what things to hold onto -- things that require a human, even if only an emotional support human. At the root, every delegation framework points at the same tasks: the four primary qualifiers are those which are frequent, readily teachable, have low stakes each time, do not have to be done by you (which generally means there is no "special sauce" or emotional connection or other non-replicable element to the action).

Let us leave the personal aspect of this for today, and focus our energy where we generally do: on the enterprise. Please take a look at what those four words actually describe inside a business. Reconciliation. Provisioning. Access review. Invoice approval. Month-end close. The repetitive control functions (often easiest to identify in the finance function since the nature of much of that work IS so repetitive) -- the work that exists in the first place because somebody decided a second person ought to look at things, *probably because they identified that is was very expensive over time to NOT have a second person look at things, for lots of reasons.* They score highest on every delegation instrument in broad circulation, and they deserve to, because on the merits they are exactly the work you would want to hand over.

A year ago, "handing that function over to an agent" meant bringing in someone to help translate the prompts, navigate the guardrails, create the repetition, and so on, to get to a reliable, governed outcome. Now, the way you hand one over is to record yourself doing it. That is it.

Anybody who has shared a screen knows the half-second of dread. You hit the button and then remember what else is open. The client folder, the spreadsheet with everybody's salaries in it, the browser tab you would rather not explain, the message from your partner about the thing you forgot to do, the password manager sitting in the tray with a little green tick on it. Nothing usually happens. You close the tab, everyone is kind about it, the meeting moves on.

Take that half-second, stretch it to ten minutes, remove the audience who would have been kind about it, and point it at the one workflow in your business that touches the most systems. That is what it means when I say "record yourself to set up the agent." With all the bumps and bruises that can come along, intentionally or inadvertently.

## What is actually in the recording

A recorded workflow is not a task description. It is a credentialed traversal of your systems, performed by somebody with every right to be there, with the access paths left in (you).

Think about what is genuinely captured when you record a reconciliation. Which system you open first, and what you do when single sign-on drops you into the wrong tenant. The report you pull that appears in no documentation. The file share you go to because the official export has been broken since March and nobody logged it. The colleague you message when a number does not tie, and the fact that they answer, which tells you something about them too. That you still hold standing access to three things nobody remembers granting, because you covered for somebody in 2023 and it was never taken away.

None of that is in the process document. All of it is in the recording, because the recording is what happens and the process document is what was intended.

Here is the asymmetry that I think all of that critically exposes, once you really consider what it means. The delegation audit scores stakes **per execution**. One invoice. One reconciliation. One access review. Low each time, which is correct, and which is precisely why the task scores well enough to hand over. But recording yourself doing it one time and handing that to an agent to repeat is no longer per execution, not really. Now it is a process, whether you meant it to be or not, and that means it is standardized, and replicable, and knowable, and has suddenly become, in addition to everything else, a brand new attack surface. 

Attackers price the same thing **per process**. Not what one run is worth. What durable knowledge of how the run works is worth, applied every month, at machine speed, by something that already holds the credentials and does not get tired or curious or nervous.

Those two numbers are not close to each other, in merit, in risk weighting, in potential impact. Sadly, only one of them appears on the scoring sheet.

## What the vendors publish, and what one of them does not

Yesterday I went through what OpenAI documents about ChatGPT's Computer History -- what is captured, how long raw events survive, what is and is not trained on, and which accounts have it off out of the box. I am not going to run that again. The useful thing today is not what the document says. It is that the document exists at all.

Because I went and looked at the equivalent for xAI's Grok Bot this morning, and it does not. And this matters a lot, because Grok Bot has positioned itself as the most approachable agentic recording tool now available. It has caught the zeitgeist a fair bit, and so the sound and fury around the release has been substantial.

The teaching feature is documented, in xAI's own words, as recording *"one browser workflow from the computer view,"* and the *"recording is limited to ten minutes."* So: what it captures, and how long the capture runs. Both stated plainly.

Then, on the three questions any security person asks immediately afterward -- how long is the recording kept, is it used to train anything, and can an administrator switch the feature off -- the documentation says nothing. Not "no." Nothing at all. I checked this morning rather than trusting a note I made a fortnight ago, precisely because a fortnight is long enough for a shipping product to publish something, and those guys have freely admitted they are iterating fast, and the three gaps are still gaps.

I want to be careful here, because this distinction is really important, and as with so many things with these new tools, rigor has become more important, not less. **Absence of documentation is not evidence of practice.** I am not telling you that xAI retains those recordings, or trains on them, or handles them carelessly. I have no idea. *That is the problem.* Neither do you, and neither does the person in your finance team who is about to record a month-end close because somebody sent them a link. And as we have discussed seemingly endlessly over the past few weeks, *that* has the potential to be a governance failure of the highest order.

There is one line about secrets on that page, and where it sits turns out to be the interesting part. It says *"Strip secrets and anything confidential before you share"* -- and it appears in the section about sharing a bot with other people, not the section about recording your workflow into one. The documented advice covers the moment you hand the bot to a colleague. There is nothing about the ten minutes in which you hand your systems to the bot -- and by extension, to the ultimate owner behind that bot.

Even read at its most generous, that is a control handed to the least-equipped party in the transaction. The person recording is being asked to notice, in real time, which of the things on their own screen is sensitive, while doing their actual job, inside a feature whose entire premise is that they work normally so the model learns the real process rather than a sanitized one.

You cannot both work naturally and audit your own screen. The feature requires the first. The only available safeguard requires the second.

## The regulators got here first, and they were not thinking about agents

This is the part almost nobody has written, and it is the part that will matter to a compliance function long before anybody's threat model catches up.

The Information Commissioner's Office -- the UK data protection regulator -- published guidance on monitoring workers on 16 June. Its definition is deliberately, almost aggressively broad:

> "We use the term 'monitoring workers' to mean any form of monitoring of people who carry out work on your behalf."

Any form. Not covert monitoring, not productivity surveillance, not the things that word usually conjures. Any form.

What follows from that is not advisory. You **must** identify a lawful basis. You **must** make workers aware of how and what personal information you are collecting. And:

> "You must carry out a DPIA before undertaking any processing likely to cause high risk to workers' and other people's interests."

Now put those two things next to each other. On the ICO's own definition, an employee recording ten minutes of their own working screen so that a piece of software can learn to do their job is monitoring of a person who carries out work on your behalf. It is being processed by a third party. It captures whatever was on the screen, including the parts about colleagues and customers.

I would be very surprised if anybody who spent yesterday sorting their tasks into two columns has done a data protection impact assessment about it. I certainly have not seen a single delegation framework that mentions one, and to be honest, until I looked at the ICO's notes side by side, I had not even put together the risk factors and the likelihood of preexisting governance or regulations with anywhere near that level of formality.

One honest caveat, and it is the regulator's own: that guidance is currently marked as under review and subject to change because of the Data (Use and Access) Act. The definition is what it is today. It may not be what it is in six months, and anybody building a policy on it should know that.

France's CNIL, in the same note I quoted yesterday, puts the durable version of the worry in a single clause: give an agent your working environment and *"celle-ci peut consulter l'ensemble des informations disponibles."* It can consult everything available to it. The ten minutes end. The access does not.

And your own (Australian) signals directorate has already written the control, though not for this. The ASD's short-term priority list says that personnel and services, *"including AI agents,"* get the minimum access required to do their duties. Note what a recording does to that sentence. It does not grant the agent access. It teaches the agent the shape of yours.

Those of you who work in the same industry as me know the beats of this particular drum. Reconciliation, access review, AML flagging, month-end, floor drops, promo chip transfers -- these are real concepts, real working points, that a regulator will ask us to evidence. What actually happens if somebody in a licensed operation records a reconciliation into a tool whose retention policy nobody can find? I have customers in the USA who for decades kept more than 10 years of paper receipts on relatively non-descript items, just to ensure that the regulator did not have anything to write a finding about. So -- and I ask this as a question since I am not sure any of us really know -- does AUSTRAC's or your state's equivalent of NSW's OLGR's view of "who had access to this data" survive the answer "a vendor, for an unstated period"? Even worse, "a vendor, and an unknown list of potential sub-processors of that vendor?"

This is not going to be caught by a security team, it is going to be done by a good, conscientious person in finance who was trying to save everybody four hours a month. It is going to be done in the search for efficiency and will be an unintended by-product until the day the demand shows up. You are best served to prepare for that eventuality now.

## What to do before somebody in your finance team does it for you

**Find out whether anybody has already recorded one.** Not whether it is permitted -- whether it has happened. These features ship inside products you already pay for, they are enabled by a link in a release note, and the person who tries one is not doing anything wrong. Ask in the places where people are proud of saving time, because that is where the answer is.

**Then decide which workflows are recordable, in writing, before the question is live.** This is a short list and it will take somebody an afternoon. Anything that traverses a system holding customer data, anything that touches a payment path, anything whose access was granted by exception, and anything you would have to describe to a regulator. Those are not recordable. Almost everything else probably is, and saying so out loud is what keeps the rule credible.

**And make somebody go and find the retention answer before the recording, not after.** How long is it kept, where, is it trained on, and can you turn it off centrally. If the vendor documents all four, you have a decision to make. If the vendor documents none of them, you do not have a decision to make, because you cannot make one without the answers. That is not a judgment about the vendor. It is arithmetic about what you know.

## The part a recording cannot carry

There is a reason this bothers me more than the access-path problem, and it took me a while to work out what it was.

A recording captures every step of what you did. It cannot capture one thing, which is *why you stopped, or even just paused, in the recording*.

The moment in a reconciliation where an experienced person pauses on a line that balances perfectly and looks at it twice anyway -- because the amount is round, because that counterparty has been quiet for two months, because something about it is faintly wrong in a way they could not write down if you asked them to. Nothing happens in the recording. There is no click to capture. The screen does not change. It is the single most valuable thing in the entire ten minutes and it is the only part that leaves no trace.

So the recording transmits the process perfectly and the judgment not at all. What gets handed over is the shape of the work. What stays behind, uncopied and unrecorded and increasingly unpracticed, is the part that was actually doing the controlling.

Teach it the reconciliation, by all means. Just be clear about which half you taught it, and which half is still sitting in one person's head, and how long you intend to keep paying for that person to have it.
